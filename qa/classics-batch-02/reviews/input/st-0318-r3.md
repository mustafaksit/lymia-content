# The Nutcracker and the Mouse King

Summary: A girl looks after a broken wooden nutcracker and finds herself in a strange battle at midnight.

## Level A1

It was Christmas Eve at the Stahlbaum house. Marie and Fritz waited at the door. At last the door opened. Godfather Drosselmeier came with a gift. Under the tree, Marie found a wooden man. He had a big head and thin legs. His face was not pretty. It was a nutcracker. "I love him," said Marie.

Fritz gave the Nutcracker a very big nut. Crack! Three small teeth fell out. Marie put a ribbon around his head.

At night, Marie stayed by the toy cupboard. The clock struck twelve. Mice came out from under the floor. Their king had seven heads and seven crowns. He was the Mouse King.

Then the toys came alive. "Soldiers, follow me!" cried the Nutcracker. The toy soldiers fought the mice. But there were too many mice. The Mouse King ran at the Nutcracker.

Marie took off her shoe. She threw it at the Mouse King. All the mice ran away. Then everything went dark for Marie. She woke up in her bed. Her mother did not believe her story.

The Mouse King came back at night. "Give me your sweets!" said the Mouse King. Marie gave him her sugar toys. But he wanted more and more. "Bring me a sword," said the Nutcracker. Fritz gave him a little sword. That night the Nutcracker fought the Mouse King. He won and brought Marie the seven crowns.

"Come with me," said the Nutcracker. He took Marie to the Doll Kingdom. It had lemonade rivers and candy trees. They went to the Marzipan Castle. Later, Marie woke up at home again.

"I like you, little Nutcracker," said Marie. "Your face does not matter." Soon a young man came to the house. He was Godfather Drosselmeier's nephew. "I am the Nutcracker," he told Marie. "Your kind words saved me." He asked Marie to be his queen. She said yes. A year later, he came for her. Now she is queen of the Doll Kingdom.

## Level A2

On Christmas Eve, Fritz and Marie Stahlbaum waited outside the big room. At last a bell rang, and the doors opened.

Godfather Drosselmeier was a small old man with a black eye patch. This year he made a toy castle with tiny moving people. But the people did the same thing again and again. Soon Fritz and Marie were bored.

Then Marie saw a little wooden man under the tree. He had a big head and a kind face. "He is a nutcracker," said her father. "Marie, you must look after him." Fritz gave him a very hard nut, and three teeth fell out. Marie tied a ribbon from her dress around his head.

That night, Marie stayed up late by the glass cupboard. The clock struck twelve. Suddenly mice ran out from every hole in the floor. Then a huge mouse came up with seven heads and seven crowns. It was the Mouse King.

The Nutcracker jumped out of the cupboard. "Wake up, soldiers, and follow me!" he shouted. Fritz's toy soldiers and the dolls ran to help him. But there were too many mice. The Mouse King came close to the Nutcracker. Marie took off her left shoe and threw it at him. At once, the mice and their king disappeared. Everything went dark, and Marie woke up in her own bed. Nobody believed her story.

A few days later, Godfather Drosselmeier told a story. The queen of the mice made a baby princess ugly. A young man cracked a hard nut for her. Then she was beautiful again. But he stepped on the queen of the mice. So she made him ugly, and he became a nutcracker. The princess laughed and sent him away. This young man was Godfather Drosselmeier's nephew.

Now the Mouse King came back every night. "Give me your sugar toys," whispered the Mouse King. Then he showed his sharp teeth to the Nutcracker. Marie gave him her sweets, but he wanted more and more. "Bring me a sword," said the Nutcracker. Fritz gave Marie a little sword from one of his soldiers. In the night, the Nutcracker fought the Mouse King and won. Then he brought Marie the seven crowns.

"Come with me, Marie," said the Nutcracker. They climbed through the sleeve of her father's old coat. On the other side was the Doll Kingdom. There were rivers of lemonade and forests full of sweets. In the Marzipan Castle, the Nutcracker's sisters welcomed Marie. Slowly Marie fell asleep, and she woke up at home again.

She showed the seven crowns to her family. But her father said, "No more silly stories, Marie." Later, Marie whispered, "Dear Nutcracker, your face does not matter. I like you just as you are."

That afternoon, Godfather Drosselmeier came with his nephew. The young man knelt in front of Marie. "Your kind words broke the spell," he said. "I am your Nutcracker." He asked her to be his queen, and Marie said yes. A year later, he came for her in a golden carriage. Now Marie is the queen of the Doll Kingdom.

## Level B1

On Christmas Eve, Fritz and Marie Stahlbaum sat in a dark corner and waited. At last a little bell rang, and the doors flew open. The Christmas tree was shining with candles, golden apples and sweets of every kind.

Godfather Drosselmeier was not a handsome man. He was small and thin, with a black patch over one eye. This year he brought a little castle with tiny ladies and gentlemen inside. After a few minutes, the children grew bored, because the figures always did the same thing.

Under the tree, Marie found a wooden man with a large head and a friendly smile. "He is a nutcracker, and he belongs to all of you," said her father. "But you will look after him, Marie, because you like him most." Fritz pushed the hardest nut he could find into the little man's mouth. There was a loud crack, and three small teeth fell to the floor. Marie quickly took the Nutcracker away and tied a white ribbon around his head.

That night Marie stayed up alone to put her toys away in the glass cupboard. She laid the Nutcracker gently in a doll's bed inside it. Soon the big clock struck twelve. Hundreds of mice were coming out from the cracks in the floor. Then the floor opened, and a mouse with seven heads and seven shining crowns came up. This was the Mouse King.

The Nutcracker jumped out of his bed and called his army. "Drummer, beat the drum!" cried the Nutcracker. "Soldiers, come with me!" Fritz's toy soldiers marched out, and even the dolls joined them. But for every mouse that ran away, ten more came. The Mouse King was coming closer, and all seven heads were laughing.

Marie could not bear it any more. She pulled off her left slipper and threw it hard at the Mouse King. In a moment, the mice and their king disappeared. Marie felt dizzy, and everything went black. When she woke up, she was lying in her own bed. "You played with your toys too late," said her mother. Nobody believed a word of Marie's story.

A few days later, Godfather Drosselmeier came to visit and told a long story. Once, the queen of the mice made a baby princess terribly ugly. Only a very hard nut could help her. At last a young man cracked it with his teeth, and the princess became beautiful again. This young man was Drosselmeier's own nephew. But as he stepped back, he stepped on the queen of the mice. At once, he became small and ugly, with a huge head and a wide wooden mouth. The princess laughed at him and sent him away. "Only two things can break the spell," said Drosselmeier. "He must win against the Mouse King, the son of the mouse queen. Also, a girl must love him."

After that, the Mouse King came to Marie's bed every night. "Give me your sweets, or I will bite your Nutcracker," he whispered. Marie put her sugar dolls and her marzipan sweets near the cupboard. The next night he wanted her picture books and her new dress. "I want to help you, but I have nothing left," she said to the Nutcracker. "Just bring me a sword," he answered in a low voice. Fritz took a little sword from one of his old soldiers and gave it to her. That night Marie heard a great noise and then a knock at her door. The Nutcracker stood there, holding the seven tiny crowns of the Mouse King.

"Now let me show you something wonderful," said the Nutcracker. He led Marie to the wardrobe, and they climbed up the sleeve of her father's coat. On the other side lay the Doll Kingdom. They walked through a forest full of golden fruit and along a river of lemonade. Finally, they reached the Marzipan Castle, where the Nutcracker's sisters were waiting. Marie was so happy that she slowly fell asleep. She woke up in her own bed, with the morning sun on her face.

At breakfast, she told her family everything, but they only laughed. So she ran to get the seven little crowns and showed them to her mother. Her father was angry and told her to stop telling silly stories. Later, Marie whispered to the Nutcracker, "I do not care about your face. I will always like you just as you are."

That afternoon, Godfather Drosselmeier came with a young man. It was his nephew, and he cracked nuts for everyone with a smile. When they were alone, the young man knelt down in front of Marie. "Your kind words broke the spell," he said. "I am your Nutcracker." He asked Marie to be his queen, and she said yes. A year later, he came for her in a golden carriage. Today Marie is still the queen of the Doll Kingdom.

## Level B2

On Christmas Eve, the Stahlbaum children were kept out of the large living room all day. At last a silver bell rang, the doors were thrown open, and light poured out. The tree stood in the middle of the room, hung with golden apples, sugared almonds and countless candles.

Godfather Drosselmeier was short and thin, with a wrinkled face and a black patch over his right eye. He could repair any clock in the city, and every Christmas he built the children a wonderful toy. This year it was a castle with golden towers and tiny people who walked from room to room. For a while Fritz and Marie watched in delight, but the little figures only ever repeated the same movements.

Beside the tree, Marie noticed a wooden man in a neat little coat, with a head far too large for his body. His face was not handsome, but his pale green eyes looked so kind that she loved him immediately. Her father explained that he was a nutcracker and that he belonged to the whole family. "Since you like him so much, Marie, you shall take special care of him," he added. Fritz, however, wanted to test him and forced the biggest, hardest nut he could find between his jaws. With a loud crack, three tiny teeth fell out, and the little man's chin hung loose. Marie snatched him away, wrapped a white ribbon from her dress around his head, and held him close.

That night, when the others went to bed, Marie begged to stay up a little longer to put her toys away. She laid the Nutcracker in a doll's bed in the glass cupboard and promised to have his teeth mended. On top of the big clock sat a golden owl, and in the candlelight its face suddenly looked exactly like Godfather Drosselmeier's. The clock struck twelve, and from every crack in the walls came the sound of tiny feet and shrill whistles. Then the boards near Marie's feet split open, and a mouse with seven heads rose up, each head wearing a shining crown. This was the Mouse King.

The Nutcracker threw off his blanket and jumped bravely down from the shelf. "Drummer, sound the alarm!" he cried, and Fritz's toy soldiers came marching out of their boxes. The toy cannons fired sweets at the mice, but there were simply too many of them. Soldier after soldier fell over, and soon the Nutcracker stood alone with his back against the cupboard. The Mouse King pushed his way towards him, and all seven of his heads were squeaking with joy.

Marie could bear it no longer. Without thinking, she pulled off her left slipper and threw it straight at the Mouse King. In that instant the whole army vanished, as if it had never been there. When she opened her eyes, she was lying in her own bed, and her mother was sitting beside her. Her mother explained that she had stayed up far too late and had a bad dream. Nobody in the house believed a word about the battle.

A few days later, Godfather Drosselmeier came to visit, and Marie asked him for a story. Long ago, he said, the queen of the mice put a spell on a baby princess and made her very ugly. Only the hardest nut in the world could save her. After many years of searching, the king's clockmaker found the nut, and his own nephew cracked it with his teeth. At once the princess became beautiful again. But as the young man stepped backwards with his eyes closed, he trod on the queen of the mice. In that moment the spell passed to him: his head grew huge, his mouth grew wide, and he became a nutcracker. The princess, who now found him ugly, laughed at him and sent him away. The spell would break only when he defeated the mouse queen's seven-headed son and won a girl's love despite his looks. Marie was sure it was the story of her own Nutcracker.

From then on, the Mouse King came to Marie's room every night. Sitting on her pillow, he squeaked that he would bite the Nutcracker unless she gave him her sweets. Marie laid her sugar dolls and her marzipan figures beside the cupboard, and by morning they were gone. Then the Mouse King demanded her picture books and her new Christmas dress. In despair, Marie sat in front of the cupboard and whispered that she had nothing left to give. "Do not give him anything more," the Nutcracker answered softly. "Only bring me a sword." Fritz, who had just sent one of his old colonels into retirement, gladly handed over the colonel's little sword. Late that night, the Nutcracker knocked at Marie's door with seven little gold crowns in his hand. "Thanks to you, the Mouse King is defeated," he said with a deep bow.

"Now, dear Marie, allow me to show you my kingdom," said the Nutcracker. He led her to the great wardrobe in the hall, where her father's travelling coat was hanging. They climbed up through one of its sleeves, and suddenly Marie was standing in a sweet-smelling meadow. This was the Doll Kingdom. They walked through a Christmas forest whose trees were hung with golden fruit, and along a river of lemonade. Finally, they reached the Marzipan Castle, which rose above a town of sweets with pink and white towers. Inside, the Nutcracker's sisters, tiny princesses dressed in gold, welcomed Marie and began to cook a feast for her. Marie watched them, happier than ever, until everything around her slowly faded into mist. She woke up in her own bed, and the morning sun was shining on her blanket.

Marie told her family all about the Doll Kingdom, but they only laughed at her. To prove that it was true, she fetched the seven little crowns and laid them in her mother's hand. Her father became quite angry and told her never to speak of such nonsense again. One afternoon she said quietly to the Nutcracker, "If you were real, I would never laugh at you like that princess did." "I would love you just as you are," she added.

That very afternoon, Godfather Drosselmeier arrived with a young man at his side. It was his nephew, and he politely cracked nuts for everyone. When the others had left the room, he knelt down in front of Marie. "Dear Marie, you have broken the spell," he said. "You said you would love me as I was, and so I am free. I am your Nutcracker, and I have come to ask you to be my queen." Marie said yes. Exactly a year later, he came to fetch her in a golden carriage and took her to the Doll Kingdom. They say she is still queen there today.

## Level C1

On Christmas Eve the Stahlbaum children were banished from the drawing room for the whole day, and the house seemed to hum with secrets. As dusk crept in, Fritz and his younger sister Marie sat huddled in a corner, too excited to ask for a lamp. At last a silvery bell rang out, the doors swung wide, and a flood of light spilled across the threshold. In the middle of the room stood the tree, its branches heavy with golden apples, sugared almonds and more candles than anyone could count.

Godfather Drosselmeier was not, by any ordinary measure, an attractive man. Small and wiry, with a face as wrinkled as a walnut, he wore a black patch over one eye and a curious wig of fine white glass. Yet there was no clock in the city he could not coax back to life, and every Christmas his clever hands produced some marvel for the children. This year it was a castle with golden towers and mirrored halls, through which tiny ladies and gentlemen strolled while a little man waved from a balcony.

Beside the tree, half hidden among the branches, stood a small wooden man in a neat coat, his head absurdly large for his slender body. His face could hardly be called handsome, yet his pale green eyes held such a gentle, patient kindness that Marie loved him at first sight. "He is a nutcracker, and he belongs to all of you," her father told her, "but since you are so fond of him, Marie, he shall be in your special care." Fritz, naturally, wished to see what the little fellow could do, and he crammed the largest, hardest nut he could find between those wooden jaws. There was a dreadful crack, three tiny teeth tumbled onto the carpet, and the Nutcracker's chin dropped loose. Marie snatched him away, bound his poor head with a white ribbon from her dress, and cradled him in her arms.

That night, after the rest of the household had gone up to bed, Marie begged leave to stay a little longer and tidy her toys into the glass cupboard. She settled the Nutcracker in a doll's bed on the lowest shelf, promising him in a whisper that Godfather Drosselmeier would mend his teeth. Just then the tall clock in the corner began to whir and grumble, and Marie, glancing up, saw that the gilded owl perched on top of it wore Drosselmeier's face. The clock struck twelve, slowly and heavily, and with the last stroke came a scratching and a shrill whistling from every crack in the walls. Then the floor near Marie's feet burst open, and up rose a mouse with seven heads, each wearing a glittering crown, all seven of them squeaking in chorus. This, Marie understood with a shiver, was the Mouse King.

Behind the glass, the cupboard came alive. The Nutcracker flung off his blanket, leapt down from the shelf and called his troops to arms in a voice far louder than his size suggested. Drums rattled, Fritz's toy soldiers came tumbling out of their boxes, and even the dolls and the clowns hurried to join the ranks with whatever they could carry. For every mouse that fled, however, ten more came swarming from the walls, and one by one the brave little soldiers toppled over. At last the Nutcracker stood alone, his back against the cupboard, while the Mouse King pressed towards him with all seven heads squealing in triumph.

Marie could bear it no longer. Scarcely knowing what she did, she pulled off her left slipper and flung it with all her strength into the thick of the enemy, straight at the Mouse King. In that instant the whole army vanished as though it had never existed, and the room fell silent. When she woke, she was lying in her own bed, winter sunshine was pouring through the window, and her mother was sitting beside her with an anxious face. Her mother gently insisted that it had all been a dream.

A few days later Godfather Drosselmeier came to visit, and at Marie's request he told a long and peculiar tale. Long ago, he said, a king held a great feast, but before the sausages could be made, the queen of the mice and her family ate nearly all the bacon. When the king's clockmaker, who also bore the name Drosselmeier, drove her people from the palace, the mouse queen took her revenge on the king's baby daughter. The little princess, once as lovely as a rose, woke one morning with an enormous head and a wide, grinning mouth. Only the hardest nut in the world could undo the spell, and it had to be cracked by a young man who had never shaved or worn boots. After years of searching, the clockmaker found both the nut and the young man, who turned out to be his own nephew. The youth cracked the nut between his teeth, handed the kernel to the princess with his eyes shut, and the moment she swallowed it she was as beautiful as before. But as he stepped backwards, as the rules of the spell required, he trod on the mouse queen, and the curse leapt from the princess to him. In a moment his head swelled, his mouth gaped, and he became a nutcracker; the princess, ungrateful to the last, laughed and had him turned out of the palace. A wise astronomer declared that only two things could free him: he must defeat the seven-headed son of the mouse queen, and a girl must love him despite his looks. Drosselmeier ended there with a sly smile, and Marie, sitting very still, knew precisely whose story she had just heard.

From that night on, the Mouse King paid Marie a visit whenever the house was asleep. Perched on her pillow, he hissed that he would bite her precious Nutcracker unless she handed over her sweets. Trembling, Marie laid her sugar dolls and marzipan figures beside the cupboard, and by morning not a crumb was left. The next night he wanted her picture books, the night after that her new Christmas dress, and there seemed to be no end to his greed. At last Marie sat weeping in front of the cupboard and confessed to the Nutcracker that she had nothing left to give. "Give him nothing more," the little man murmured, his wooden lips scarcely moving. "Only bring me a sword, and leave the rest to me." Fritz, who had lately retired one of his old colonels, was happy to hand over the officer's little blade for so worthy a cause. Late that night came a soft knock at Marie's door, and there stood the Nutcracker, bowing deeply, with seven tiny golden crowns in his outstretched hand.

"My enemy is defeated, dear Marie, and I owe my victory to you alone," he said. "Allow me, in return, to show you a kingdom more wonderful than any you have dreamed of." He led her to the great wardrobe in the hall, where her father's old travelling coat hung, and together they climbed up through one of its sleeves. They stepped out into a meadow that glittered like sugar. This was the Doll Kingdom, where the Christmas forest hung its branches with golden fruit and a river of lemonade chattered over the stones. Further on rose the Marzipan Castle, its pink and white towers shining above a little town built entirely of sweets. Inside, the Nutcracker's sisters, tiny princesses dressed in gold, welcomed Marie with delight. Marie watched them, happier than she had ever been, until the walls began to blur and drift away like mist. She woke in her own bed, the morning light on her blanket.

At breakfast Marie poured out the whole adventure, but her family only laughed at her. Stung, she ran to fetch the seven little crowns and laid them in her mother's palm as proof. Godfather Drosselmeier picked them up with a curious smile and declared that they came from his own watch chain and that he had given them to her long ago. Her father, losing patience, forbade her ever again to speak of mouse kings and sugar kingdoms. One afternoon she whispered to the Nutcracker, "If only you were alive, I would never treat you as that foolish princess did. Your face would not matter to me in the least."

That very afternoon Godfather Drosselmeier arrived with a young man at his side, whom he introduced as his nephew. To the children's delight, the courteous nephew cracked their nuts for them without breaking a single tooth. When the others had drifted out of the room, he knelt before Marie and took her hand. "Dearest Marie, your words have broken the spell," he said. "You did not mind my ugly face, and that alone set me free. I am your Nutcracker, and I have come to ask you to be my queen." Marie said yes with all her heart. Exactly a year later he came to fetch her in a golden carriage and carried her off to the Doll Kingdom. They say she reigns there still, among the sugar meadows and lemonade rivers, the queen of a land that only the right eyes can see.
