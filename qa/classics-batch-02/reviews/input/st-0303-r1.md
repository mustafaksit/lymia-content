# Oliver Twist: Oliver Asks for More

Summary: A hungry boy in a workhouse asks for more food, and his life suddenly changes.

## Level A1

Oliver was a small boy. He had no mother or father. He lived in a big, cold workhouse. The children there were always hungry. Every day they got one small bowl. The food in it was thin and gray.

One evening, the boys made a plan. "We need more food," said the boys. They chose Oliver to ask. Oliver walked to the master with his bowl. "Please, sir, can I have more?" asked Oliver.

The master looked at him in shock. "More?" cried the master. The men of the workhouse were angry too. They put Oliver in a dark room.

Mr. Sowerberry took Oliver to his shop. An older boy, Noah, worked there too. Noah said bad things about Oliver's mother. Oliver got very angry, and the boys fought. Oliver won, but everyone was angry with him.

Early the next morning, Oliver ran away. He walked to London for seven days. Near London, he met a boy. The boy's name was Jack Dawkins. People called him the Artful Dodger. The Dodger gave Oliver some food.

"Come and meet my friend," said the Dodger. The friend was an old man, Fagin. Fagin lived with many boys. He gave Oliver food and a bed. Fagin played a strange game with the boys. They took things from his pockets.

Oliver went out with the Dodger and Charley. An old man stood near a book shop. The Dodger took something from his pocket. Now Oliver understood. The boys were pickpockets! Oliver was afraid, and he ran.

"Stop, thief!" people shouted. A man caught Oliver. A policeman took him to a judge. Then a man from the book shop came. "This boy did not take it," he said.

The old man was Mr. Brownlow. "You are a good boy," said Mr. Brownlow. He took Oliver to his warm house. Oliver was ill for many days. At last, Oliver was safe and happy.

## Level A2

Oliver Twist had no mother or father. He grew up in a workhouse for poor people. The building was cold, and the children were always hungry. Each boy got one small bowl of thin, gray food.

After three months, the hungry boys made a plan. "One of us must ask for more," they said. They played a game to choose, and Oliver lost. That evening, the boys ate quickly and looked at Oliver. He was afraid, but he was very hungry. Oliver walked up to the master with his bowl. "Please, sir," said Oliver, "can I have a little more?"

The master's face went white. "What?" cried the master. Then he shouted for Mr. Bumble. Mr. Bumble ran to the men in charge of the workhouse. The men were shocked and angry. They locked Oliver in a dark room for a week. Then they put a notice on the gate. Any man could have Oliver and five pounds too.

Mr. Sowerberry made coffins, and he took Oliver to his shop. Another boy, Noah, also worked there. Noah was big and lazy, and he was cruel to Oliver. One day, Noah said cruel words about Oliver's mother. Oliver jumped up in anger. The two boys fought, and Oliver won. They locked Oliver in the cellar and sent for Mr. Bumble.

Early the next morning, Oliver opened the door and left. He walked towards London, seventy miles away. The road was long, and the nights were cold. After seven days, he came to a small town near London. His feet hurt, and he sat down on a step.

A strange boy in a man's big coat looked at him. His name was Jack Dawkins, but people called him the Artful Dodger. The Dodger bought Oliver bread and ham. "I know a kind old man in London," he said.

They walked into London late at night. In an old, dirty house, an old man stood by the fire. His name was Fagin. Fagin gave Oliver hot food and a bed. Every day, Fagin and the boys played a strange game. Fagin put things in his pockets and walked around. The boys took the things very quietly. Oliver laughed, but he did not understand.

After many days, Oliver went out with the Dodger and Charley Bates. Near a book shop, an old gentleman stood and read a book. The Dodger quietly took a handkerchief from his pocket. Suddenly, Oliver understood the game. The boys were pickpockets! Oliver was very afraid, so he ran. "Stop, thief!" shouted the gentleman.

Many people ran after Oliver. A man stopped him, and he fell on the road. A policeman took Oliver to a judge. The judge wanted to send Oliver to prison. Then the owner of the book shop ran in. "Another boy took it, not this one!" he cried.

The old gentleman was Mr. Brownlow. Oliver was ill, and he fell to the floor. Mr. Brownlow took him home in a carriage. For many days, Oliver lay in bed with a fever. Mr. Brownlow's housekeeper cared for him very kindly. For the first time, Oliver felt safe.

## Level B1

Oliver Twist began his life in a workhouse, a place for people who had no money. His mother died on the night of his birth, so he grew up alone. The workhouse was cold and gray, and the children in it were always hungry. Every day, each boy received one small bowl of thin porridge, and nothing more.

After three months, the boys were desperate. They decided that someone had to ask for more, and they drew lots to choose. The choice fell on Oliver. That evening, the master was standing beside the pot in his cook's apron. When the porridge was gone, the boys whispered and pushed Oliver forward. He got up, walked to the master and held out his bowl. "Please, sir," he said in a small voice, "may I have a little more?"

The master stared at him and could not believe his ears. Then he grabbed Oliver by the arm and shouted for Mr. Bumble, the beadle. Mr. Bumble hurried to the gentlemen who ran the workhouse. "Oliver Twist asked for more!" he cried. One gentleman in a white waistcoat said that the boy would end badly. They shut Oliver in a dark room for a week. They also put a notice on the gate. It offered five pounds to anyone who would take Oliver away.

Mr. Sowerberry, who made coffins for the town, took Oliver to work in his shop. Another boy, Noah, also worked there, and he loved to make Oliver's life hard. One afternoon Noah began to say cruel things about Oliver's mother. Oliver's face went red, and something inside him changed. He jumped up and fought Noah, and soon the bigger boy was lying on the floor. They locked Oliver in the cellar and sent for Mr. Bumble.

That night, alone in the shop, Oliver made a decision. At first light, he opened the door and stepped into the quiet street. On a stone by the road he read that London was seventy miles away. He walked for seven days, sleeping in fields and asking for bread at doors.

In a small town near London, Oliver sat down on a doorstep to rest. A boy of about his age was watching him from across the road. He was wearing a man's coat that was far too big for him. His name was Jack Dawkins, but everybody called him the Artful Dodger. The Dodger bought Oliver bread and ham and listened to his story. "An old gentleman in London will give you a bed for free," said the Dodger.

They reached London late at night and walked through dark, narrow streets. In a dirty room up some broken stairs, an old man was cooking sausages. Several boys were sitting around a table and talking. "Fagin, this is my new friend, Oliver Twist," said the Dodger. Fagin smiled and gave Oliver food and a bed of old sacks.

In the days that followed, Oliver watched a very strange game. Fagin put a watch and a handkerchief in his pockets and walked around the room. The boys followed him closely and took the things so softly that he never felt them. Oliver laughed until the tears came, but he had no idea what the game meant.

At last Fagin let Oliver go out with the Dodger and Charley Bates. Near a bookstall, they saw an old gentleman who was reading a book. The Dodger crept up behind him and pulled a handkerchief from his pocket. Then he and Charley ran off around the corner. In that moment, Oliver understood everything: the boys were pickpockets. Afraid and confused, he started to run too.

"Stop, thief!" shouted the old gentleman, and soon the whole street was running after Oliver. At last a man stopped Oliver, and he fell to the ground. A policeman took him to a judge, a hard and angry man. The judge did not listen to Oliver and was ready to send him to prison. Just then, the owner of the bookstall rushed in. "Another boy took the handkerchief, not this one!" cried the man from the bookstall.

The judge had to let Oliver go. But Oliver was pale and ill, and he fainted on the floor. The old gentleman was Mr. Brownlow, and he felt very sorry for the boy. He took Oliver home in a carriage and called a doctor. For many days Oliver lay in bed with a high fever. Mr. Brownlow's kind housekeeper sat beside him and cared for him like a mother. For the first time in his life, Oliver felt safe.

## Level B2

Oliver Twist came into the world in a workhouse, a grim building where the poor of the town lived on charity. His mother died on the night of his birth, and no one knew who his father was. Even his name was not his own: Mr. Bumble, the parish beadle, invented it for him.

The workhouse fed its children as little as possible. Apart from a bowl of thin porridge three times a day, the boys got almost nothing. After every meal they went on gazing at the big pot, as though they could fill their stomachs by looking at it. After three months of this, the hungry boys held a secret meeting. They agreed that someone had to ask the master for more, and they drew lots to decide who it would be. The lot fell to Oliver.

That evening the master, in his cook's apron, served the porridge as usual, and it disappeared in moments. The boys whispered to each other and nudged Oliver, whose heart was pounding. Hungry and frightened in equal measure, Oliver walked to the master with his bowl and spoon in his hands. "Please, sir," he said, his voice shaking, "I would like a little more." The master, a fat and healthy man, went pale. For several seconds he stared at the small boy in silence.

Then he grabbed Oliver by the arm and shouted for the beadle. Mr. Bumble hurried to the gentlemen of the board with the shocking news. One of them, a man in a white waistcoat, declared that the boy would certainly come to a bad end. Oliver was locked in a dark, cold room for a week, where he cried alone through the long days and longer nights. Meanwhile, a notice appeared on the gate, offering five pounds to anyone who would take the boy away.

In the end it was Mr. Sowerberry, the town's undertaker, who agreed to take Oliver as his apprentice. Another boy, Noah, also worked there, and because he was older, he treated Oliver like a servant. For weeks Oliver accepted every insult in silence. Then one day Noah began to say cruel things about Oliver's mother. That was more than Oliver could bear. Red with anger, he fought the much larger boy, and soon Noah was on the floor, crying for help. Mrs. Sowerberry rushed in, and together they shut Oliver in the cellar and sent for Mr. Bumble. Oliver was punished severely, but the punishment did not break him.

That night, alone in the dark shop, he cried, and then he made up his mind. As soon as the first light appeared, he slipped out of the door and ran until the town was behind him. A stone at the side of the road told him that London was seventy miles away. In a city so big, he thought, no one could ever find him. For seven days he walked, sleeping in fields and begging at cottage doors for a little bread and water.

In a small town near London, he sat down on a doorstep, too tired to go any further. Soon he noticed a boy watching him from the other side of the street. The boy was about Oliver's age, but he had the confident manner of a grown man. He wore a man's coat so long that it reached almost to his heels. "Hungry, are you?" asked the stranger, crossing the road. He introduced himself as Jack Dawkins, though his friends knew him as the Artful Dodger. After buying Oliver a meal of bread and ham, the Dodger listened to his story. "There's an old gentleman in London who'll give you a bed and never ask for a penny," said the Dodger.

They entered London after dark and made their way through narrow, dirty streets until they reached an old house. Up a dark staircase, in a smoky room, an old man stood at the fire, cooking sausages with a long fork. Several boys sat around a table, and dozens of silk handkerchiefs hung drying on a line above them. "This is Fagin," said the Dodger, and the old man smiled and bowed to Oliver as if he were an honored guest. Early the next morning, half awake, Oliver watched Fagin take a small box from a hiding place in the floor. Inside were gold watches and sparkling rings, and the old man looked at them with great delight. When he saw that Oliver was awake, Fagin quickly shut the box and smiled.

Over the following days, Oliver often watched a curious game. Fagin filled his pockets with a watch and a handkerchief, then strolled around the room like a gentleman in the street. Meanwhile, the Dodger and Charley Bates followed him so closely and so lightly that he never felt their hands. Oliver laughed until he cried, though he had no idea what the game was really for.

At last Oliver was allowed to go out with the two boys. Near a bookstall, they saw an elderly gentleman so lost in a book that he seemed to forget the world around him. The Dodger slipped up behind him, drew a silk handkerchief from his pocket, and ran off with Charley around the corner. Oliver stood frozen in shock. Suddenly the watches, the handkerchiefs and the strange game all made sense: his new friends were pickpockets. In a panic, he began to run.

At that moment the gentleman put his hand in his pocket, turned, and saw a boy racing away. "Stop, thief!" he cried, and the cry spread down the street, with the Dodger and Charley shouting loudest of all. Before long, a man stopped Oliver, who fell to the ground. A police officer took him to the magistrate, a rude, impatient man who refused to listen to Oliver. He was about to send the boy to prison when the owner of the bookstall burst in, out of breath. "I saw it all with my own eyes," cried the bookseller, "and it was another boy who took the handkerchief."

The magistrate had no choice but to let Oliver go. But the boy was pale and shaking, and before anyone could speak, he fainted on the floor. The old gentleman, whose name was Mr. Brownlow, was filled with pity. He called a carriage and took Oliver to his own comfortable house. For many days Oliver lay in bed with a burning fever, hardly knowing where he was. Mr. Brownlow's housekeeper, a kind old woman, sat by his bed and cared for him as gently as a mother. When at last the fever passed, Oliver woke in a clean, quiet room full of soft light. For the first time in his life, he felt that he was truly safe.

## Level C1

Among the public buildings of a certain English town stood a workhouse, and it was there, on a cold night, that Oliver Twist drew his first breath. His young mother lived just long enough to see him, and nobody in the place could say who she was or where she had come from. Even his name was an invention: Mr. Bumble, the parish beadle, chose it from an alphabetical list he kept for such cases, having reached the letter T.

The gentlemen of the board who governed the workhouse were wise, practical men, or so they believed. Having decided that the poor found the house far too comfortable, they set about correcting the problem with a menu built almost entirely on thin porridge. The result was exactly what anyone but a board member might have predicted. At every meal the children emptied their small bowls in a few desperate mouthfuls and then sat gazing at the great copper pot, as if hunger alone might make it fill again.

After three months of this, the boys reached the edge of what they could endure. A secret council was held, and it was agreed that one of them must walk up to the master after supper and ask for more. Lots were drawn, and the lot fell to Oliver Twist. That evening, when the porridge had been served and swallowed with the usual speed, the boys began to whisper and wink at him. Small, pale and frightened, but driven past caring by an empty stomach, Oliver rose from the bench, crossed the room with bowl and spoon in hand, and looked up at the master. "Please, sir," he said, astonished at his own courage, "I would like a little more."

The master was a fat, healthy man, yet he turned quite pale. He gazed down at the small rebel in stunned silence, then seized him by the arm and shrieked for the beadle. Within minutes, Mr. Bumble burst into the room where the board sat in solemn discussion and announced, with a face of deep alarm, that Oliver Twist had asked for more. The gentlemen could hardly have looked more appalled if he had announced a fire in the cellar. The gentleman in the white waistcoat, who prided himself on his judgment of character, declared that the boy would certainly come to a bad end.

Oliver spent the following week shut in a dark, cold room as punishment for his outrageous greed. Meanwhile, the board, eager to be rid of such a dangerous character, hung a notice on the gate promising five pounds to anyone prepared to take the boy away.

The man who finally accepted the offer was Mr. Sowerberry, the town's undertaker, a tall, thin man with a professional air of quiet sorrow. Another boy, Noah, was already employed in the business, and having at last found someone smaller than himself, he took full advantage of it. For weeks Oliver endured his insults without complaint. Then one afternoon, bored and looking for sport, Noah began to speak about Oliver's mother in the most contemptible terms he could think of. Something in the quiet, patient child snapped. Pale with rage, Oliver fought the larger boy with a strength that astonished them both, and within a moment Noah lay on the floor, howling for help. Mrs. Sowerberry came running, and between them, she and Noah shut Oliver in the cellar and sent for Mr. Bumble. After examining the case, the beadle concluded that the problem was not madness but meat: Mrs. Sowerberry had fed the boy too well, and it had given him ideas.

That night, alone in the silent workshop, Oliver finally allowed himself to cry, and when the tears were over, he knew what he would do. At the first gray light, he unbarred the door and slipped out into the empty street. Some miles along the road he came to a milestone telling him that London lay exactly seventy miles away, and the name filled him with hope. For seven days he walked, sleeping under hedges and begging at cottage doors, though a few kind strangers gave him more than they could spare.

By the seventh morning he had limped into a small town just outside London and sunk down on a doorstep, too weary to go further. After a while he became aware that a boy across the street was studying him with considerable interest. The stranger was about Oliver's age, yet he carried himself with the easy swagger of a grown man, and his enormous coat, turned back at the cuffs, reached almost to his heels. He introduced himself as Jack Dawkins, adding with some pride that among his friends he was better known as the Artful Dodger. Having bought Oliver a generous meal of bread and ham, he listened to the whole sad tale and then announced that he knew just the man to help. There was, he said, an old gentleman in London who would give Oliver a bed for nothing and never expect a penny in return.

They entered London after nightfall, and the Dodger led him through a maze of narrow, filthy streets where the air hung thick with smoke. At the top of a dark, broken staircase, in a room black with age and dirt, a very old man stood before the fire, cooking sausages with a long toasting fork. Around a table sat several boys, none of them older than the Dodger, talking and laughing like men twice their age. "Fagin, this is my friend Oliver Twist," said the Dodger, and the old man bowed low and declared himself honored to make the boy's acquaintance.

Early the next morning, through half-closed eyes, Oliver watched the old man lift a small box from a hiding place beneath the floor. From it Fagin drew one glittering object after another: gold watches, rings and brooches, which he turned over in his fingers with an expression of deep delight. The instant he noticed that Oliver was awake, he snapped the lid shut, and his manner changed to one of fatherly cheer, as if nothing had happened.

In the days that followed, Oliver was often entertained by a curious game. Fagin would fill his pockets with a watch, a purse and a handkerchief and stroll about the room with a stick, stopping to gaze into imaginary shop windows. Meanwhile, the Dodger and Charley Bates followed so closely and so lightly that, by the end of the walk, everything had vanished from his pockets without his feeling a thing. Oliver laughed until the tears ran down his face, never suspecting what the game was meant to teach.

At length, after much pleading, Oliver was permitted to go out with his two companions. They wandered without any obvious purpose until they reached a bookstall, where an elderly gentleman stood reading as comfortably as if he were sitting in his own armchair at home. To Oliver's dismay, the Dodger crept up behind him, drew a silk handkerchief from his pocket, passed it to Charley, and the two of them vanished around the corner. In one dreadful moment, the watches, the handkerchiefs and the curious game all fell into place: his cheerful new friends were pickpockets. Confused and frightened, Oliver took to his heels.

The gentleman, missing his handkerchief, turned just in time to see a small boy racing away, and naturally concluded that he had found the thief. "Stop, thief!" he shouted, and the cry passed from mouth to mouth along the street, with none shouting louder than the Dodger and Charley Bates themselves. Before long, a man stepped into Oliver's path, and the exhausted boy fell to the pavement, surrounded by a crowd of eager faces. A police officer marched him off to the nearest magistrate, a man whose temper was famous throughout the district and who rarely listened to anyone except himself. The magistrate was on the point of sending Oliver to prison when the owner of the bookstall burst in, breathless. He had seen everything, he declared, and it was another boy who had taken the handkerchief, while this one had only stood by in astonishment.

The magistrate had no choice but to release the prisoner, which he did with very poor grace. Oliver, however, was beyond hearing it: white and trembling, he swayed and fainted on the stones outside. The old gentleman, whose name was Mr. Brownlow, was filled with remorse, and he called a coach and carried the boy to his own house in a quiet, pleasant street. There, for many days, Oliver lay in the grip of a fever, drifting between sleep and waking while a motherly housekeeper sat beside his bed. When the fever finally passed, Oliver woke in a clean, sunlit room and heard a kind voice asking how he felt. For the first time in his short and difficult life, he understood that he was safe.
