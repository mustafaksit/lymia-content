# The Crystal Egg

Summary: A poor old shopkeeper in London discovers that a crystal egg in his window shows another world.

## Level A1

Mr Cave had a small, old shop. It was in Seven Dials, in London. He sold old things there. Mr Cave was thin and often ill. His wife and her children were unkind.

A crystal egg sat in the window. It was clear and very smooth. The price was five pounds. One afternoon, two young men came in. They wanted the crystal egg. But Mr Cave did not sell it. "A man bought it today," said Mr Cave. It was a lie.

Mrs Cave was very angry. "We need the money!" she cried. The next day, he took the egg away. He gave it to Mr Wace. Mr Wace was a young scientist. "Please keep it safe," said Mr Cave.

Then Mr Cave told him a secret. "I see a world inside," said Mr Cave. Mr Wace closed the curtains and looked. He saw it too!

Inside the egg was a wide land. There were big buildings and strange trees. Tall poles stood near the buildings. Each pole had a crystal egg on top. Strange creatures flew in the air. They had big heads and long arms.

One night, the men saw two moons. "This is Mars!" said Mr Wace. "The Martians have eggs too. Maybe they can see us." Once a Martian came very close. Mr Cave saw its large eyes.

The men watched Mars for many weeks. Mr Wace wrote everything in a book. Later, Mr Cave took the egg back.

Then Mr Wace was busy for some days. One night, Mr Cave sat in his shop. He held the crystal egg in his hands. He died quietly that night.

Mrs Cave sold the egg to a man. Then a tall stranger bought it. Nobody knows his name. Mr Wace came back too late. He still looks for the crystal egg.

The next day, he carried the egg across London. He took it to Mr Wace, a young man of science. Mr Wace worked at a big hospital. "Please keep this egg for me," said Mr Cave. "My family wants to sell it."

He looked into it, and he saw another world. Mr Wace did not believe him at first. But they closed the curtains, and Mr Wace looked into the egg. He saw the other world too!

For many weeks, Mr Cave came to Mr Wace in the evenings. They watched the strange world together. Mr Wace wrote notes in a book. One evening, they saw two small moons in the sky. "Our Earth has one moon, but Mars has two," said Mr Wace. "We can see Mars in this egg!"

## Level B1

Mr Cave kept a small, dusty shop near Seven Dials, in London. He sold old books, bottles and other old things. He was a thin, quiet man with pale blue eyes, and his health was poor. His wife was much younger than him, and she had a grown son and daughter. The three of them often spoke to him in a hard and unkind way. Mr Cave seldom answered them, but he was not a happy man.

In the window, there was an egg of crystal. It was smooth and clear, and it shone a little in the light. The price on its card was five pounds. One afternoon, two customers came in and asked about it. One was a young clergyman, and the other was a dark young man. They were both ready to pay, but Mr Cave suddenly looked nervous. "I'm afraid it isn't for sale any more," he told them. "A gentleman bought it this morning, and he will come back for it soon."

The two men left, and Mrs Cave came out of the back room. She heard the whole story, and she was furious. "We need every penny, and you send customers away!" she cried. Her son agreed with her, and he said he would sell the egg himself. Mr Cave did not argue, but that evening he made a quiet plan.

The next day, he wrapped the egg in a cloth and carried it across London. He took it to Mr Wace, a young man who taught science at a big hospital. "Please hide this for me," Mr Cave said. "My family wants to sell it, but I can't lose it. It isn't an ordinary piece of glass."

Then he told Mr Wace an amazing story. A few weeks earlier, he was lying awake at night, unable to sleep. He went down to the shop, and there he noticed a faint light. The crystal egg was glowing softly, although the room was dark. When he looked into it closely, he saw pictures of a strange country. Mr Wace thought the old man was imagining things. Still, they made the room dark, and he looked for himself.

At first, he saw only a pale mist inside the crystal. Then the mist cleared, and a wide view opened in front of him. The picture was clearest when a thin beam of light fell on the egg. There was a long, flat plain, with huge buildings and strange trees. Near the buildings stood tall poles, and on top of each one was a crystal egg. Big creatures were flying through the air on wide wings, a little like birds. They had round heads, large eyes and long, thin arms.

Mr Wace was now very excited, and he wanted to watch again and again. For many weeks, Mr Cave came to his room in the evenings, and they looked together. Mr Wace wrote careful notes about everything that they saw. One evening, two small moons crossed the sky of that strange world. "There's only one place like that," said Mr Wace. "Mars has two small moons, so we are looking at Mars!"

Soon the two men guessed something even stranger. The eggs on the poles were very like their own egg. Perhaps the Martians used them to look at the Earth. Once a creature came very close and seemed to stare straight at Mr Cave. He saw its large eyes clearly, and he jumped back from the crystal. Some time later, Mr Cave took the egg back home to his shop.

Then Mr Wace was busy with his work for several days. During that time, Mr Cave was tired and ill. One night, he sat alone with the egg in his shop, and he died quietly there. His wife found him in the morning, with the crystal egg in his hands.

Mrs Cave wanted money, so she sold everything in the shop to a dealer. The crystal egg went to the dealer with the rest. Soon he sold it again, to a tall dark man in gray. Nobody knew the man's name or address. When Mr Wace finally came to the shop, the egg was not there any more. He is still looking for it, and he still hopes that it will appear one day.

## Level B2

Mr Cave kept a small and rather untidy antique shop near Seven Dials, in the heart of London. He was a thin, pale old man with watery blue eyes, and for some time his health had been failing. His wife, who was much younger than he was, had a grown son and daughter from an earlier marriage. All three of them treated him with very little patience, and his days at home were seldom peaceful.

In the shop window, among dusty books and other odds and ends, lay a crystal egg. It was polished smooth, and a card beside it gave the price as five pounds. One afternoon, two customers came in together: a young clergyman and a dark young man. The clergyman held it up to the window, and the young man turned it slowly in his hands. They examined the egg with real interest, and they seemed quite willing to pay the price. To their surprise, the old man became nervous and began to make excuses. Finally, he told them that the egg had already been sold that morning.

The young men left, puzzled, and Mrs Cave, who had heard everything from the back room, came out in a fury. "Five pounds, and you turned it away!" she cried. "Do you think we're rich?" Her son was even louder, and he declared that he would sell the egg himself if he found a buyer. Mr Cave said very little, but he had no intention of giving the egg up.

The next morning, he wrapped the egg carefully and took it to Mr Jacoby Wace. Mr Wace was a young teacher of science at a large hospital, and he knew the old man slightly. He was surprised to see his visitor so anxious. "I must ask you to keep this for me," Mr Cave said quietly. "If it stays in my shop, they will sell it, and I couldn't bear that." And then, rather shyly, he explained why.

Some weeks before, on a night when he could not sleep, he had gone down to the shop. The room was quite dark, yet the crystal egg was giving off a faint, milky light. Curious, he had bent over it and looked inside. Instead of his own reflection, he saw a distant landscape, full of color and movement. Since then, he had looked into it many times, always alone and always in secret. He had told no one at home, since he was sure they would laugh or sell the egg at once.

Mr Wace listened politely, but at first he did not believe a single word of it. However, when the room was darkened and a thin beam of light fell on the crystal, he saw the vision too. A pale mist moved inside the egg, then cleared like a curtain being drawn back. It was rather like looking down from a great height through a small round window. Below him lay a broad plain, stretching away into the distance. There were enormous buildings, and trees of a kind that neither man had ever seen.

Near the buildings stood tall, thin masts, and on top of each mast shone a crystal egg exactly like Mr Cave's. Creatures moved through the air on wide, shining wings, and some of them settled on the masts. Their heads were round and large, and long, thin arms hung below them. As far as the men could tell, these creatures were the masters of that world.

For several weeks, Mr Cave came to Mr Wace's rooms in the evenings, and the two of them watched together. Mr Wace kept a careful record, noting the time, the light and every detail of the view. They soon found that the picture was clearest when the egg was turned at a certain angle to the light. Mr Wace decided to say nothing to anyone else until he had more proof. One night they noticed two small moons crossing the sky. "Two small moons," Mr Wace said slowly. "There is only one planet that fits. We are looking at Mars."

The idea that followed was even more remarkable. If the eggs on the masts were like this one, perhaps all the crystals were linked in some way. The Martians might be using them to watch the Earth, just as the two men were watching Mars. Once, while Mr Cave was alone at the crystal, a creature came very close and seemed to look straight out at him. He saw its large, dark eyes only a few inches away, and he drew back with a cry of surprise.

After some weeks, Mr Cave took the egg home to his shop again. Then, for several days, Mr Wace was kept busy by his work and could not visit. During those few days, the old man grew steadily weaker. One night he sat alone in the shop with the crystal, and he died there quietly before dawn. His wife found him in the morning, with the egg held gently in his hands.

Mrs Cave lost no time, and she sold the contents of the shop to a dealer. The crystal egg went with the rest. By the time Mr Wace heard the news and hurried to Seven Dials, the dealer had already sold it again. The buyer was a tall dark man in gray, and nobody had asked him his name. Mr Wace has searched for the egg ever since, and he has even placed notices in the newspapers. He still believes that he has looked into another world, and he means to find the egg again. Somewhere in London, perhaps, it still sits on a shelf, quietly showing a window into another world.

## Level C1

Near Seven Dials, in a part of London where narrow old streets run into one another, there once stood a small and rather dingy shop. Its window promised antiques but offered mostly dust. The shop had seen better days, and so, it must be said, had its owner. Its owner, Mr Cave, was a thin, stooping man with pale, watery eyes and a voice that always seemed on the point of apologizing. His health had been poor for some time, and at home he found little comfort, for his wife, a good deal younger than himself, had brought two grown children into the house. Between the three of them, the old man was hurried, scolded and generally overruled, and he had learned to bear it in silence.

Among the odds and ends that crowded the window lay an egg of polished crystal, with a little card beside it announcing a price of five pounds. Nobody had paid it much attention until one afternoon, when two customers stepped in together: a young clergyman and a dark young man. They turned the egg over in their hands, held it up to the light, and made it plain that they were willing to pay what the card asked. It was at this point that Mr Cave behaved very strangely. He grew flustered, invented one difficulty after another, and at last declared, with obvious discomfort, that the egg had been sold that very morning. The clergyman, more amused than offended, shrugged and moved towards the door, while his companion lingered a moment longer, looking at the egg with evident regret.

The customers went away puzzled, and from the back room, where she had heard every word, Mrs Cave emerged in a state of fury. "Five pounds walks in through the door, and you send it straight back out again!" she cried. Her son took up the quarrel with even greater energy, announcing that if his stepfather would not sell the thing, he would sell it himself. Mr Cave let the storm pass over him, as he always did; but this time, behind his mild expression, he had already made up his mind.

Early the next day, he wrapped the crystal in a cloth and carried it across London to Mr Jacoby Wace, a young teacher of science at one of the large hospitals. Mr Wace knew him only slightly, and he was surprised to find the old man standing at his door, trembling with anxiety. "I want you to keep this for me, sir," Mr Cave said. "If it stays at home, they'll sell it, and I could not bear to lose it." Then, haltingly, as if he expected to be laughed at, he explained what made a cheap piece of crystal so precious.

Some weeks earlier, unable to sleep, he had crept downstairs in the middle of the night and found the shop not quite dark. A faint glow, as pale as moonlight on milk, was coming from the crystal egg. Bending over it, he had looked not at his own reflection but into a deep and distant country, full of movement and strange color. Since then, he had returned to it again and again, always alone and always at night, guarding the secret as carefully as a miser guards his gold.

Mr Wace, being a man of science, listened with polite doubt and privately concluded that his visitor was unwell. Yet when the room was darkened and a single thin beam of light was directed onto the crystal, he found himself staring into the very scene the old man had described. At first there was only a soft, drifting mist; then, as if a curtain had been drawn aside, a landscape opened beneath him. It was rather like gazing down from a high tower through a small round window, and the view shifted a little whenever the egg was turned. A vast plain stretched away into the distance, crossed by huge buildings unlike anything on Earth and dotted with trees whose shapes he could not name.

Most remarkable of all were the masts. Tall and slender, they rose here and there near the buildings, and at the top of each one gleamed a crystal egg. So far as Mr Wace could judge, each was identical to the one in front of him. Around these masts drifted the creatures of that world, gliding on wide, glittering wings and sometimes settling on the masts as birds settle on a branch. Their heads were large and rounded, their eyes enormous, and beneath them hung long, thin arms that moved with a delicate, unhurried grace. Whatever they were, it seemed clear that they were the masters of the place.

From that day the two men shared an extraordinary secret. Several evenings a week, Mr Cave made his way to Mr Wace's rooms, and together they bent over the crystal. The young scientist wrote down everything: the hour, the angle of the light, the colors, and the movements of the winged creatures. They learned by trial and error that the vision was clearest at one particular angle to the light, and that any lamp in the room spoiled it. Mr Wace, for his part, resolved to say nothing to his colleagues until his notes were complete, knowing very well how they would greet such a tale. It was on one of these evenings that they noticed two small moons hanging in the sky of the strange world. "There is only one planet with two little moons like those," Mr Wace said at last, very quietly. "We are looking at Mars."

The discovery led them to a thought that was stranger still. If the crystals on the masts were the same as theirs, might they not be connected, each one a window opening onto the others? In that case, the Martians had perhaps sent this egg to Earth on purpose, and were watching London as eagerly as two Londoners were watching Mars. Once, while Mr Cave sat alone at the crystal, one of the creatures came so close that its face seemed to fill the whole egg. For a long moment its great dark eyes looked straight into his, and the old man pulled back with a gasp, his heart beating fast.

In time the storm at home died down, and Mr Cave carried the egg back to his shop, where he could look into it in the quiet hours after closing. Then Mr Wace's work kept him away for several days, and during that short absence the old man's strength began to fail. One night he sat alone in the back of the shop with the crystal before him, and some time before dawn he died, quietly and peacefully. His wife found him in the morning, with the egg still held gently between his hands.

Mrs Cave wasted little time on grief, and within days the whole stock of the shop had been sold to a dealer, crystal egg and all. By the time Mr Wace learned the news and hurried to Seven Dials, the dealer had already parted with the egg as well. He remembered the buyer only as a tall dark man dressed in gray, and nobody had thought to ask his name.

Mr Wace has been searching ever since. He has placed notices in the newspapers and visited dealers all over London. Meanwhile, his careful notes, written in the evenings with Mr Cave beside him, lie in a drawer, waiting for proof. Somewhere, perhaps, on a dusty shelf or in a stranger's study, the crystal egg still glows faintly in the dark. And on the far side of the sky, someone may still be looking through it.
