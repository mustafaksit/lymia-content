# Pollyanna

Summary: An orphan girl moves in with her strict aunt and teaches a gloomy town a happy game.

## Level A1

Pollyanna Whittier was eleven years old. Her mother and father were dead. So she went to live with her aunt. Aunt Polly was rich, but she never smiled.

Aunt Polly gave Pollyanna a little room. It was hot, and it had no pictures. But Pollyanna looked out of the window. "I can see the hills!" said Pollyanna.

Nancy worked in Aunt Polly's kitchen. "Why are you so happy?" asked Nancy. "I play a game," said Pollyanna. "My father taught me the glad game. Once I wanted a doll. But a box came with crutches in it. I did not need them. So I was glad!"

Mrs. Snow was ill in bed. She did not like anything. Pollyanna made Mrs. Snow's hair pretty. "Look in the mirror!" said Pollyanna. Then Mrs. Snow began to smile.

Mr. Pendleton lived alone on a hill. He was rich, but he was sad. He fell and hurt his leg. Pollyanna found him and ran for help. Long ago, he loved Pollyanna's mother. "Please live with me," said Mr. Pendleton. "I can't leave Aunt Polly," said Pollyanna.

Then a bad thing happened. A car hit Pollyanna in the street. Her legs did not move. "Now nothing is good," she cried.

But the town did not forget her. Many people came to Aunt Polly's door. "Now we play Pollyanna's game," they said. Jimmy Bean was a boy with no family. Mr. Pendleton took Jimmy into his home. Aunt Polly cried and learned the game too.

Long ago, Aunt Polly loved Dr. Chilton. But they argued and stopped talking. Dr. Chilton knew a way to help Pollyanna. Jimmy ran to Aunt Polly with the news. So Aunt Polly asked the doctor to come. Later, the two of them married.

Pollyanna went to a hospital far away. Then a letter came from her. "Today I can walk again!" she wrote. "I am so glad about my legs!"

## Level A2

Pollyanna Whittier was eleven when her father died. Her mother died years before, so the girl had no home. Her mother's sister, Miss Polly Harrington, agreed to take her. Miss Polly lived in a big house in Beldingsville. She was rich, but she was cold and strict.

Nancy, the young maid, met Pollyanna at the station. Aunt Polly gave her a small, hot room under the roof. It had no pictures and no mirror. "I'm glad there is no mirror, because I can't see my freckles. And this window is better than any picture," said Pollyanna.

"How can you be glad about everything?" asked Nancy. "It's a game," said Pollyanna. "My father taught it to me. Once I wanted a doll, but only a pair of crutches came. We were glad because I did not need them!"

One evening Pollyanna was late for supper. "You can eat bread and milk in the kitchen," said Aunt Polly. She wanted to punish the girl, but Pollyanna was happy. "I love bread and milk, and I love Nancy!" she said. Soon Nancy began to play the game too.

Mrs. Snow was a sick woman in the town. She stayed in bed and complained about everything. When people brought her chicken, she wanted lamb. Pollyanna brushed Mrs. Snow's hair and put a flower in it. Then she gave her a mirror. "You are pretty!" said Pollyanna. Mrs. Snow looked, and she smiled for the first time in years.

Mr. John Pendleton lived alone in a big house on a hill. He was rich, but he did not talk to anyone. One afternoon Pollyanna found him on the ground in the woods. He fell and hurt his leg, so he could not walk. Pollyanna ran to his house and called Dr. Chilton. Later she visited Mr. Pendleton often, and he grew kinder. Many years ago, he loved Pollyanna's mother. "Come and live with me," he said to Pollyanna. "I can't leave Aunt Polly," she answered.

Then, on her way home from school, a car hit Pollyanna. Now her legs did not move. Maybe she could never walk again. "Now I can't find anything good," Pollyanna cried.

But the people of Beldingsville did not forget her. Many of them came to Aunt Polly's door. "Please thank Pollyanna for us. Her glad game changed our lives," they said. Mr. Pendleton took Jimmy Bean, a poor boy, into his home. Aunt Polly began to cry. "I play the game too now," she told Pollyanna.

Dr. Chilton loved Aunt Polly when they were young. But they argued, and for years they did not speak. Now he knew a way to help Pollyanna. But Aunt Polly was too proud to ask him. Jimmy heard about this, and he ran to Aunt Polly. That same day, she asked Dr. Chilton to come. Soon after, the two of them married.

Pollyanna went to a hospital far away for almost a year. Then she wrote a letter home. "Today I walked to the window by myself! Now I know how good it is to have legs," she wrote.

## Level B1

Pollyanna Whittier was eleven when her father, a poor minister in the West, died. Her mother was already dead, so the little girl was alone in the world. Her only family was her mother's sister, Miss Polly Harrington, who lived in Beldingsville. Years before, Pollyanna's mother married the minister against her family's wishes. Miss Polly never forgot that, and she did not want a child in her quiet house. Still, she believed in duty, so she agreed to take her niece.

Nancy, the young maid, was waiting at the station. On the way home, Pollyanna talked without stopping, and Nancy liked her at once. Miss Polly met her niece without a kiss. She gave her a hot little room in the attic with no pictures and no mirror. For a moment Pollyanna's face fell, but then she went to the window. "I'm glad there's no mirror, because I won't see my freckles. And who needs pictures with a view like this?" she said.

Nancy was puzzled. "How can you be glad about a room like this?" she asked. "It's the game," Pollyanna explained. "Father and I always played it. Once I hoped for a doll from a box of church gifts. But only a pair of small crutches came. Father said I could be glad, because I didn't need them! The harder it is, the more fun the game gets."

On her first evening, Pollyanna climbed a big rock and came home late for supper. Miss Polly sent her to the kitchen to eat bread and milk with Nancy. She meant it as a punishment, but Pollyanna was delighted. "I love bread and milk, and I love you, Nancy!" she said. Soon Nancy was playing the game too. She hated Mondays, but now she found a reason to be glad about them. On Monday evening, the next Monday was a whole week away!

In the town lived Mrs. Snow, a sick woman who never left her bed. Whatever people brought her, she always wanted something else. So Pollyanna came with jelly, chicken and lamb, and Mrs. Snow could choose. Then the girl brushed her hair and put a pink flower in it. "You look lovely," Pollyanna said, and she held up a mirror. Mrs. Snow stared at herself, and her face grew softer. Slowly she began to play the game, and she started knitting for other people.

Mr. John Pendleton was the richest and loneliest man in town. He lived in a big gray house on a hill and spoke to almost no one. One day Pollyanna found him in the woods, lying on the ground with a hurt leg. He gave her his key, and she ran to his house to telephone Dr. Chilton. While he was getting better, she visited him often. Slowly the unfriendly old man began to laugh. One afternoon he told her a secret. Long ago, he loved her mother, but her mother married someone else. "Come and live with me, Pollyanna. This house needs a child to make it a home," he begged. Pollyanna was sorry, but she could not leave Aunt Polly.

Then, on her way home from school, a car hit Pollyanna in the street. For days, she lay in bed, and her legs did not move. A famous doctor came from a big city to see her. Later Pollyanna heard the truth by accident: she might never walk again. For the first time, the glad game did not help. "I can't find a single thing to be glad about," she cried.

Then a strange thing began to happen. One by one, people from all over Beldingsville came to Miss Polly's door. Some brought flowers, and all of them had the same message. "Tell her that we are playing her game now," said one woman. Mr. Pendleton came too, and he was taking Jimmy Bean into his home. Jimmy was a lonely boy, and Pollyanna always wanted a family for him. At last Miss Polly understood what Pollyanna gave to the whole town. She went upstairs and took her niece's hand. "I'm playing the game too, my dear," she said softly.

Miss Polly also had an old sadness. Many years ago, she and Dr. Chilton loved each other. But they quarreled, and after that they never spoke. Now the doctor believed he could help Pollyanna. But Miss Polly was too proud to call him. Jimmy heard the doctor say this to Mr. Pendleton, and he ran to Miss Polly. That same day, Dr. Chilton came, and Miss Polly met him at the door. Soon Pollyanna learned that her aunt was going to marry him.

Dr. Chilton sent Pollyanna to a special hospital far away. Almost a year later, a letter arrived in Beldingsville. "Dear Aunt Polly, today I walked to the window by myself! Now I know how wonderful legs are, so I'm even glad about those months," Pollyanna wrote.

## Level B2

Pollyanna Whittier was only eleven when her father, a poor minister in the far West, died and left her alone. Her mother had died years earlier, so the only family she had left was her mother's sister, Miss Polly Harrington of Beldingsville. Long ago, the Harrington family had been bitterly against that marriage, and Miss Polly had never quite forgiven it. Now she lived by herself in the big Harrington house, rich, proud and rather lonely, caring more for duty than for joy. She did not want a child, but it was clearly her duty, so she wrote that the girl could come.

Nancy, the young maid, went to meet the train, half afraid of what she would find. Out jumped a thin, freckled girl who threw her arms around her and began talking at once. Miss Polly received her niece without a kiss and took her straight up to a small room under the roof. The attic was hot and bare, with no carpet, no pictures and no mirror. For a moment Pollyanna's face fell, but then she ran to the window and gasped at the green hills. "I'm glad there's no mirror, because then I can't see my freckles. And who would want pictures on the wall with a view like this outside?" she said to Nancy.

Nancy stared at her, and Pollyanna explained. "It's a game Father taught me, the glad game. Once I wanted a doll very much, and we hoped to find one in a box of gifts from some church ladies. But there was only a pair of little crutches inside. Father said we could be glad about them, because I didn't need them! We've played it ever since, and the harder it is to find something, the more fun it becomes."

On her very first evening, Pollyanna climbed a big rock to watch the sunset and came home late for supper. As a punishment, Miss Polly sent her to the kitchen to eat bread and milk with Nancy. Pollyanna was delighted, since she loved bread and milk and loved Nancy even more. Before long, Nancy was playing the game too. She even found a reason to be glad about Mondays: once one was over, the next was a week away. Old Tom, the gardener, told the girl that her aunt had once been very pretty. She had also once been in love, he said.

One day Pollyanna met a boy named Jimmy Bean, who had left a children's home because he wanted a real family. She brought him straight to Aunt Polly, but her aunt refused to take in a strange boy. Next, Pollyanna went to the ladies of the church and begged them to find Jimmy a home. They listened politely, but they preferred to send their money to poor children on the other side of the world. Pollyanna could not understand it, but she did not give up on her friend.

She also began to carry food from her aunt's kitchen to Mrs. Snow. Mrs. Snow was a sick woman who had spent years in bed in a dark room. Mrs. Snow always wanted whatever she had not been given; if someone brought chicken, she would have preferred lamb. So, on her second visit, Pollyanna arrived with jelly, chicken and lamb, and the astonished woman could find nothing to complain about. Then the girl brushed Mrs. Snow's hair, tucked a pink flower into it and held up a mirror. For the first time in years, Mrs. Snow looked at herself and almost smiled. Before long she was knitting bright little things for other people and playing the game herself.

The person Pollyanna liked best, however, was the strangest man in town, Mr. John Pendleton. He lived alone in a great gray house on Pendleton Hill, and people said he kept a secret. One afternoon, in the woods near his house, she found him on the ground. He had fallen and hurt his leg, and he could not stand. He sent her to his empty house with his keys, and she telephoned Dr. Chilton, who soon came with help. During the long weeks of getting better, he looked forward to her visits, and slowly he learned to laugh again. At last, he told her his secret. As a young man, he had loved her mother, but she had chosen to marry someone else. "Come and live with me, Pollyanna. A house needs a child in it before it can be a real home," he begged. But Pollyanna could not leave her aunt, and she sadly said no.

Then, one day on her way home from school, Pollyanna was struck by a car as she crossed the street. She was carried home and put to bed, and when she woke up, she could not move her legs. A famous doctor came from a distant city. Later Pollyanna overheard words she was never meant to hear: she might never walk again. For the first time in her life, the game failed her. "I can't think of a single thing to be glad about now, so even the game doesn't work anymore," she sobbed.

Then something remarkable happened. One after another, the people of Beldingsville began to knock at Miss Polly's door. Some had stopped worrying since Pollyanna taught them the game, and others had learned to laugh again. Mrs. Snow sent word that she was glad for the first time in years. Mr. Pendleton came to say that he had taken Jimmy Bean as his own boy, just as Pollyanna had wished. Each visitor left the same message for the girl upstairs: they were all playing her game. Miss Polly listened, and slowly she realized how much her little niece had given the whole town. She went upstairs, sat by the bed and took Pollyanna's hand. "I'm playing it too now, my dear," she said, "and it's helping me more than you know."

Miss Polly had a sorrow of her own. Twenty years earlier, she and Dr. Chilton had loved each other, but a foolish quarrel had separated them. Since then, her pride had kept them apart. Dr. Chilton believed that a doctor he knew could help Pollyanna, but he could not come unless Miss Polly asked him. One day Jimmy overheard him telling Mr. Pendleton this, and the boy ran straight to the Harrington house. That same day, Dr. Chilton walked through Miss Polly's door, and soon Pollyanna learned that her aunt was going to marry him.

Pollyanna was sent to a hospital in another town, where she stayed for nearly ten months. Then a letter came home to Beldingsville. "Dear Aunt Polly, today I walked from my bed to the window all by myself! I'm glad I couldn't walk for a while, because now I understand what a gift legs are," she wrote.

## Level C1

When the letter arrived announcing that her sister's child was now an orphan, Miss Polly Harrington read it twice, folded it neatly and decided, without the slightest pleasure, to do her duty. Her sister had married a penniless minister against the wishes of the whole family and gone off with him to the far West. The Harrington family had never quite forgiven either of them. Now both parents were dead, and eleven-year-old Pollyanna Whittier had nobody in the world except an aunt she had never met. Miss Polly, who lived alone in the largest house in Beldingsville with her money, her pride and a great many rules, wrote back that the girl might come.

Nancy, the young maid, was sent to the station, quietly dreading the sort of child such a mistress would be expecting. What stepped off the train was a thin, freckled girl who flung her arms around her, mistook her at first for Aunt Polly, and then chattered the whole way home. Her aunt received her with a cool handshake and led her straight up to the attic, to a small, airless room with bare boards, bare walls and no mirror at all. For one moment the brightness went out of the girl's face; then she crossed to the window, looked out over the hills, and it came flooding back. "I'm glad there's no looking-glass, because then I can't see my freckles. And nobody needs pictures on the wall with a picture like that outside," she told Nancy, who was blinking back tears on her behalf.

It was Nancy who first heard about the game. Pollyanna's father, it seemed, had invented it on the day a box of gifts arrived from some distant church ladies, a box in which his daughter had hoped to find a doll. "There wasn't any doll, only a little pair of crutches that somebody had sent. Father said the game was to find something to be glad about in anything, and we decided I could be glad about those crutches, because I didn't need them! The harder it is, the better the game gets," the child explained, as though it were the simplest thing on earth.

Miss Polly, meanwhile, set about bringing up her niece according to her own strict notions. When Pollyanna wandered off on her first evening to climb a great rock and watch the sunset, she returned to find herself condemned to bread and milk in the kitchen. She was enchanted, for she adored bread and milk and was already rather fond of Nancy. Before long Nancy was playing the game as well, and she even contrived to be glad about wash-day Mondays, since by Monday evening the next one lay a week away. From Old Tom, the gardener, who had served the family for decades, Pollyanna learned that her aunt had once been a pretty girl. There had once been a young man, too. One stifling summer night, unable to sleep under the roof, the child crept out with her pillow onto a flat roof below her window. It was Aunt Polly, startled and secretly touched, who found her there and brought her down to sleep in her own room.

Pollyanna's wanderings carried the game far beyond the Harrington gate. She found Jimmy Bean, a ragged boy who had walked out of a children's home in search of a real family, and marched him straight to her aunt, who refused him. Undaunted, she put his case before the ladies of the church, only to discover that they would rather send their money to children on the other side of the world. Those children, it appeared, looked better in the yearly report. She did not quite understand this, but she resolved that Jimmy would have a home somehow.

There was Mrs. Snow, too, an invalid who had lain for years in a darkened room, finding fault with everyone and everything. Whatever was brought to her was invariably the wrong thing; she wanted chicken on jelly days and lamb on chicken days. Pollyanna, having been warned, arrived on her second visit with all three, and the astonished woman, for once, had nothing to grumble about. The child then drew back the curtains, arranged the thin hair on the pillow, slipped a pink flower into it and held up a hand mirror. In it, Mrs. Snow saw a face she had nearly forgotten. Within weeks, she was knitting soft, bright things for strangers and, to her own surprise, looking for reasons to be glad.

In the woods below Pendleton Hill, Pollyanna one afternoon came upon the town's most famous recluse, Mr. John Pendleton, lying among the leaves with a hurt leg. He gave her his keys; she ran through his silent house to the telephone and summoned Dr. Chilton, a quiet and kindly doctor. The weeks of recovery turned into a friendship that astonished the whole town, for the grim old man began to look forward to her chatter and, eventually, to laugh at it. Then, one day, he confided his secret: as a young man he had loved Pollyanna's mother, who had chosen the poor minister instead. "Come and live with me, Pollyanna. A house is never a home until there is a child in it," he pleaded. She loved him dearly, yet she could not bring herself to leave Aunt Polly, who, she suspected, had begun to need her more than either of them admitted. Instead, she begged him to give his lonely house to Jimmy Bean, who needed a home far more than she did.

She also met the town's minister one afternoon on the hill, sitting with his head in his hands and a stern sermon in his pocket. Pollyanna told him that her father had once counted all the happy, rejoicing verses in the Bible. There were so many of them, she said, that people were surely meant to be glad, and the minister went home and wrote a very different sermon.

It was on an ordinary afternoon, on her way home from school, that a car struck Pollyanna as she crossed the road. She was carried home and laid in the best bedroom, and when she woke she found that her legs would not obey her. A celebrated specialist travelled from a distant city to examine her. Through a door left carelessly ajar, she overheard the verdict she was never meant to hear: she might never walk again. For the first time, the game failed her entirely. "Aunt Polly, I can't find anything at all to be glad about," she whispered, and for days she simply lay with her face turned to the wall.

What happened next astonished Miss Polly more than anything in her orderly life. People she barely knew began to appear at her door, one after another, with flowers and halting words, each insisting that the child upstairs must hear what she had done for them. A tired widow had put on a brighter ribbon; a quarrelling couple had made peace; a man close to giving up had found the courage to go on. Mrs. Snow sent word that she was knitting and glad. Mr. Pendleton came in person to announce that he had taken Jimmy Bean as his own son, exactly as Pollyanna had once begged him to. Only then did Miss Polly grasp what the whole town had understood for months. She climbed the stairs, sat beside the bed and confessed, rather shyly, that she was playing the game herself.

Yet a deeper unhappiness remained, and it concerned Dr. Chilton. Twenty years earlier, he and Polly Harrington had quarrelled over some trifle, and her pride, which had outlived the quarrel by two decades, still kept her door shut against him. He was convinced that a colleague of his could cure the child, but a doctor could hardly force his way into a house uninvited, and Miss Polly would never invite him. It was Jimmy Bean who, hearing the doctor explain all this to Mr. Pendleton, ran the whole way to the Harrington house and blurted it out. That very day, Dr. Chilton stood in Miss Polly's hall, and before long Pollyanna was rejoicing, a little tearfully, that her aunt was going to marry him.

The specialist's hospital lay many miles from Beldingsville, and Pollyanna spent almost ten months there, learning patiently to stand, then to take a step, then another. At last a letter reached her aunt and her new uncle, written in a large, excited hand. "Today I walked all the way from my bed to the window, six whole steps! I'm almost glad that my legs stopped working for a while, because nobody really knows how wonderful legs are until they have had to do without them," she wrote.
