# Little Women: A Christmas Without Presents

Summary: Four poor sisters wait for a Christmas without presents while their father is far away.

## Level A1

It was a cold night in December. Four sisters sat by the fire. Their names were Meg, Jo, Beth and Amy. Their father was far away in the war. "Christmas is no fun without presents," said Jo. "We are poor this year," said Meg. "We have Mother and each other," said Beth.

Each girl had one dollar. Jo wanted a new book. Then Jo looked at Mother's old slippers. "Let's buy presents for Mother," said Beth. Meg bought gloves, and Jo bought slippers.

Mother came home in the evening. The girls called her Marmee. She had a letter from Father. "Be good, my dear girls. Help your mother and work hard. I love you all," wrote Father. The girls cried a little. "We can try," said Amy.

On Christmas morning, each girl found a book. It was a gift from Marmee. But Marmee was not at home. Hannah worked in the house. "Your mother is not here. She went with a poor woman," said Hannah.

Soon Marmee came back. "Merry Christmas, girls!" she said. Then she looked sad. "A poor family lives near here. There are six children and a new baby. They have no fire and no food. Can you give them your breakfast?"

The girls were very hungry. For a minute, nobody spoke. "Yes, let's go!" said Jo. They carried the food through the snow. Hannah carried wood for a fire. The children sat together in one bed. The girls gave food to the children. They ate and laughed. "You are angels!" they cried.

At home, the sisters ate bread and milk. But it was a happy breakfast. Then they gave Marmee the presents. She smiled and kissed her girls.

In the evening, there was a surprise. The table had cake, ice cream and flowers! Amy asked, "Who sent this?" Marmee smiled. "Old Mr. Laurence sent it. He lives next door. He heard about your breakfast." Beth looked at her flowers. "I want to send these to Father."

## Level B1

On a cold December evening, the four March sisters sat by the fire with their sewing. "A Christmas without presents is hardly a Christmas at all," Jo grumbled from the rug. Meg sighed and looked down at her old dress. "I hate being poor," she said. "It isn't fair, because other girls have so many pretty things," said Amy, the youngest. From her corner, Beth spoke quietly. "We still have Father and Mother, and we have each other." The fire seemed brighter for a moment, but then Jo remembered the war. "We haven't got Father, and we won't have him for a long time," she said. Nobody answered, because their father was far away with the army.

Meg was sixteen and very pretty, and she taught small children for money. Jo was fifteen, tall and boyish, and she spent her days with a rich old aunt. Beth was thirteen, shy and gentle, and she stayed at home to help. Amy was twelve, and she loved drawing more than anything.

Their mother, whom the girls called Marmee, didn't want presents this year. The winter was hard for everyone, and soldiers were suffering in the army. Still, each girl had one dollar of her own. Jo was planning to buy a book, and Beth wanted new music for the old piano. Amy wanted good drawing pencils, and Meg wanted pretty things too. Then Jo noticed Marmee's slippers, which were warming by the fire. "They're worn out, so Marmee should have a new pair," she said. "Let's each buy her something and get nothing for ourselves," Beth said. The others agreed at once. Meg chose gloves, Jo chose slippers, and Beth chose handkerchiefs. Amy decided on a small bottle of perfume, because it was cheap.

When Marmee came home, she brought good news. "There's a letter from Father," she said, smiling. The girls gathered around her chair. Father wrote about the cold camps and the long marches. At the end, he sent a special message to his girls. He asked them to be good, to work hard and to fight their own faults. Amy hid her face and said she was a selfish girl. "I'll try to be better," she promised. "Father calls me his little woman. I'll try to be one, and not so wild," said Jo. Beth said nothing, but she decided to try too.

On Christmas morning, each girl found a little book under her pillow. It was Marmee's gift, and it was full of good stories for hard days. But when they ran downstairs, Marmee wasn't there. "A poor woman came begging, and your mother went to see what she needed," said Hannah. Hannah was the family's old servant and a good friend to them all. Just then Amy came in, a little red in the face. That morning she took her small bottle back to the shop for a bigger one. "I want to stop being selfish," she explained.

Soon Marmee came back, and the girls wished her a merry Christmas. "Thank you, my dears," she said. Then her voice grew serious. "Before we eat, I want to tell you something. Near here, there is a poor woman with a new baby and six other children. They have no fire, so the children are all in one bed to keep warm. They have nothing to eat. Will you give them your breakfast as a Christmas present?" The girls were very hungry, and for a minute nobody said a word. Then Jo cried, "I'm so glad you came before we started!"

They carried the cakes, the bread and the cream through the quiet streets. Hannah followed with wood for the fire. The room was bare and cold, and some of the windows were broken. Mrs. Hummel cried with happiness when she saw them. Hannah lit a fire, and Marmee took care of the mother and the tiny baby. The girls fed the hungry children, who laughed and talked in German. The children called them "angel children," and the girls felt warm inside. Back home, they ate bread and milk, and it was their best breakfast ever.

After breakfast, they gave Marmee their presents. She was so surprised and pleased that she nearly cried. She put on the new slippers and the gloves at once.

That evening, a wonderful supper appeared on the table. There was ice cream, cake, fruit, sweets and four bunches of flowers. "Did fairies bring it?" asked Amy. Marmee laughed and shook her head. "No, old Mr. Laurence sent it. He knew your grandfather a long time ago. Hannah told one of his servants about your breakfast, and he liked your kindness." "His grandson lives with him, and I want to meet that boy one day," said Jo. Beth held her flowers and thought of Father in the cold camps. "I wish I could send these to Father," she said softly.

## Level B2

It was a gray December evening, and the four March sisters sat sewing by a small fire. "Christmas without presents is hardly worth having," Jo grumbled, lying on the rug. Meg sighed and smoothed her old dress. "Being poor is simply dreadful," she said. "It isn't fair that some girls have lovely things while others have none," added Amy, the youngest. "We've still got Father and Mother, and we've got each other," said Beth from her corner. For a moment the fire seemed brighter, until Jo spoke again. "We haven't got Father now, and we won't have him for a long time," she said. No one spoke for a minute. Each of them thought of their father, far away where the fighting was, and wondered when he would come home.

The sisters were very different from one another. Meg, sixteen and pretty, earned a little money by teaching small children. Jo, fifteen, was tall and boyish, and she spent her days reading to a rich and difficult old aunt. Beth, thirteen, was so shy that she stayed at home and helped with the housework. Amy, twelve, went to school, loved drawing, and liked to think of herself as a young lady.

Earlier that winter, their mother had asked them not to buy presents. The girls called her Marmee, and she believed they shouldn't spend money on pleasure while soldiers were suffering. Still, each girl had saved a single dollar, and they began to talk about what they would buy. Jo wanted a book she had been dreaming about, and Beth wanted new music for the old piano. Amy needed drawing pencils, and Meg admitted that she wanted pretty things too. Then Beth put Marmee's slippers by the fire to warm, and Jo saw how worn out they were. "Marmee ought to have a new pair," she said. "Let's each buy her something, and buy nothing for ourselves," said Beth. The idea delighted everyone. Meg would buy gloves, Jo the slippers, and Beth some handkerchiefs, which she would sew herself. Amy chose a small bottle of perfume, because it would leave her a little money for pencils.

At six o'clock Marmee came in, tired but cheerful, and announced that there was a letter from Father. The girls crowded around her. Father had been too old to fight, so he had gone to the army to look after the soldiers' hearts and spirits. His letter described the camps and the marches, and at the end he sent a message to his girls. He asked them to be good, to do their work cheerfully and to win the battle against their own faults. When he came home, he wrote, he hoped to be prouder of his little women than ever. Amy hid her face and confessed that she was selfish, but she promised to try harder. "Father calls me his little woman. I'll try to deserve it and stop being so rough and wild," said Jo. Beth wiped away a tear and said nothing, but she made her own quiet promise.

On Christmas morning, each girl found a little book under her pillow, a gift from Marmee to guide them through the year. When they hurried downstairs, however, their mother was gone. "Some poor woman came begging, and your mother went straight off to see what was needed," said Hannah. Hannah had worked for the family since Meg was born, and she was more of a friend than a servant. Just then Amy slipped in from the cold, looking rather embarrassed. She had taken her small bottle back to the shop and exchanged it for the largest one. "I spent all my money on it, because I don't want to be selfish anymore," she explained.

Marmee soon returned, and the girls wished her a merry Christmas. "Merry Christmas, little daughters," she said. Then her face grew serious. "Before we sit down, I want to say one thing. Not far from here, a poor woman is lying in bed with a newborn baby. Her six other children are crowded into one bed to keep from freezing, because they have no fire. There is nothing at all to eat. Will you give them your breakfast as a Christmas present?" They were all unusually hungry, and for a minute no one spoke. "I'm so glad you came before we began!" Jo burst out.

So they set off through the early streets, carrying the buckwheat cakes, the bread and the cream. Hannah followed them with wood for the fire. The room they entered was bare and miserable, with broken windows and no warmth at all. Mrs. Hummel wept with relief when she saw them. Hannah built a fire and covered the broken glass with old hats and her own shawl. Marmee gave the mother tea and warm porridge and dressed the little baby gently. Meanwhile, the girls fed the hungry children, who laughed and chattered in German. "Angel children!" they called them, and the sisters felt the name was the nicest present of all. When they finally went home, they ate bread and milk, and no breakfast had ever tasted better.

Afterward they gave Marmee their presents, and she was touched almost to tears. She put on the slippers at once and tucked a handkerchief into her pocket. The handkerchief smelled sweetly of Amy's perfume, and the gloves fit perfectly.

In the evening the girls performed a play that Jo had written, full of castles, a witch and secret plots. Afterward, Hannah announced that supper was ready, and the girls could hardly believe their eyes. On the table stood ice cream, cake, fruit, French sweets and four large bunches of flowers. "Is it fairies?" asked Amy. Marmee smiled at their amazed faces. "It was old Mr. Laurence. He knew your grandfather many years ago. When Hannah told his servant about your breakfast, he asked if he could share in your Christmas." "His grandson lives there too. Our cat ran away once, and he brought her back. He seems lonely, and I mean to know him someday," said Jo. Beth looked at her flowers for a long moment and thought of the cold camps far away. "I wish I could send my bunch to Father," she said.

## Level C1

Gray December light was fading from the windows as the four March sisters sat sewing by a fire that gave more cheer than heat. "Christmas without presents hardly deserves the name," Jo grumbled, stretched out on the hearth rug with her knitting abandoned beside her. Meg looked down at her faded dress and sighed that poverty was a dreadful thing. Amy, the youngest, sniffed that it was hardly fair for some girls to own every pretty thing while others owned nothing at all. From her corner Beth answered, in her contented way, that they still had their parents and one another. The fire seemed to burn brighter at those words, until Jo, never one to soften a truth, reminded them that Father was gone and would be gone for a long while. Nobody added that he might never come back, yet the thought hung over the room, for he was far away where the fighting was.

Anyone watching them that evening would have found four very different girls. Meg, at sixteen, was pretty and a little vain, and she earned modest wages teaching the children of a wealthy family. Jo, a year younger, was all long limbs and sharp opinions, a tomboy who kept a demanding old aunt company by day and dreamed of becoming a writer. Beth, thirteen and painfully shy, kept house with quiet devotion and loved the old piano that would not stay in tune. Amy, twelve, carried herself like a young lady of fashion and worried, with great seriousness, about the shape of her nose.

Weeks earlier, their mother, whom they had always called Marmee, had suggested that there should be no presents that year. The winter promised to be hard for everybody, she reminded them, and it seemed wrong to spend money on pleasure while the soldiers were suffering. Each girl, however, had quietly kept a single dollar, and the conversation soon turned to how that dollar might be spent. Jo longed for a particular book, Beth for new music and Amy for good drawing pencils, while Meg, trying to sound sensible, admitted to wanting a few pretty things. It was Beth, setting Marmee's slippers on the hearth to warm, who changed the plan, for Jo noticed how worn they had become and declared that their mother deserved a new pair. "Then let us each buy her something and buy nothing at all for ourselves," Beth suggested, and the proposal was taken up with delight. Meg settled on gloves, Jo on the slippers, and Beth on a set of handkerchiefs that she meant to hem with her own careful stitches. Amy, who still had an eye on her pencils, chose a small bottle of perfume so that a little of her money would remain.

At six o'clock Marmee came home from her work, tired but warm-hearted as ever, and announced with a smile that she had brought a letter from Father. The girls drew their chairs close around her. Too old to be called up as a soldier, he had gone to the army as a chaplain, to care for the spirits of the men rather than to fight beside them. His letter spoke cheerfully of camps and marches and gave little attention to the hardships that must have filled his days. Only at the end did his tone change, when he asked his girls to be patient, to do their duties without complaint and to fight the faults within themselves bravely. He hoped, he wrote, that when the long year was over he would be prouder than ever of his little women. Amy buried her face in her mother's shoulder and confessed that she was a selfish girl, though she meant to do better. Jo, staring hard at the fire, said that her father liked to call her his little woman and that she would try to deserve the name. She would stop being so rough and wild, she added. Beth said nothing, but she wiped her eyes on the stocking she was knitting and set to work again with a new determination.

Christmas dawned gray and cold, and Jo was the first to wake. Beneath her pillow lay a small book, and each of her sisters soon found one too, a gift from Marmee meant to guide them through the year ahead. Yet when they hurried downstairs to thank her, their mother was nowhere to be found. Hannah, who had served the family since Meg was born and was more a friend than a servant, explained that some poor creature had come begging. The mistress, she said, had gone straight off with her. A moment later Amy crept in from the street, flushed and a little embarrassed, carrying a bottle far grander than the one she had bought. She had exchanged her small bottle for the largest in the shop and spent every cent she had, because, she admitted, she was truly trying not to be selfish anymore.

When Marmee returned, the girls greeted her with a chorus of good wishes, and she thanked them before turning serious. "Not far from here lies a poor woman with a newborn baby," she said. "Her six other children are huddled together in one bed to keep from freezing, since they have no fire, and there is nothing in the house to eat. My girls, will you give them your breakfast as a Christmas present?" They had waited nearly an hour and were unusually hungry, so for a minute nobody spoke. Then Jo broke the silence, declaring that she was only glad their mother had come back before they started.

They set off in the early morning, when the streets were still nearly empty, carrying the buckwheat cakes, the bread, the muffins and the jug of cream. Hannah followed, her arms full of firewood. The room they entered was bare and miserable, with broken windowpanes, ragged blankets and a sick mother trying to quiet her crying baby. Mrs. Hummel wept with relief, convinced that heaven had sent them. While Hannah built a fire and stuffed the broken panes with old hats and her own shawl, Marmee gave the mother tea and warm porridge. Then she dressed the little one as tenderly as if it were her own. The girls, meanwhile, set out the food and fed the hungry children, who laughed and chattered in a German that none of the sisters could quite follow. One word, however, needed no translation, and when the children called them angel children, the girls agreed that it was the finest gift they had received. Walking home, they decided that no one in the city could be merrier than four hungry girls who had given their breakfast away, and they made do with bread and milk.

After that came the presents for Marmee, who laughed and cried at once over her daughters' modest gifts. The slippers went on immediately, a handkerchief scented with Amy's perfume went into her pocket, and the gloves, she announced, were a perfect fit.

In the evening Jo's play was staged for a small audience of friends, a breathless drama full of castles, a witch and secret plots, in which the scenery collapsed only once.

Hardly had the laughter faded when Hannah announced that supper was served, and the girls stared at the table in astonishment. Before them stood pink and white ice cream, cake, fruit, French sweets and, in the middle, four great bunches of flowers. "Is it fairies?" Amy breathed, gazing at the feast as if it might vanish. The truth was simpler, and stranger, for it had come from old Mr. Laurence, the rich and rather proud gentleman next door. He had known their grandfather long ago, Marmee explained. When Hannah mentioned the breakfast to one of his servants, the old gentleman sent a polite note asking permission to show his friendly feeling in this way. Jo remarked that his grandson lived there too, a boy who had once returned their runaway cat and who looked as if he needed company. She was determined, she added, to know him. Beth said little, but she lifted her bunch of flowers and gazed at it for a long moment. "I wish I could send these to Father," she said softly, "for I'm afraid his Christmas is not as merry as ours."
