# Odysseus and Circe

Summary: A Greek king and his tired sailors land on the island of a powerful sorceress.

## Level A1

Odysseus was a Greek king. His home was the island of Ithaca. The war at Troy was over. Now Odysseus sailed home with his men. The way was long and hard. One evening, their ship came to Aeaea. The men were very tired and sad.

Odysseus climbed a hill. He saw smoke above the trees. "Somebody lives there," said Odysseus. Eurylochus took some men to the house. Wolves and lions sat near the door. But the animals were kind and friendly.

A woman sang inside the house. Her name was Circe. She was a sorceress. "Come in, please!" said Circe. The men went in, but Eurylochus stayed outside. Circe gave the men a sweet drink. Then she touched them with her magic stick. Suddenly, the men were pigs!

The pigs cried, but they could not speak. Circe gave them nuts to eat. Eurylochus ran back to the ship. "Our friends are gone!" he cried.

Odysseus went to the house alone. On the way, he met the god Hermes. "Take this plant," said Hermes. "It has a black root. Her magic can't hurt you now."

Circe gave Odysseus the sweet drink too. She touched him with her magic stick. But nothing happened! Circe was very surprised. "You are Odysseus!" she said. "Hermes told me about you."

"Please free my men," said Odysseus. Circe put magic oil on the pigs. They were men again, young and tall. Everybody cried with joy.

They all stayed with Circe for a year. Then the men wanted to go home. Circe told Odysseus about the Sirens. "They sing beautiful songs," said Circe. "Sailors go to them and forget their homes."

Soon the ship came near the Sirens. Odysseus put wax in his men's ears. "Tie me to the mast," he said. Then he heard the lovely song. Odysseus pulled at the ropes. But his men only tied him tighter. The ship passed the Sirens. Then the men let their king go.

## Level A2

The war at Troy was over at last. Odysseus, a Greek king, wanted to go home to Ithaca. But the sea was wild, and the journey was long. After many hard days, his ship came to the island of Aeaea. The tired men lay on the beach for two days.

On the third morning, Odysseus climbed a high rock. He saw smoke above the trees. Eurylochus took twenty-two men and went to find the house.

Wolves and lions walked around the house. The men were afraid, but the animals played like happy dogs. Inside, a woman sang a lovely song. She was Circe, a powerful sorceress. "Come in, come in," she said. All the men went inside, but Eurylochus did not. He was afraid of a trick, so he waited outside.

Circe gave her guests a sweet drink with honey. But there was dark magic in the drink. The men drank, and then she touched them with her magic stick. At once, they had the heads and bodies of pigs. Their minds stayed the same, so they cried and cried. Circe gave them nuts and locked them up.

Eurylochus waited a long time, but nobody came out. He ran back to the ship in tears and told Odysseus everything. "I must help my friends," said Odysseus.

On the forest path, a young man stopped him. It was the god Hermes. "Take this plant with you," said Hermes. "It has a black root and a white flower. With this plant, Circe's magic can't hurt you. When she touches you with her stick, pull out your sword."

Odysseus went to the house, and Circe gave him the same drink. Then she touched him with her stick. But Odysseus did not change. He pulled out his sword, and Circe fell to her knees. "No man can drink my drink and stay the same," said Circe. "You must be Odysseus. Hermes told me about you long ago." Odysseus made her promise to do him no harm.

"Now please free my men," said Odysseus. Circe put magic oil on each pig. Soon they were men again, younger and taller than before. They all cried with joy.

Odysseus brought the other men from the ship. They all stayed with Circe for a whole year. Then the men said, "Remember Ithaca. It is time to go home." Before they left, Circe told Odysseus about the Sirens. "Sailors hear their song and forget their homes," she said. "Put wax in your men's ears. You can listen, but your men must tie you to the mast."

Near the island of the Sirens, the wind stopped. Odysseus softened some wax and put it in his men's ears. Then the men tied him to the mast with strong ropes.

Soon the sweet voices of the Sirens came over the water. "Come to us, famous Odysseus!" sang the Sirens. "We know all about the war at Troy." Odysseus pulled hard at the ropes. But Eurylochus and another man only tied him tighter. The ship moved on, and the song grew quiet. At last, the men took the wax from their ears. Then they let their king go.

## Level B1

After ten long years, the war at Troy was finally over. Odysseus, the clever king of Ithaca, set sail for home with his men. The journey was full of trouble, and by now he had only one ship. One evening, it came into a quiet bay on the island of Aeaea. The men were so tired that they lay on the beach for two whole days.

On the third morning, Odysseus climbed a hill and saw smoke above the forest. He divided his men into two groups. Eurylochus took twenty-two men to find the house, while the others waited by the ship.

In the forest, they found a fine stone house. Wolves and mountain lions were walking around it, but the animals did not attack. Instead, they came up to the men like friendly dogs. Inside, a woman was singing as she worked on a beautiful cloth. This was Circe, a sorceress who knew many kinds of magic. "Welcome, strangers," she said with a warm smile. "Come in and rest." All of them followed her inside, except Eurylochus. He had a feeling that something was wrong, so he stayed outside.

Circe gave the men a sweet drink of honey and barley. But she secretly mixed magic herbs into it. As soon as they drank, she touched each man with her wand. At once, they had the heads, the voices and the bodies of pigs. Their minds, however, were still human, and they cried as she shut them in a pen.

Eurylochus waited and waited, but nobody came out. At last, he ran back to the ship and told Odysseus the story. "I can't leave my friends there," said Odysseus. Eurylochus begged him not to go, but Odysseus went alone.

On the forest path, he met a young man with a golden wand. It was the god Hermes, who wanted to help him. "Take this plant with you," said Hermes. "It has a black root and a flower as white as milk. Her drink and her wand will have no power over you. When she touches you, pull out your sword. Then make her promise that she will never harm you."

Circe welcomed Odysseus just like the others and gave him the magic drink. He drank it, and then she touched him with her wand. "Now go and lie down with your friends in the pen!" she said. But nothing happened. Odysseus pulled out his sword, just as Hermes said, and Circe fell at his knees. "No man ever drank my drink and stayed the same," she said. "You must be Odysseus. Hermes told me about you long ago." Odysseus made her promise to do him no harm, and she promised.

"Then free my men," said Odysseus. Circe walked out to the pen and rubbed magic oil on each pig. One by one, they stood up as men again. They looked younger, taller and more handsome than before. When they saw Odysseus, they ran to him and held his hands. Everyone cried with joy, and even Circe felt sorry for them.

Odysseus brought the rest of his men from the ship. At first Eurylochus did not want to come, but in the end he followed. They stayed in Circe's house for a whole year, eating well and resting. When the year was over, the men came to Odysseus. "Remember Ithaca," they said. "It is time to go home."

Circe did not try to keep them, but she told Odysseus about the dangers ahead. "First you will reach the Sirens," she said. "They sit in a green meadow and sing to every passing ship. Sailors forget their families and their homes when they hear that song. Put soft wax in your men's ears. You may listen yourself, but your men must tie you to the mast first. When you beg them to untie you, they must tie you tighter."

Near the island of the Sirens, the wind suddenly died. The sea became flat and still, so the men started to row. Odysseus warmed some wax in his hands and pressed it into his men's ears. Then they tied him tightly to the mast with ropes.

Soon a beautiful song came across the water. "Come closer, famous Odysseus, pride of the Greeks!" sang the Sirens. "Stop your ship and listen to our voices. We know everything about the war at Troy, and everything in the world." Odysseus wanted to hear more with all his heart. He frowned and nodded at his men, ordering them to untie him. But Eurylochus and another man stood up and tied him even tighter. The others kept rowing until the song was far behind them. Then the men took the wax out of their ears and set their king free. The ship sailed on across the quiet sea.

## Level B2

When the long war at Troy ended, Odysseus, king of Ithaca, set out for home with his men. Storms and strange lands delayed him again and again. By the time he reached the island of Aeaea, only one ship remained of his whole fleet. The exhausted crew lay on the shore for two days and two nights, too tired and sad to speak.

On the third morning, Odysseus climbed to a high point and saw smoke rising from a thick forest. He divided the crew into two groups, one led by himself and the other by Eurylochus. They drew lots to decide which group would explore, and the lot fell to Eurylochus. He set off with twenty-two men, and many of them were weeping as they left.

Deep in the woods, they came upon a handsome house of polished stone. Wolves and mountain lions were wandering around it. Instead of attacking, however, the animals gently rubbed against the men, the way dogs greet their master. Through the open doors, the sailors could hear a woman singing in a lovely voice as she worked at her weaving. She was Circe, a sorceress with beautiful long hair, and she invited them in with a gracious smile.

Everyone accepted except Eurylochus, who suspected a trap and stayed hidden outside. Circe gave her guests a drink mixed with cheese, barley and golden honey. Secretly, though, she had stirred a powerful herb into it, one that made men forget their homes. As soon as the men had emptied their cups, she touched each of them with her wand. In a moment, they had the heads, the voices and the shape of pigs, although their minds remained exactly as before. Crying in their own strange way, they were driven into her pigsties, and she threw them acorns to eat.

Eurylochus waited for hours, but no one came out. Finally, he ran back to the ship, so shocked that at first he could not say a word. When he managed to speak, he told Odysseus how the whole group had gone inside and vanished. Eurylochus begged him to sail away while they still could, but Odysseus refused to abandon his friends.

As he walked through the quiet valley, he met a young man with a golden wand. It was Hermes, the messenger of the gods, who had come to help him. "Your friends are locked up in Circe's sties," said Hermes. "If you go in without help, you will end up there with them. Take this plant into her house. It has a black root and a flower as white as milk, and her drink will have no power over you. When she touches you with her wand, draw your sword and step toward her boldly. Then make her swear a solemn oath by the gods that she will never harm you." Hermes handed him the plant and disappeared among the trees.

Circe welcomed Odysseus exactly as she had welcomed his men and mixed the magic drink in a golden cup. He emptied it calmly, and then she touched him with her wand. "Off you go to the sty, and lie down with your friends!" she cried. Nothing happened. Odysseus drew his sword and stepped toward her, just as Hermes had told him, and Circe dropped to her knees. "No man has ever tasted my drink and stayed unchanged," she said in amazement. "You must be Odysseus. Hermes told me long ago that you would come here on your way home from Troy." Before trusting her, Odysseus made her swear the great oath of the gods, and she swore it.

Afterward, her maids prepared a splendid meal, but Odysseus sat at the table without touching any food. "Why do you sit there in silence?" asked Circe. "How can any honest man eat," he answered, "while his friends are still locked in a sty? If you truly mean me well, set them free." Circe went straight out and rubbed a new magic oil on each animal. The men stood up as themselves again, but younger, taller and more handsome than before. They recognized Odysseus at once and held his hands, weeping so loudly that the whole house was filled with the sound. Even the sorceress was moved by their joy.

Then Circe told Odysseus to bring the rest of his crew from the shore. Eurylochus warned the others that the sorceress would turn them all into pigs, wolves or lions. In the end, however, he followed, because he did not want to stay behind alone. Circe kept her promise. She gave the travelers baths, clean clothes and endless feasts, and they rested in her house until a whole year had passed.

At last, the men came to Odysseus. "Have you forgotten Ithaca?" they asked. "It is time to think of home." Circe did not try to stop him, but before they set out, she warned him about the Sirens. "Anyone who hears their voices never returns to his family," she said. "They sit in a flowery meadow and sing so sweetly that sailors forget everything else. Soften some wax and put it in your men's ears, so that they hear nothing. If you want to hear the song yourself, have them tie you to the mast. If you beg them to set you free, they must only tie you tighter."

As they drew near the island of the Sirens, the breeze suddenly died. The sea became perfectly calm, as if some god had put the waves to sleep. The crew lowered the sail and took up the oars. Odysseus softened a large cake of wax in his strong hands and pressed it into every man's ears. Then they bound him upright to the mast with strong ropes.

They were only a shout away from the shore when the Sirens noticed the ship and began their song. "Come here, famous Odysseus, glory of the Greeks!" they sang. "Stop your ship and listen to us. No sailor passes this place without hearing our sweet voices, and he leaves us wiser than before. We know everything that happened at Troy, and everything that happens on earth." The song was so beautiful that Odysseus longed to hear more. He frowned and nodded at his men, ordering them to set him free. But they only rowed harder, while Eurylochus and another sailor rose and tied him even more tightly. Only when the voices had faded completely did the men take the wax from their ears and free their captain. The ship sailed on across the calm sea.

## Level C1

The war at Troy had been over for some time, yet Odysseus, king of rocky Ithaca, was no nearer home than on the day he had set sail. When at last he steered into a quiet harbor on the island of Aeaea, a single vessel was all that remained of his fleet. The men hauled it onto the sand and lay there for two days and two nights, worn out in body and sick at heart.

On the third morning, Odysseus climbed a rocky lookout above the shore. From there he saw a thread of smoke rising through the thick oaks in the middle of the wooded island. Returning to the ship, Odysseus divided his men into two companies, keeping one for himself and giving the other to Eurylochus, his kinsman. They shook lots in a bronze helmet, and the lot of Eurylochus jumped out first. He set off with twenty-two companions, several of them in tears.

In a sheltered valley they found the house of Circe, built of polished stone. All around it roamed wolves and mountain lions, yet these beasts, far from springing at the strangers, came up wagging their long tails like dogs greeting their master. The sailors were no less frightened for that. From inside drifted the voice of a woman singing as she moved back and forth before a great loom, weaving a cloth so fine that only a goddess could have made it. At a call from the sailors, the singing stopped, the shining doors swung open, and Circe herself, a sorceress with beautiful braided hair, invited them in.

They all followed her, trusting and foolish, except Eurylochus, who sensed a trick and stayed outside where he could watch. Circe gave her guests seats of honor and served them a rich mixture of cheese, barley meal and yellow honey. Into it, however, she had stirred a magic herb that would make them forget their native land. Then she passed among them with her wand, touching each one in turn, and in that instant they had the heads, the grunting voices, the bristles and the round bodies of pigs. Only their minds were unchanged, so they knew exactly what had happened to them. Crying in their own strange way, they were shut up in the sties, where Circe tossed them acorns, the plain food of pigs.

Eurylochus waited until he could wait no longer, then ran back through the forest to the ship. Only after much questioning did he manage to tell the story of the house, the singing woman, and the companions who had gone inside and never come out. Odysseus took up his bow and ordered Eurylochus to lead the way, but Eurylochus took hold of his knees and begged to be left behind. "Don't take me back there," he pleaded. "You will never return, and you will never bring back any of the others. Let us sail away while we still can." "Then stay here by the ship and rest," replied Odysseus. "I am going, because I have no choice."

He had almost reached the house when a young man with the first soft beard of youth stepped into his path, a golden wand in his hand. It was Hermes, the messenger of the gods, and he took Odysseus kindly by the hand. "Where are you going, poor man, alone over these hills in a country you do not know?" he asked. "Your friends are penned up in the sties of Circe, and if you walk in there as you are, you will soon be grunting beside them. Still, I can save you." He bent down and pulled a plant out of the earth, something no mortal could easily do, and showed Odysseus its black root and its flower as white as milk. With this herb, he explained, the drink of Circe would have no effect; when she lifted her wand, Odysseus must draw his sword and step boldly toward her. Before he agreed to anything, though, he should make her swear the great oath of the gods that she would plan no further harm against him.

Circe welcomed him as warmly as she had welcomed the others, seating him on a handsome chair decorated with silver. She mixed the drink in a golden cup, stirring in her herb with a smile, and he emptied it. "Now off to the sty with you," she said, touching him lightly with her wand, "and lie down with your friends." To her astonishment, nothing at all happened. Odysseus drew his sword and stepped toward her, as Hermes had told him to do, and Circe cried out and sank to her knees before him. "Who are you, and where do you come from?" she asked. "No man has ever tasted this drink and stayed himself, so yours must be a mind that magic cannot touch. You must be Odysseus, the man of many turns, for Hermes of the golden wand always told me you would come this way, sailing home from Troy." Odysseus would not trust her until she had sworn the great oath, and she swore it at once.

Her maids, daughters of the springs and rivers, prepared a bath and a splendid meal. Yet when the food was set before him, Odysseus sat without touching it, his thoughts elsewhere. "Why do you sit there like a man who cannot speak?" Circe asked. "What honest man could bear to eat," replied Odysseus, "while his companions are shut in a sty? If you really wish me to enjoy your table, set them free first, so that I can see them with my own eyes."

Circe went straight out and opened the doors of the sty. She went down the line, rubbing each with a different ointment, and the bristles that her first drink had given them fell away. In a moment they were men again, younger than before, taller and far more handsome. They knew Odysseus at once and seized his hands, and such a sweet longing to weep came over them all that the whole house echoed with the sound. Even the sorceress, watching them, was touched with pity.

At her suggestion, Odysseus went down to the shore to bring the rest of the crew. When the men by the ship saw him alive, they crowded around him like calves around their mothers coming back from the meadow. Only Eurylochus held back, warning them that Circe would turn them all into pigs or wolves or lions to guard her house forever. In the end, though, he came along with the others, unwilling to be left behind alone. Day after day they feasted in the halls of Circe, until a whole year had gone round.

At last his companions remembered home. "Strange man," they said to Odysseus, "isn't it time you thought of your own country?" That night he asked Circe to keep her word and send him on his way, and she did not try to hold him against his will. She did, however, warn him about what lay ahead, beginning with the Sirens. "Whoever sails too close to them and hears their voices never sees his wife and little children again," she told him. "They sit in a green meadow and enchant every passing sailor with their high, clear song. Soften some honey-sweet wax and stop the ears of your companions so that none of them can hear. If you yourself wish to listen, let them bind you hand and foot, upright against the mast. Should you beg them to release you, let them tie you all the tighter."

As they approached the island of the Sirens, the wind dropped as suddenly as if someone had shut a door, and a breathless calm lay over the sea. The sailors rolled up the sail and bent to their oars until the water foamed white around the blades. Odysseus cut a great wheel of wax into pieces and worked them in his strong hands until they were soft. He pressed the wax into the ears of every man, one after another, and they in turn bound him to the mast with stout ropes.

They were no further from the shore than a man's voice can carry when the Sirens saw the swift ship coming and lifted their voices. "Come this way, famous Odysseus, great glory of the Greeks," they sang, "and stop your ship to hear us. No one has ever rowed past this place without listening to the honey-sweet song from our lips, and every sailor goes away delighted and wiser than before. We know every hardship of the long war at Troy, and we know everything that happens on the wide earth." Hearing them, Odysseus wanted nothing in the world so much as to listen longer, and with frowns and nods he ordered his crew to set him free. But they leaned into their oars and rowed harder, while Eurylochus and one other sailor sprang up and bound him with even more rope. Not until the song had faded into the sound of the waves did his loyal companions take the soft wax from their ears and loosen the ropes that held him. The ship moved on over the quiet sea.
