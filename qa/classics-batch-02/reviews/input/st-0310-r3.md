# Micromegas

Summary: A giant from the star Sirius and his friend from Saturn visit Earth and meet its tiny thinkers.

## Level A1

Micromegas lived near the star Sirius. He was a young giant. He was twenty-four miles tall! His mind was big, too. He wrote a book about very small animals. The leaders of his land hated it. "Go away for eight hundred years," they said.

So Micromegas left his home. He flew from star to star. At last, he came to Saturn. The people there were one mile tall. To Micromegas, they looked very small.

Micromegas met a wise man from Saturn. They talked for many days. "We have seventy-two senses," said the Saturnian. "We have nearly one thousand," said Micromegas. Soon the two giants were good friends.

The friends traveled together. They jumped onto the ring of Saturn. They stayed on Jupiter for a year. Mars was too small for them. Then they came down on Earth.

Earth looked tiny to them. They walked all around it quickly. The sea came only to the Saturnian's knees. "Nobody lives here," said the Saturnian.

Then the necklace of Micromegas broke. Its diamonds were big and very clear. The friends used them as microscopes. Through one diamond, they saw a whale. Soon they saw a little ship. Micromegas put the ship on his thumb.

Little men stood on the ship. They were wise men, and they could talk! Micromegas made a tube from his fingernail. Now the giants could hear the little men. One little man measured the Saturnian. "You are one mile tall," he said. He was right! "Amazing!" said the Saturnian.

Next the giants asked about the soul. Each little man gave a different answer. Then a tiny man in a hat spoke. "Everything in the world is for us!" The two giants laughed and laughed.

Micromegas smiled at the little men. "Here is a book for you. It shows the meaning of everything." The wise men took the book home. An old man opened it. Every page was white and empty. "I knew it," said the old man.

## Level A2

A young giant lived on a planet near the bright star Sirius. His name was Micromegas, and he was twenty-four miles tall. He was also very clever. When he was young, he studied very tiny insects. Then he wrote a book about them. An important old official in his land did not like the book. The judges argued about it for two hundred and twenty years. In the end, they sent Micromegas away for eight hundred years.

He wanted to see other worlds and learn more. He traveled from planet to planet on sunbeams and comets. After a long trip, he came to Saturn. The people there were only about a mile tall. At first, he laughed at their small size, but soon he stopped.

On Saturn, Micromegas met a clever man from the planet's Academy. They talked for many days and became good friends. "How many senses do your people have?" Micromegas asked. "Only seventy-two, and we always want more," said the Saturnian. "We have almost a thousand, and we want more too," said Micromegas. People on Saturn lived for fifteen thousand years. The two friends decided to travel together. They jumped onto the ring of Saturn and then onto its moons. They stayed on Jupiter for a whole year. Next they passed Mars, but it was too small for them. At last, they came down on Earth, near the Baltic Sea.

Earth looked very small to the travelers. They walked all the way around it in thirty-six hours. The deepest sea came only up to the Saturnian's knees. "Nobody lives on this ugly little planet," said the Saturnian. "Maybe the people here are just very small," answered Micromegas.

Suddenly the diamond necklace of Micromegas broke. The diamonds were huge and clear, so they made good microscopes. The Saturnian looked through one and saw a tiny whale. Then Micromegas saw a ship on the Baltic Sea. He picked it up and put it on his thumbnail. The people on the ship were very afraid.

The ship carried a group of scientists home from the far north. Micromegas made a speaking tube from a piece of his fingernail. Now the giants and the tiny men could talk. One little man took out his tools and measured the Saturnian. "You are about one mile tall," said the little man. He was right, and the Saturnian was amazed.

"You are so small, but you think so well! You must be very happy," said Micromegas. One scientist shook his head. "No, we do many foolish things. Our kings send big armies to fight over a heap of mud. It is smaller than your heel." Micromegas felt sorry for the people of Earth.

Then the giants asked about the soul. Each scientist gave a different answer, and nobody agreed. At last, a little man in a square cap spoke. "The stars, the planets, and even you giants are here for us!" The two giants laughed and laughed. The ship fell into the Saturnian's pocket!

Before he left, Micromegas gave the scientists a present. "Here is a book about the meaning of everything." The scientists took the book to a big city. There, an old scholar opened it. All the pages were empty. "Ah, I knew it," said the old scholar.

## Level B1

On a planet that circles the bright star Sirius lived a young giant. His name was Micromegas, and he was twenty-four miles tall. He had a brilliant mind and wanted to understand everything. At four hundred and fifty, which is young on Sirius, he studied tiny insects. Then he wrote a book about them.

An important official in his country read the book and decided that it was dangerous. In truth, the official knew very little about science. The judges argued about the book for two hundred and twenty years. They never read it, but in the end they banned it anyway. Micromegas had to leave the court for eight hundred years. He was not unhappy, however, because now he was free to travel.

Micromegas understood how the stars move. He used this knowledge to fly from one world to another, riding on sunbeams and comets. After a long journey, he arrived on Saturn. The people there were only about a mile tall, so they seemed like dwarfs to him. At first, he laughed at their small size, but he soon stopped. He was a wise giant, and size did not matter to him.

On Saturn, Micromegas met the secretary of the planet's Academy. He was a clever man, and the two of them soon became friends. "How many senses do the people of your planet have?" Micromegas asked. "We have seventy-two, but we always feel that we need more," said the Saturnian. "We have nearly a thousand, and we feel the same," Micromegas replied. They discovered that people on Saturn lived for fifteen thousand years. On Sirius, life lasted seven hundred times longer. Even so, both friends agreed that life was far too short.

The two thinkers decided to travel together. First they jumped onto Saturn's ring, and then they hopped from moon to moon. They stayed on Jupiter for a whole year and learned many secrets there. Next they passed Mars, but it was so small that they did not stop. At last, they came down on Earth, on the northern shore of the Baltic Sea. It was the fifth of July, seventeen thirty-seven.

The travelers decided to walk around this little planet. It took them only thirty-six hours. The deepest seas came up to the Saturnian's knees, but they hardly reached Micromegas's heel. "This planet has a very bad shape, and nobody lives on it," said the Saturnian. "Perhaps the people here are just too small for us to see," answered Micromegas.

At that moment, the string of Micromegas's diamond necklace broke. The diamonds were huge and perfectly clear, so the two friends used them as microscopes. Through one of them, the Saturnian saw something moving in the sea. It was a whale, and he laughed because it was so tiny. Soon Micromegas spotted something even smaller: a ship that was sailing on the Baltic. He lifted it gently and placed it on his thumbnail.

The ship was carrying a group of scientists. They were returning from the far north, where they measured the shape of the Earth. When the ship rose into the air, everyone on board thought it was a terrible storm. Micromegas cut a piece from his fingernail and made a long speaking tube from it. With this tube, he could hear their tiny voices, and they could hear his.

Micromegas was amazed that such small creatures could think and speak. Then one of the scientists took out his tools and measured the Saturnian. "You are about a mile tall," the little scientist said. The answer was correct, and the Saturnian could hardly believe it. "Now I see that we must never judge anything by its size," said Micromegas.

"With such great minds, you must be very happy," Micromegas went on. One of the scientists sadly shook his head. "No, we do many foolish things. At this moment, two great armies are fighting over a heap of mud. It is no bigger than your heel. The kings never even go there themselves." Micromegas was angry with those foolish kings, and he felt sorry for their people.

Next the giants asked the scientists about the soul. Each one gave a different answer, and nobody agreed. Only one man honestly said that he did not know, and Micromegas liked him best. Then a tiny scholar in a square cap spoke loudly. "The stars, the planets, and even you giants exist only for us," he declared. The two giants laughed so hard that the ship fell into the Saturnian's pocket.

Before leaving, Micromegas promised to write them a book of wisdom. "It will show you the meaning of all things," he said kindly. Then he gave them the book. The scientists carried the book to the great Academy of Sciences in a big city. There, the old secretary opened it with excitement. Every page was completely blank. "Just as I expected," said the old secretary.

## Level B2

Somewhere near the great star Sirius there is a planet, and on that planet lived a young giant named Micromegas. His name suits him well, because it means "small and great" at the same time. He stood twenty-four miles tall, which was perfectly ordinary in his part of the universe. His mind, however, was far from ordinary. At four hundred and fifty, still a boy on Sirius, he studied insects too small for normal eyes. Then he wrote a book about them.

An important official of his country, a man who knew almost nothing, read the book and declared it dangerous. The judges argued about it for two hundred and twenty years. Not one of them actually read it, yet in the end they condemned it all the same. Micromegas was ordered to stay away from the court for eight hundred years. He soon cheered up, since exile gave him the chance to travel and improve both his mind and his heart.

Because he understood the forces holding the stars in their places, he could leap from one world to another. Sometimes he rode a sunbeam, and sometimes he caught a passing comet. After a long journey, he reached Saturn, whose people were only about a mile tall. At first the giant smiled at these dwarfs, as a tall man might smile at a child. Then he reminded himself that a thinking being should never be mocked for its size.

On Saturn he made friends with the secretary of the planet's Academy, a clever and well-read man. "Tell me, how many senses do your people have?" Micromegas asked. "Only seventy-two," the Saturnian replied, "and we complain about it every day." "On my planet we have nearly a thousand, and we complain just as much," said Micromegas. They went on to compare their lives. A Saturnian lived about fifteen thousand years, while a native of Sirius lived seven hundred times longer. Even so, both agreed that life was over almost before it had begun.

Having become close friends, the two philosophers decided to travel together. They jumped onto Saturn's ring, which they found quite comfortable, and then visited its moons one by one. On Jupiter they stayed for a whole year and learned a great many secrets. Mars, on the other hand, seemed so tiny that they feared there was no room to sleep. Finally, on the fifth of July, seventeen thirty-seven, they landed on the northern shore of the Baltic Sea.

Curious about this little ball, they decided to walk around it, and the whole trip took them thirty-six hours. The Saturnian, whose legs were much shorter, had to hurry and was soon out of breath. The deepest oceans came up only to his knees, while for Micromegas they were no more than a puddle. "This planet is so badly made that nothing could live on it," the Saturnian announced. "Perhaps its people are simply too small for us to notice," Micromegas answered.

Just then, the necklace of huge diamonds that Micromegas wore around his neck broke. The stones were so large and so clear that the two friends realized they could use them as microscopes. Looking through one of them, the Saturnian saw something moving in the sea. He lifted it out on his fingertip and laughed, for it was a whale no bigger than a speck. A moment later, Micromegas noticed something even tinier: a ship sailing on the Baltic. He picked it up very carefully and set it on his thumbnail.

On board were scientists returning from the far north, where they had gone to measure the shape of the Earth. When their ship suddenly rose into the sky, the passengers were sure that a terrible storm had caught them. Micromegas could see them moving, but at first he could not hear a word. So he trimmed a piece from his fingernail and rolled it into a speaking tube. Through this tube, the giants could at last hear the tiny voices, and the scientists could hear the giants.

The giant could hardly believe that such small creatures were able to think. To prove it, one of the scientists pointed his instruments at the Saturnian and announced, "You are about one mile tall." The answer was exactly right, and the Saturnian was astonished. "I see now that nothing should ever be judged by its size," said Micromegas. "You have so little body and so much mind that you must be the happiest creatures alive."

One of the scientists sadly corrected him. "We are often foolish and unkind. At this very moment, two huge armies are fighting over a heap of mud no bigger than your heel. The kings who sent them will never even see that heap of mud." Micromegas was so angry with those kings that he wanted to stop the whole war himself. In the end, he simply felt sorry for the people who had to fight for them.

Wanting to change the subject, the giants asked their little friends about the soul. Every scientist gave a different answer, and each quoted a different famous teacher. Only one admitted honestly that he did not know how he thought, and Micromegas found him the wisest of them all. Then a tiny scholar in a square cap interrupted everyone. He announced that he knew the whole secret, which could be found in one great book. According to that book, the stars, the planets, and even the two giants existed only for the sake of humans. At this, the travelers burst into such laughter that the ship slid off the giant's thumbnail and fell into the Saturnian's pocket. They had to search for quite a while before they found it again.

Before leaving Earth, Micromegas promised to write the little people a book of philosophy. "In it, you will find the meaning of all things," he told them kindly. He handed them the volume, and the scientists carried it to the Academy of Sciences in a great city. There the old secretary opened it eagerly, expecting to discover the answer to everything. Every single page was blank. "Ah," he said, "just as I suspected."

## Level C1

Orbiting the star Sirius is a planet, and on that planet there once lived a young giant called Micromegas. It is a fitting name for a creature both very small and very great, depending on what one compares him with. He measured twenty-four miles from head to foot, a height considered entirely unremarkable among his own people, whose sense of proportion was naturally different from ours. What set him apart was not his body but his mind. Before he was two hundred and fifty, he had worked out more of the laws of geometry by himself than most scholars learn in a lifetime.

At about four hundred and fifty, which on Sirius counts as the end of childhood, he turned his attention to insects invisible to any ordinary eye. He then wrote a book describing them. The book soon came to the notice of a powerful official, a man of great importance and very little learning, who declared that it contained dangerous ideas. What followed was a trial lasting two hundred and twenty years. The judges argued fiercely throughout, and when at last they condemned the book, it turned out that none of them had troubled to read it. Micromegas was banished from the court for eight hundred years, a sentence he bore with remarkable good humor.

Indeed, exile suited him perfectly, for it freed him to travel from planet to planet and, as he put it, to improve his mind and his heart. Having studied the forces holding the stars in their courses, he knew exactly how to use them, now riding a sunbeam, now hitching a lift on a passing comet. In this way he eventually reached Saturn, whose inhabitants stood a mere mile high. His first impulse was to smile at such dwarfs, yet he checked himself at once, reflecting that a thinking creature need not be enormous to be worthy of respect.

It was on Saturn that he befriended the secretary of the planet's Academy, a learned and agreeable man with whom he could talk for days on end. "How many senses do your people possess?" the giant asked him one evening. "Seventy-two," replied the Saturnian, "and not a day passes without our wishing for more." "We have nearly a thousand," Micromegas admitted, "and we feel exactly the same restlessness." Their lives, they discovered, differed just as greatly: a Saturnian might live fifteen thousand years, whereas a native of Sirius lasted seven hundred times as long. Yet neither of them could shake off the sense that existence was over almost before it had properly begun. On this point, at least, the two philosophers found themselves in complete agreement.

Determined to see more of the universe together, the friends set out at once. They leapt first onto Saturn's ring, which proved surprisingly comfortable, and from there they hopped from one of its moons to the next. On Jupiter they lingered for a whole year, learning a great many secrets that, sadly, have never reached us. Mars struck them as so small that they doubted it could offer room enough for a night's sleep. They passed it by, noticing as they went two little moons that our own astronomers had not yet seen. At last, on the fifth of July, seventeen thirty-seven, they came down on the northern shore of the Baltic Sea.

Curious to know what kind of world they had found, they decided to walk around it, and the whole circuit took them thirty-six hours. The Saturnian, whose legs were far shorter, had to scurry along beside his companion and was soon gasping for breath. For him the deepest ocean barely rose above the knee, while for Micromegas it was hardly more than a puddle that dampened his heel. "This planet is so badly shaped, so lumpy and so ridiculous, that no sensible being could possibly live on it," the Saturnian concluded. "Perhaps," Micromegas replied, "its people are simply too small for creatures like us to notice."

As he spoke, the string of his diamond necklace snapped, scattering stones so large and so flawless that the two friends realized at once they would make excellent microscopes. Peering through one of them, the Saturnian caught sight of something stirring in the water and lifted it out on his fingertip. He burst out laughing, for it was a whale, smaller than a crumb. Not long afterwards, Micromegas spotted something smaller still: a ship making its slow way across the Baltic. With the greatest delicacy, he picked it up and settled it on his thumbnail.

Aboard was a company of scientists on their way home from the far north, where they had traveled to measure the precise shape of the Earth. Feeling their vessel rise into the sky, the passengers were convinced that a fearful storm had seized them, and confusion broke out on deck. Micromegas could see the tiny figures running about, but their voices were far too faint for him to hear. He therefore trimmed a sliver from his fingernail and rolled it into a speaking tube, through which the giants and the scientists could, for the first time, understand one another.

The giant could scarcely believe that such minute specks were capable of thought. Then one of them calmly aimed his instruments at the Saturnian and announced that the Saturnian was about a mile tall. It was exactly right, and the Saturnian was dumbfounded. "I see more clearly than ever that nothing should be judged by its apparent size," said Micromegas, deeply moved. "Since you have so little matter and so much mind, you must surely be the happiest beings in creation."

One of the scientists, however, gently set him straight. "We are capable of a great deal of folly. At this very moment, two vast armies are fighting over a heap of mud no larger than your heel, and not one soldier among them cares in the least who owns it. The question is merely which of two kings it belongs to, and neither king will ever set foot on it." Micromegas was so indignant that he longed to step in and put an end to the whole affair. In the end, however, his anger gave way to pity for the ordinary people sent to fight.

Hoping to find a happier subject, the giants asked the little philosophers what they thought the soul might be. Each answered differently, and each supported his answer by quoting a different famous teacher, sometimes in a language he could not actually understand. Only one confessed, with refreshing honesty, that he had no idea how he came to think at all, and Micromegas judged him the wisest of the whole company. It was at this point that a tiny scholar in a square cap pushed forward to declare that he alone possessed the full secret, set down long ago in one great book. According to that book, he explained, the stars, the planets, the suns, and indeed the two giants themselves had all been created solely for the benefit of humankind. The travelers fell into such helpless laughter that the ship slipped from the giant's shaking thumbnail and tumbled into the Saturnian's pocket. It took them a long and anxious search to recover it.

Before taking his leave, Micromegas promised the little people a book of philosophy, written specially for them, in which they would find at last the meaning of all things. He handed them the volume with great kindness, and the scientists carried it home to the Academy of Sciences in a great city. There the aged secretary opened it with trembling eagerness, ready to read the answer to every question ever asked. He turned one page, then another, and then all the rest; every single one was perfectly blank. "Ah," said the old man, closing the cover, "I suspected as much."
