# Dummling and the Golden Goose

Summary: A kind young man shares his food with a stranger and finds a goose with golden feathers.

## Level A1

A man had three sons. They called the youngest son Dummling. Everyone laughed at him. "He is a fool," said his brothers.

The oldest son went to cut wood. His mother gave him a sweet cake. In the forest sat a little gray man. "I am hungry. Please give me some cake," said the man. "No, it is my cake!" said the son. Soon his axe hit his arm. He went home. The second son did not share either. He hurt his leg and went home too.

Then Dummling went to the forest. He had only a dry cake. The little gray man came to him. "Let us share my cake," said Dummling. Now the cake was sweet and good! "Cut down that tree," said the gray man.

Dummling cut down the tree. A goose sat in its roots. All its feathers were gold! He took the goose to an inn.

The man at the inn had three daughters. Each girl wanted a gold feather. The oldest girl touched the goose. Her hand stuck to it! Her sister touched her and stuck too. The third girl stuck too.

In the morning, the girls ran behind Dummling. A priest saw them. "Stop, girls!" cried the priest. He took the youngest girl's hand. Now the priest stuck too! Two farmers tried to help him. They stuck as well.

Dummling came to a big city. The king's daughter never laughed. The king made a promise. "Make her laugh and marry her!" The princess saw the funny line. She laughed and laughed!

But the king did not like Dummling. He gave him three hard tasks. First, someone must drink a whole cellar. Dummling found a thirsty man in the forest. The thirsty man drank it all. Next, someone must eat a mountain of bread. A hungry man ate it all. Last, the king wanted a special ship. It had to sail on land and water.

The little gray man brought the ship. "You were kind," said the gray man. Now the king could not say no. Dummling married the princess. Later, he became king himself.

"Father, let me go and cut wood," said Dummling. "Your brothers came home hurt, so stay here," said his father. But Dummling asked again and again. At last his father let him go. In the morning Dummling put the goose under his arm. The three girls had to run behind him. On the road a priest saw them. "Shame on you, girls!" cried the priest. He took the youngest girl's hand, and now he stuck too. A man from the church caught the priest's coat and stuck too. Then two farmers came and stuck too. Now seven people ran in a long line behind Dummling.

That evening Dummling came to a city. The king's daughter there never laughed. The king made a promise. "Any man can marry her, but he must make her laugh." The princess saw the funny line from her window. She laughed and laughed and could not stop.

"Last of all, bring me a ship. It must sail on land and on water," said the king.

Dummling went to the forest once more. The little gray man sat there. "You were kind to me. I drank for you and ate for you. Now this ship is for you," said the little man. Dummling sailed it over the land to the palace. This time the king could not say no. Dummling married the princess, and later he became king.

## Level B1

There was once a man who had three sons. Everyone called the youngest son Dummling and treated him as a fool. His family laughed at him and always gave him the smallest share of everything.

One day the oldest son decided to go into the forest to cut wood. His mother packed a fine sweet cake and a bottle of drink for him. In the forest he met a little gray man, who wished him good morning. "I am so hungry and thirsty. Will you give me some of your cake and drink?" asked the little man. "No, go away, because then there will be less for me," said the oldest son. He walked off and began to cut a tree. Before long his axe slipped and hurt his arm. He had to go home. The second son went into the forest the next day with the same kind of food. He also refused the little gray man, and soon he hurt his leg.

Then Dummling said, "Father, let me go and cut wood." "Your brothers came back hurt, and you understand nothing about it," said his father. Dummling kept asking, and at last his father gave up. His mother gave him a plain cake from the ashes and a bottle of sour drink. The little gray man appeared again and asked for something to eat. "I only have a dry cake and sour drink, but please share them," said Dummling. When they sat down, the dry cake had become rich and sweet. The sour drink tasted fresh and good too. After the meal the little man said, "You have a good heart. Cut down that old tree over there. You will find something at its roots."

Dummling cut down the tree and found a goose with feathers of pure gold. He lifted it up and carried it to an inn, where he stayed the night.

The innkeeper had three daughters, and all of them wanted one of the golden feathers. While Dummling was out of the room, the oldest daughter grabbed the goose by the wing. Her hand stuck fast, and she could not pull it away. The second daughter came and touched her sister, and she was stuck too. Then the third daughter touched the second one, and now she could not move away either.

In the morning Dummling picked up his goose and set off. He did not worry about the girls behind him. They had to run after him wherever he went. In a field they met a priest. He was shocked to see three girls chasing a young man. "Shame on you!" cried the priest. He took the youngest girl by the hand, but he stuck to her as well. The man who rang the church bell saw the priest and caught his sleeve. He stuck too. Two farmers were coming home from work, and the priest called to them for help. As soon as they touched the bell-ringer, they joined the line. Now seven people were running behind Dummling and his goose.

Dummling soon came to a city where the king had a daughter who never laughed. The king had promised that the first man who made her laugh could marry her. The princess saw the seven people running behind the goose. She began to laugh, and she could not stop. Dummling asked for her hand, but the king did not like him. "First, find a man who can empty my cellar," said the king. Dummling thought of the little gray man and went back to the place in the forest. A man with a sad face was sitting there. He told Dummling that he was always terribly thirsty. Dummling led him to the king's cellar. The man emptied every barrel before the day was over.

The king was still not happy. Now he wanted a man who could eat a mountain of bread. In the same place Dummling found a hungry man. This man ate the whole mountain in a single day. Then the king made a last demand. "Bring me a ship that can sail on land and on water," said the king. Dummling went into the forest once more, and the little gray man was waiting for him. "I drank for you and I ate for you. Now the ship is yours, because you were kind to me," said the little man. When the king saw the ship coming over the land, he could not refuse any longer. The wedding took place. Later Dummling became king and lived happily with his wife.

## Level B2

There was once a man with three sons, and the youngest of them was called Dummling. Everyone in the family looked down on him, laughed at his mistakes, and left him out whenever they could.

One morning the eldest son set out to cut wood in the forest. Before he left, his mother gave him a fine, sweet cake and a bottle of something to drink. As he entered the forest, a little old gray man stepped onto the path and wished him good day. "Give me a piece of the cake from your pocket and a sip from your bottle. I am so hungry and thirsty," said the little man. The clever son only shrugged. "If I share with you, there will be less for me," he replied, and he walked on. He had not been chopping for long when the axe slipped and struck his arm, and he had to go home. The little gray man had made sure of that.

The next day the second son went out, and his mother packed the same kind of food for him. The little gray man was waiting, and he asked for a piece of cake and a drink. The second son was just as unkind as his brother. Before long he hurt his leg and had to be carried home.

Then Dummling spoke up. "Father, let me go out and cut wood." "Your brothers have hurt themselves doing it, and you understand nothing about such work," his father replied. But Dummling begged for so long that his father finally said, "Go, then. You will learn when you get hurt." His mother gave him nothing better than a flat cake baked in the ashes and a bottle of sour drink.

When he reached the forest, the little gray man greeted him and asked for some food. "All I have is a cake from the ashes and a sour drink. But if that is good enough for you, let us sit down and eat together," said Dummling. When they sat down and Dummling took out his food, the cake had become rich and sweet. The drink tasted fresh as well, and they ate and drank together. Afterwards the little man said, "Because you have a good heart, I will give you some luck. Over there stands an old tree. Cut it down, and you will find something among its roots." With that, the little man went on his way.

Dummling cut down the tree, and among the roots sat a goose whose feathers were made of pure gold. He lifted it out and took it to an inn, where he planned to spend the night. The innkeeper had three daughters, and when they saw the goose, they were curious and wanted one of its golden feathers. "I am sure I can find a moment to pull one out," thought the eldest. As soon as Dummling stepped out, she grabbed the goose by its wing, but her fingers stuck fast to it. Soon the second sister came for a feather too, and the moment she touched her sister, she was stuck as well. The third sister came last, although the others shouted at her to keep away. She did not understand why, so she touched the second sister and was caught like the others. They had to spend the whole night beside the goose.

The next morning Dummling tucked the goose under his arm and set off. He paid no attention to the three girls hanging on behind him. Wherever his legs took him, left or right, they had to run after him. In the middle of a field they met the priest, who was horrified by what he saw. "Have you no shame, you girls? Why are you chasing a young man across the fields?" cried the priest. He seized the youngest by the hand to pull her back, but the moment he touched her, he was stuck too. Now he had to run along behind. Soon the man who rang the church bell came by and saw the priest trotting after the girls. "Father, wait, you have work at the church today!" he called, and he caught the priest's sleeve. He, too, was stuck at once. As the five of them hurried along, two farmers were coming back from the fields with their tools. The priest called to them for help, but as soon as they touched the bell-ringer, they joined the line. Now there were seven people running behind Dummling and his goose.

That evening Dummling came to a city ruled by a king whose daughter was so serious that nobody could make her laugh. The king had announced that whoever managed to make her laugh would be allowed to marry her. Dummling marched with his goose and his long, stumbling line past her window. When the princess saw them, she burst out laughing and could not stop. Dummling then asked for her hand, but the king did not like the idea of such a son-in-law. Instead, he kept finding new conditions. First, he said, Dummling had to bring him a man who could drink every barrel in the royal cellar.

Dummling thought of the little gray man and went back to the place where he had cut down the tree. There sat a man with a very sad face. "I am so thirsty that nothing seems to help," the man complained. "Then come with me, and you will have plenty," said Dummling. He led the man to the king's cellar, and before evening the man had emptied every barrel.

Dummling asked for his bride again, but the king now demanded a man who could eat a whole mountain of bread. Once more Dummling went into the forest. At the same spot he found a man pulling his belt tight because he was so hungry. The man came along gladly and ate the entire mountain of bread in a single day.

For the third time Dummling asked for the princess, and the king set his last condition. "Bring me a ship that can sail on land as well as on water. If you can do that, you may marry her at once," said the king. Dummling went straight to the forest, and there sat the little gray man with whom he had shared his cake. "I drank for you and I ate for you, and now I will give you the ship. All this is because you were kind to me," said the little man.

When the king saw the ship come sailing over the land, he could no longer refuse. The wedding was held. When the old king's days were over, Dummling took the crown and lived happily with his wife for many years.

## Level C1

There was once a man who had three sons, and the youngest of them, Dummling, was the one the whole household had decided to think little of. He was mocked at the table, overlooked in every plan, and, whenever there was a slice of anything to share, quietly handed the thinnest.

It was the eldest son who first set out to cut wood in the forest. His mother, wanting him to be well fed for the work, packed him a handsome sweet cake and a bottle of something to drink. He had hardly passed the first trees when a little old gray man, no taller than a child, stepped onto the path and wished him a good day. "Spare me a piece of that cake and a mouthful from your bottle. I am very hungry, and my throat is dry," said the little man. The eldest son, who considered himself the sensible one of the family, saw no reason to be generous. "If I give you my cake and my drink, I shall have none myself," he said, and he turned his back and walked on. He chose a tree and began to chop, but before long the axe slipped and struck his arm, so that he had to go home at once. It had been no accident; the little gray man had arranged it.

The second son, sent out the following day with the same fine provisions, met the same little figure at the same bend in the path. The request was repeated politely, and it was refused just as rudely, with much the same reasoning about there being less for oneself. The punishment followed without delay: a careless stroke hurt his leg, and he had to be carried back to the house.

At this point Dummling, who had watched both brothers come home hurt, asked whether he might try his luck. "Your brothers have hurt themselves at it," his father replied, "and you, who understand nothing at all, think you can do better?" Dummling, however, was not easily put off, and he went on asking until his father, tired of the subject, gave in. "Then go, and you will learn something when you are hurt as well," his father said. His mother saw no reason to waste good baking on him, so his bag held only a flat cake baked among the ashes and a bottle of sour drink.

In the forest the little gray man appeared once again, and once again he asked for food. "All I have is a cake from the ashes and a drink that has gone sour," Dummling said, "but if you do not mind that, sit down, and we will share it." They sat down together, and when Dummling unwrapped his poor meal, he found a rich, sweet cake in its place and a bottle whose contents tasted fresh and fine. When they had finished, the little man looked at him with approval. "Because you have a good heart and share what little you own, I am going to give you some luck. There is an old tree over there; cut it down, and you will find something waiting among its roots," he said. And without another word, he went off between the trees.

Dummling did as he was told, and when the old tree crashed down, he found a goose sitting among its roots with feathers of pure gold. He lifted it out, tucked it carefully under his arm, and made his way to an inn, where he meant to spend the night. The innkeeper had three daughters, who no sooner caught sight of the goose than they began to wonder what a single golden feather might be worth. "I shall find a chance to pull one out," the eldest promised herself, and as soon as Dummling stepped out of the room, she reached for the bird's wing. Her fingers stuck to it at once, and no amount of tugging could free them. Soon the second sister crept in with the same idea, but the moment she brushed against her sister's shoulder, she found herself fastened there as well. The youngest came last, and although the other two cried out to her to stay away, she could not see why she should be the only one without a feather. She laid her hand on the second sister and was caught just like the others, so the three of them spent the night fixed to the goose.

The next morning Dummling picked up his goose and walked off, showing no interest whatever in the three girls who hung on behind him. Whether he turned left across a meadow or right along a ditch, they had to follow, stumbling at every step. In the middle of a field they met the priest, who could hardly believe what he was seeing. "Shame on you, you wild girls! What do you mean by running after a young fellow across the open fields?" cried the priest. With these words he seized the youngest by the hand to drag her back, and in that instant he was stuck too and had to trot along behind them. Shortly afterwards the man who rang the church bell came along and saw the priest hurrying after three girls. "Father, slow down! Have you forgotten that there is a christening at the church today?" he called. He caught hold of the priest's sleeve, and he was stuck as fast as the rest. As the five of them puffed along, two farmers were coming back from the fields with their tools over their shoulders. The priest begged them to set him and the bell-ringer free, but as soon as they touched the bell-ringer, they too were part of the line. So there were seven of them now, running in a long, wobbling chain behind Dummling and his goose.

By evening they reached a city whose king had a daughter so solemn that nobody had ever managed to make her laugh. The king had therefore proclaimed that whoever succeeded in doing so should have her hand in marriage. Hearing this, Dummling led his goose and its unwilling followers straight past the princess's window. At the sight of seven people tripping over one another to keep up with a bird, she burst into such laughter that it seemed she might never stop.

Dummling promptly asked for his bride, but the king had no wish for a son-in-law of that sort and began to invent obstacles. Before there could be any wedding, he said, Dummling must bring him a man able to empty every barrel in the royal cellar. Dummling remembered the little gray man and went back to the spot where he had felled the tree. There he found a man sitting with a gloomy face, who explained that he suffered from a thirst so great that nothing he drank seemed to reach it. "Then you are exactly the person I need. Come with me, and you shall have more than enough," said Dummling. He led the man down into the king's cellar, where the stranger set to work on the barrels one after another, and by nightfall not one of them held a drop.

When Dummling once more asked for the princess, the king, annoyed that such a fool should succeed, demanded a man who could eat a mountain of bread. Dummling did not hesitate but went straight back to the forest, and in the same place sat a man pulling his belt tighter and tighter. "I have eaten a whole oven of loaves, yet I am still hungry," the man sighed, and he gladly agreed to come along. The king had flour brought from every corner of the kingdom and baked into an enormous mountain of bread. Yet the man from the forest ate the whole of it in a single day.

For the third time Dummling asked for his bride, and for the third time the king found a condition. "Bring me a ship that sails on land as easily as on water," said the king, "and the princess shall be yours at once." Dummling walked straight into the forest, and there, sitting where it had all begun, was the little gray man with whom he had once shared his cake. "It was I who drank for you and I who ate for you. Now I shall give you the ship as well, because you were kind to me when no one else was," said the little man. The king, seeing the ship come gliding over the fields toward his palace, could find no further excuse. The wedding was held with all due ceremony. When in time the old king's reign came to an end, Dummling inherited the kingdom and lived with his wife in great contentment for the rest of his days.
