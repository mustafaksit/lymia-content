# The Gold-Bug

Summary: A clever man finds a strange gold beetle on a quiet island and starts a hunt for hidden treasure.

## Level A1

William Legrand lived on Sullivan's Island. The island was long, quiet and sandy. Legrand was poor, but very clever. He loved shells and bugs. His old friend Jupiter lived with him. Jupiter was kind and loyal.

One cold evening, I visited them. "I found a new beetle today!" said Legrand. The beetle was gold and very heavy. But an officer from the fort had it. So Legrand drew it on old parchment.

I sat near the fire with the picture. Then Wolf, the big dog, jumped on me. I played with him, and then I looked. "Your beetle looks like a skull!" I laughed. Legrand took the parchment back. His face went white. He put it in his desk.

Later, Jupiter came to my house in Charleston. "Legrand is very strange now," said Jupiter. So I went back with him.

That night, we walked into the hills. Legrand carried the beetle on a string. I thought, "My poor friend is mad." Jupiter and Wolf came with us. We stopped under a very tall tree. Jupiter took the beetle and climbed up. High up, he found an old skull. "Drop it through the left eye!" called Legrand. Jupiter let it down on the string.

Legrand measured fifty feet from the tree. We dug there, but we found nothing. "Show me your left eye," said Legrand. Jupiter touched his right eye. He mixed up left and right! He climbed again and used the other eye. We dug in a new spot.

Soon we found an old wooden box. Inside was a lot of gold! We saw coins, rings and bright jewels. We carried it all home before morning.

Later, Legrand told me everything. The heat of the fire showed secret marks. Captain Kidd, the famous pirate, made them. The marks were a code. Legrand solved it and found the tree. "But why the beetle?" I asked. Legrand smiled at me. "You thought I was mad. So I played a little joke on you."

## Level A2

William Legrand lived on Sullivan's Island, near the city of Charleston. He came from a rich old family, but he lost his money. He lived in a small hut and collected shells and insects. His old friend Jupiter lived there too and looked after him.

One cold evening in October, I visited the hut. Legrand was very excited about a new beetle. "It is bright gold and very heavy," he said. But an officer from the fort had the beetle for the night. So Legrand drew it for me on a piece of old parchment.

I took the drawing and sat near the fire. The big dog Wolf jumped on me. I played with him, with the parchment still in my hand. "This beetle looks just like a skull!" I laughed. Legrand took the parchment and stared at it. His face went white, then red. He locked it in his desk and said very little.

About a month later, Jupiter came to see me in Charleston. "Legrand does not eat or sleep well," said Jupiter. Jupiter also told me about strange numbers and signs. Jupiter blamed the gold bug for everything. He also brought a note from Legrand. It said, "Please come back with Jupiter."

At the hut, Legrand held the gold beetle in his hand. "This beetle can make me rich," he said. I looked at him and thought, "My poor friend is mad." We walked for two hours into the wild hills. Jupiter carried the tools, and Wolf ran with us. At last, Legrand stopped under a very tall old tree. "Climb up, Jupiter, and take the beetle with you," he said. Jupiter tied the bug to a string and climbed up.

On the seventh big branch, he found an old skull. "Drop the beetle through its left eye!" shouted Legrand. The beetle fell slowly on its string. Then Legrand measured fifty feet from the tree, past the beetle.

We dug for a long time, but we found nothing. "Show me your left eye," Legrand said to his friend. Jupiter pointed at his own right eye. High in the dark tree, he mixed up left and right. Jupiter climbed again and used the other eye.

We dug again, and soon our tools hit something hard. It was a big wooden chest with iron rings on the sides. Inside, there were gold coins, rings, chains and bright jewels. We carried the treasure home and finished at sunrise.

Later, Legrand told me the whole story. He found the beetle and the parchment on the beach. The heat of my fire showed hidden marks on the parchment. There was a skull, a little goat and many signs. A young goat is a "kid." So the goat meant Captain Kidd, the famous pirate.

The signs were a secret code. In English, the most common letter is "e." Legrand looked for the most common sign and slowly read the message. It spoke about Bessop's Castle, a tree and a skull. An old woman showed him Bessop's Castle, a tall rock. From the rock, he saw the skull through a telescope. "But why the beetle?" I asked. Legrand smiled at me. "You thought I was mad. So I played a little joke on you."

## Level B1

Some years ago, my friend William Legrand lived on Sullivan's Island, near Charleston. The island was about three miles long and mostly sand, grass and low bushes. Legrand came from a wealthy family, but bad luck took his money away. So he built a small hut on the island and lived there quietly. He spent his days on the beach, looking for shells and insects. Jupiter, an old friend of his family, lived with him and looked after him.

On a cold evening in October, I walked out to the hut. Legrand was excited because he found a new kind of beetle that day. "It is the color of bright gold," he told me. Sadly, an officer from the fort was keeping the beetle for the night. "I will draw it for you," Legrand said. He took an old piece of parchment from his pocket and began to draw. Jupiter, who did not trust the shiny bug, shook his head.

When Legrand gave me the drawing, I was sitting close to the fire. At that moment his large dog, Wolf, rushed in and jumped on me. I played with the dog, and the parchment stayed in my hand near the flames. Then I looked at the picture. "Your beetle looks exactly like a skull," I said with a smile. Legrand was a little hurt and took the parchment back. But then he looked at it again, and his face went pale. He locked it carefully in his desk and hardly spoke for the rest of the evening.

About a month later, Jupiter came to my house in Charleston with a worried face. He told me that his friend was pale and thin. Legrand was writing numbers and strange signs on a slate all day long. Jupiter blamed the gold bug, which bit Legrand's finger on the first day. He also brought a short letter from Legrand, which asked me to come at once.

At the hut, Legrand held up the beetle, which the officer returned. "This bug will make my fortune," he said seriously. I began to worry about my friend's mind, but I agreed to go with him. That night we walked for two hours into wild and lonely hills. Jupiter carried three new spades, and Wolf ran ahead of us.

At last, we stopped under a giant tulip tree, taller than all the others. "Climb as high as you can, Jupiter," said Legrand, "and take the beetle." Jupiter did not like the beetle, but he trusted his friend, so he climbed. Near the seventh big branch, he called down in surprise. "Somebody nailed an old skull to this branch long ago!" Jupiter shouted. "Let the beetle fall through its left eye," Legrand shouted back.

The beetle came down slowly on its string and shone in the moonlight. Legrand pushed a small stick into the ground under it. Then he measured fifty feet from the tree, past the stick, and we began to dig. We dug for almost two hours, but the hole was empty. Suddenly Legrand asked Jupiter which eye he used. Jupiter touched his right eye. High up in the dark, he chose the wrong side, which anyone could do. Legrand was not angry for long, and soon he was full of fresh hope. Jupiter climbed again and used the other eye, and we moved our line.

This time, after only an hour, our spades hit something hard. It was a large wooden chest with three iron rings on each side. When we opened the lid, our lamp shone on a huge pile of gold. There were old coins, gold chains, rings, cups and shining jewels. We carried the treasure home in two trips and finished at sunrise.

The next day, Legrand finally explained everything. He found the beetle and the parchment on the beach, near an old wreck. Jupiter wrapped the bug in the parchment, and Legrand kept it in his pocket. When I held it near the fire, the heat showed a hidden skull. Later, Legrand warmed it again, and more marks appeared: a little goat and rows of signs. Another word for a young goat is "kid." So the goat was the signature of Captain Kidd, the famous pirate.

The signs were a secret code, but Legrand was a patient man. He knew that "e" is the most common letter in English. So he looked for the most common sign, and the message slowly appeared. It spoke of Bessop's Castle, a seat, a tree, a skull and a line. An old woman showed him a tall rock with the name Bessop's Castle. From a narrow seat in the rock, he looked through his telescope and saw the skull. "But why the beetle?" I asked him at the end. "You thought I was mad," Legrand said, "so I played a little game with you."

## Level B2

Some years ago I became friends with William Legrand, who lived on Sullivan's Island, not far from Charleston. The island is a thin strip of sand about three miles long, covered in coarse grass and thick bushes. Legrand came from an old and once wealthy family, but a series of misfortunes had left him poor. He had built a small hut on the island, where he spent his days fishing and collecting shells and insects. He was clever and widely read, but his moods changed quickly, from bright cheer to deep gloom. Jupiter, an old friend of the Legrand family, had refused to leave him and shared the hut with him.

One chilly evening in October, I walked out to the hut and found a cheerful fire burning. Legrand was in one of his excited moods, because he had caught a beetle that nobody had described before. "It is the color of polished gold," he told me, "and it is far heavier than any beetle should be." Unfortunately, he had lent it to an officer from the fort, so he could not show it to me. "I will draw it for you instead," he said, and he pulled an old scrap of parchment from his pocket. Jupiter, who was convinced that the bug was made of solid gold, wanted nothing to do with it.

While Legrand drew, I moved my chair close to the fire, since the night was surprisingly cold. Just as he handed me the drawing, his large dog, Wolf, burst into the room and jumped on my shoulders. I made a fuss of the dog for a minute, while the parchment stayed in my hand, near the flames. When I finally looked at the picture, I could not help laughing. "If this is your beetle, it looks remarkably like a skull," I said. Legrand was annoyed and reached out to throw the parchment into the fire. Then something on it caught his eye, and the color left his face completely. He stared at it for a long time before locking it carefully in his desk. He was silent for the rest of the evening, so I left earlier than I had planned.

About a month passed before I heard from him again. Then one morning Jupiter appeared at my door in Charleston, looking deeply troubled. He explained that Legrand had grown pale and thin and hardly ever spoke. His friend spent whole days bent over a slate, covering it with numbers and odd signs. Jupiter blamed the gold bug, which had bitten Legrand's finger on the day it was caught. He handed me a short note from Legrand, which begged me to come back with Jupiter at once.

The officer had returned the beetle, and Legrand held it up for me to see. "This bug is going to make my fortune," he said, quite seriously. I was now afraid that my friend's mind was unwell, but I agreed to go with him that night. I hoped that if I humored him, he would soon come home and rest. We set off with Jupiter carrying three new spades and Legrand swinging the beetle on a long string.

We walked for about two hours into a wild, lonely stretch of hills and rocks, with Wolf running ahead. At last Legrand stopped beneath an enormous tulip tree, which stood high above every other tree around it. "Climb up as far as you can, Jupiter," he said, "and take the beetle with you." Jupiter did not want to touch the bug, but he trusted his friend and began to climb. When he reached the seventh large branch, he called down that he had found something odd. An old skull had been nailed to the branch long ago, and the weather had worn it smooth and white. "Let the beetle drop through its left eye," Legrand called, "as far as the string will go."

The beetle came down slowly and hung, glittering, just above the ground. Legrand pressed a peg into the earth directly beneath it. Then he measured fifty feet from the trunk of the tree, in a straight line through the peg. We dug there by lamplight for nearly two hours, but we found nothing except soil and roots. Then Legrand asked Jupiter to point to his left eye. Jupiter touched his right eye without hesitation. High up in the darkness, he had simply confused his left and right, as anyone might do. Legrand's disappointment turned at once into new excitement. Jupiter climbed back up and dropped the beetle through the other eye. Now the line ended several yards from our first hole.

This time we had been digging for barely an hour when Wolf began to bark and scratch at the earth. A moment later our spades struck something hard. It was a large wooden chest, bound with iron and fitted with three iron rings on each side. When we forced open the lid, the light of our lamp fell on a treasure beyond all our dreams. There were heaps of old gold coins from many countries, along with chains, rings, cups and glittering jewels. The chest was far too heavy to lift, so we carried the treasure home in two trips and finished at sunrise.

After we had rested, Legrand finally told me the whole story. He had found the beetle on the beach, near the wreck of an old boat. Jupiter had picked up a piece of parchment lying nearby and used it to wrap the beetle. When I held the parchment near the fire, the heat revealed a skull that had been drawn in invisible ink. Later, Legrand had warmed it carefully, and a small goat had appeared in the corner, along with rows of signs. Since a young goat is called a "kid," the goat was clearly the signature of Captain Kidd, the famous pirate.

The signs were a code, but Legrand was patient and clever. He knew that the letter "e" is used more often than any other in English, so he counted every sign. The most frequent sign stood for "e," and a group of three signs that kept repeating had to be "the." The finished message spoke of a good glass, a bishop's hostel, a tree, a skull and a line fifty feet long. Legrand guessed that "bishop" might be Bessop, the name of an old family from the area. An old woman on the coast then told him about Bessop's Castle, a group of tall rocks. One of the rocks had a narrow seat in it, and Legrand sat there with his telescope. At last, he saw a white spot in a distant tree, and the white spot was the skull. "But why did you make such a fuss about the beetle?" I asked. "You were sure that I had gone mad," Legrand said, laughing, "so I gave you a little mystery of your own."

## Level C1

Many years ago I struck up a friendship with William Legrand, a man of good family who had retreated to Sullivan's Island, a few miles from Charleston. A run of misfortunes had left him poor, and he seemed glad to put the city and its memories behind him. The island hardly deserves the name: it is a thin ribbon of sand about three miles long, held together by coarse grass and a dense, sweet-smelling tangle of myrtle. There, in a modest hut, Legrand lived on what remained of his fortune, fishing, rambling along the shore and filling boxes with shells and insects. He was widely read and quick of mind, yet his spirits swung without warning from bright enthusiasm to a brooding melancholy. With him lived Jupiter, a friend of the Legrand family for many years, who had chosen to stay and watch over the younger man. Legrand listened to Jupiter as he listened to nobody else, and the two of them shared the hut like an uncle and a restless nephew.

On an unusually chilly evening in the middle of October, I made my way out to the hut and found, to my delight, a fire roaring in the hearth. Legrand greeted me in a fever of excitement, for that very afternoon he had caught a beetle which, he insisted, was entirely new to science. "It is the color of burnished gold," he declared, "and it weighs so much that you would hardly believe it was alive." The prize itself, unluckily, was not at hand, having been lent for the night to a curious officer from the fort. "Never mind," said Legrand, "I will sketch it for you," and he drew from his pocket a stained scrap of what looked like old parchment. Jupiter, who regarded the glittering creature with deep suspicion, remarked that it was solid gold through and through and that he wanted nothing to do with it.

While Legrand bent over his sketch, I drew my chair closer to the blaze, for the walk had chilled me through. No sooner had he passed me the drawing than his great dog, Wolf, came bounding in and threw his paws onto my shoulders. I spent a minute or two returning the dog's welcome, holding the parchment all the while a little too near the flames. When at last I studied the drawing, I could not keep a straight face. "I confess this is a strange beetle," I said, "because to my eye it looks exactly like a skull." Stung, Legrand snatched the parchment back, plainly meaning to toss it into the fire, but some detail on its surface stopped his hand. I watched the color drain from his face; he stared at the scrap for many minutes, then locked it away in his desk with unusual care. For the rest of the evening he sat lost in thought, answering me in single words, and I took my leave far earlier than I had intended.

A month went by without a word from the island, and then one morning Jupiter appeared at my door in Charleston, his kind face lined with worry. Legrand, he told me, had grown thin and pale, and he sat for hours covering a slate with figures and peculiar signs. In Jupiter's view the trouble had begun with the gold bug, which had nipped Legrand's finger on the day they caught it. He handed me a brief, oddly formal note in which Legrand begged me to return with Jupiter that very day on a matter of the greatest importance.

At the hut, Legrand received me with a calm that unsettled me more than any wildness could have done. The officer had returned the beetle, and Legrand held it up to the light as though it were a crown jewel. "This bug," he said gravely, "is going to make my fortune." By now I was convinced that my friend's mind had given way, yet I reasoned that if I humored him tonight, I might persuade him to rest tomorrow. So we set off at dusk, a curious little procession: Jupiter shouldering three new spades, Legrand swinging the beetle on a long string, and Wolf racing ahead.

For some two hours we pushed through wild and desolate country, all ridges, gullies and tumbled rocks. At last Legrand halted beneath a gigantic tulip tree that towered over everything around it. "Climb, Jupiter," he said, "as high as you can go, and take the beetle with you." Jupiter would have preferred to carry almost anything else, but his trust in his friend outweighed his dislike of the bug, and up he went. At the seventh great limb he cried out that he had found something: an old skull, fixed to the branch by a nail and bleached by years of sun and rain. "Find its left eye," Legrand called up, "and let the beetle down through it, as far as the string will reach."

The beetle descended slowly and came to rest a few inches above the ground, turning and glinting in the lamplight like a tiny sun. Legrand drove a peg into the earth directly beneath it, ran a tape from the trunk through the peg, and marked a spot fifty feet out. There we dug for the better part of two hours, and there we found nothing at all but roots, pebbles and the cold smell of soil. Legrand stood motionless at the edge of the pit; then, in a voice I had not heard before, he asked Jupiter to show him his left eye. Without a moment's doubt, Jupiter laid a finger on his right one. High among the leaves, in the dark, he had simply confused one side with the other, a slip that any of us might have made. What might have been despair became, in an instant, a fresh blaze of hope, and Jupiter climbed once more to drop the beetle through the proper eye. The new line ended several yards from our first hole, and we set to work again with blistered hands and lighter hearts.

We had been digging for scarcely an hour when Wolf began to bark and claw at the loose earth. Moments later a spade rang against something hard, and we uncovered a long wooden chest, bound with iron and fitted with three iron rings on each side. When we forced open the lid and raised the lamp, what lay inside left us speechless. There were heaps of ancient gold coins from half the countries of the world, along with chains, rings, heavy cups and jewels that flashed in every color. The chest itself was far too heavy to move, so we lightened it and carried the treasure back to the hut in two journeys. The sky was already turning gray when we set down the last of it, and its worth proved to be beyond anything we had imagined.

Only after we had slept did Legrand reveal how the whole affair had come about. He had found the beetle on the shore near the half-buried wreck of an old ship's boat. Jupiter, unwilling to touch the creature with bare hands, had wrapped it in a piece of parchment lying close by. That evening, finding no paper in the hut, Legrand had sketched the beetle on the back of this very scrap without noticing anything else upon it. It was the heat of the fire, while I played with Wolf, that had brought out a skull drawn long before in an ink which stays invisible until it is warmed. Alone later, Legrand had heated the parchment again and found, in one corner, the figure of a young goat, together with lines of numbers and odd symbols. A young goat, of course, is a kid, and the little figure was nothing less than the signature of Captain Kidd, the most famous pirate ever to sail along that coast.

What followed was patient work rather than magic. Knowing that "e" occurs more often than any other letter in English, Legrand counted every symbol and gave "e" to the most frequent one. Next he hunted for a group of three symbols that recurred again and again, since such a group could only be "the." Letter by letter the message surrendered, though in its finished form it read like a riddle. It spoke of a good glass, a bishop's hostel, a seat, a compass bearing, a seventh branch, a skull's left eye and a line fifty feet long. Suspecting that the "bishop" was a garbled echo of Bessop, the name of an old local family, Legrand made patient inquiries along the coast. At last an old woman told him of Bessop's Castle, a jumble of high rocks, one of which had a narrow ledge shaped like a chair. Seated on that ledge with a telescope, Legrand had at last made out a pale spot among the leaves of a distant tree, and the pale spot was the skull. "But why on earth did you make us carry that beetle all night?" I asked him finally. "You had quite decided that I was out of my mind," he said, "so I thought it only fair to give you a little mystery to puzzle over."
