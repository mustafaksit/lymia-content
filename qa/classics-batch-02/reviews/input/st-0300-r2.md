# Emma

Summary: A clever young woman loves to arrange other people's marriages but cannot see her own heart.

## Level A1

Emma Woodhouse was young, rich and clever. She lived at Hartfield with her old father. Their home was in the village of Highbury. Emma liked to find husbands for her friends. Mr. Knightley was an old family friend. "Stop this game, Emma," said Mr. Knightley. But Emma did not listen.

Emma had a new friend, Harriet Smith. A young farmer, Robert Martin, loved Harriet. He asked her to marry him. "Robert is only a farmer," said Emma. "Mr. Elton is better for you." So Harriet said no to Robert. Mr. Knightley was angry with Emma.

Mr. Elton worked at the church. Emma made a picture of Harriet. Mr. Elton loved the picture. Then, one snowy night, Mr. Elton spoke. "I love you, Emma, not Harriet!" Emma said no to him. Later, Mr. Elton married a proud lady. Poor Harriet cried and cried.

Then Frank Churchill came to Highbury. He was handsome and full of fun. Quiet Jane Fairfax came too. Jane lived with her aunt, Miss Bates. Miss Bates was poor and kind. She talked and talked all day.

In summer, the friends went to Box Hill. "Say one clever thing," said Frank. "Or say three dull things!" "Three dull things? Easy!" said Miss Bates. "Yes, but only three!" said Emma. Miss Bates went red.

Later, Mr. Knightley spoke to Emma. "You were unkind, Emma," he said. "Miss Bates is poor and old." Emma cried all the way home. The next day, she visited Miss Bates.

Soon Highbury heard big news. Frank and Jane loved each other! Then Harriet came to Emma. "I love Mr. Knightley," said Harriet. Suddenly Emma understood her own heart. She loved Mr. Knightley too!

A few days later, Mr. Knightley came. He and Emma walked in the garden. "I love you, Emma," he said. "Please marry me." Emma said yes. Robert Martin asked Harriet again. This time, she said yes. Harriet married Robert, and Emma married Mr. Knightley. Mr. Knightley came to live at Hartfield.

## Level A2

Emma Woodhouse was nearly twenty-one. She was pretty, clever and rich. She lived with her father at Hartfield, in the village of Highbury. Her father was a kind old man, but he worried about everything. Then Emma's teacher married Mr. Weston, a cheerful man. "I made this match!" Emma told everyone.

Mr. Knightley was an old friend of the family. He often told Emma about her mistakes. "It was only a lucky guess," he said. "Please leave people alone." But Emma wanted a new match.

Soon Emma met Harriet Smith, a sweet girl of seventeen. A young farmer, Robert Martin, loved Harriet. He wrote her a letter and asked her to marry him. "A farmer is not good enough for you," said Emma. So Harriet said no. Mr. Knightley was very angry. "Robert Martin is a good, honest man," he said. "You are wrong, Emma."

Emma wanted Harriet to marry Mr. Elton. He was the young man at the church. Emma painted a picture of Harriet, and Mr. Elton loved it. He also wrote a little love poem. "It is for you, Harriet!" said Emma.

At Christmas, everyone had dinner at Mr. Weston's house. Emma and Mr. Elton rode home in the same carriage. "Dear Emma, I love you!" he cried. "But what about Harriet?" asked Emma. "Harriet? I never cared about Harriet!" Emma said no to him. Later, Mr. Elton married a rich, proud lady. Poor Harriet cried for days, and Emma felt terrible.

Then Frank Churchill, Mr. Weston's son, came to Highbury. He was handsome, lively and full of jokes. Jane Fairfax came to stay with her aunt, Miss Bates. Miss Bates was poor and kind, and she talked all the time. Jane was quiet and very good at music. Emma did not like her much.

Frank and Emma laughed together a lot. But Emma did not love Frank. At a ball, Mr. Elton refused to dance with Harriet. Then Mr. Knightley came and danced with Harriet himself. Emma was very grateful to him.

In summer, the friends had a picnic at Box Hill. "Everyone must say one clever thing or three dull things," said Frank. "Three dull things? Easy for me!" laughed Miss Bates. "Ah, but only three at one time!" said Emma. Miss Bates went quiet, and her face turned red.

Later, Mr. Knightley spoke to Emma alone. "How could you be so unkind, Emma?" he asked. "Miss Bates is poor, and she was always good to you." Emma cried all the way home. The next morning, she went to see Miss Bates.

Soon there was big news in Highbury. Frank and Jane had a secret promise to marry! Emma worried about Harriet, but Harriet only smiled. "I don't love Frank," said Harriet. "I love Mr. Knightley. He danced with me at the ball." Suddenly Emma understood her own heart. She loved Mr. Knightley and wanted him for herself.

A few days later, Mr. Knightley found Emma in the garden. "I never loved Frank," said Emma. "I love you, Emma," said Mr. Knightley. "Can you love me too?" Emma said yes with all her heart. Soon Robert Martin asked Harriet again, and she said yes. Both friends married in the autumn. Emma's father did not want to lose her. So Mr. Knightley came to live at Hartfield.

## Level B1

Emma Woodhouse was clever, beautiful and rich, and she was nearly twenty-one years old. She lived with her father at Hartfield, the finest house in the village of Highbury. Her father was a gentle old man who worried all the time about his health. That autumn, Emma's governess married Mr. Weston, a cheerful neighbor, and Emma felt very proud. "I made that match," she told everyone.

Mr. Knightley, who lived at Donwell, was an old friend of the family. He was also the only person who ever told Emma about her faults. "You did not make that match," he said. "You made a lucky guess." "Then I will make another one," Emma answered with a laugh.

Soon Emma found a new friend, Harriet Smith, a sweet and pretty girl of seventeen. Nobody knew Harriet's parents, but Emma decided to find her a fine husband. However, a young farmer named Robert Martin already loved Harriet. When he wrote and asked her to marry him, she ran to Emma. Emma did not say no directly, but she made her opinion very clear. So Harriet sadly wrote back and refused him. Mr. Knightley was furious. "Robert Martin is a sensible, honest man," he told Emma. "You are filling her head with silly ideas."

Emma already had a better plan for Harriet: Mr. Elton, the handsome young vicar. She painted a picture of Harriet, and Mr. Elton praised it again and again. He also brought a little love poem, and Emma felt sure it was for Harriet.

On Christmas Eve, everyone had dinner at Mr. Weston's house. When snow began to fall, the guests hurried home in their carriages. By bad luck, Emma found herself alone with Mr. Elton. Suddenly he took her hand and told her he loved her. "But what about Harriet?" Emma cried. "Miss Smith?" said Mr. Elton. "I never thought of her for a moment!" Emma refused him coldly, and they rode the rest of the way in silence. Mr. Elton soon went away, and he came back with a rich, proud wife. Harriet cried for days, and Emma felt deeply ashamed of her plans.

Then two young people came to Highbury. Frank Churchill was Mr. Weston's son, who lived far away with his rich aunt and uncle. He was handsome and charming, and he seemed to like Emma very much. At the same time, Jane Fairfax came to stay with her aunt, Miss Bates. Miss Bates was poor and no longer young, but she was cheerful and kind to everyone. She talked so much that people often stopped listening. Jane played the piano beautifully, but she was very quiet. Emma did not really like her. Soon a fine new piano arrived for Jane, and nobody knew the sender.

Frank and Emma spent a lot of time together, laughing. But after a while, Emma saw that she did not love him. At a ball at the inn, Mr. Elton refused to dance with Harriet. Then Mr. Knightley, who almost never danced, asked Harriet to dance. Emma felt very grateful to him. Later, Harriet said she loved a man far above her. Emma felt sure she meant Frank.

In June, a large group went for a picnic on Box Hill. Everyone seemed tired and bored, so Frank suggested a game. "Each person must say one very clever thing, or three very dull things," he said. "Three dull things?" laughed Miss Bates. "That will be easy for me!" Emma could not stop herself. "Yes, but you must stop after three," she said. Miss Bates did not understand at first, and then her face turned red.

As the party was leaving, Mr. Knightley spoke to Emma alone. "Emma, how could you be so unkind to her? She is poor, and she is growing old. She knew you as a baby, and she was always good to you." Emma cried all the way home. Early the next morning, she went to visit Miss Bates.

Soon after that, Frank's aunt died, and a big secret came out. Frank and Jane had a secret promise to marry! Frank paid attention to Emma only to hide the truth. Emma felt foolish, but she worried most about Harriet. "Mr. Churchill? I don't care about him," said Harriet. "I love Mr. Knightley. He was so kind to me at the ball." In that moment, Emma understood her own heart. She loved Mr. Knightley, and she wanted him for herself.

A few days later, Mr. Knightley found Emma in the garden at Hartfield. "I never loved Frank," Emma told him. "I love you, Emma," Mr. Knightley said. "Can you love me in return?" Emma said yes with all her heart. Soon Robert Martin asked Harriet again, and this time she happily accepted. Harriet married her farmer in September, and Emma married Mr. Knightley soon after. Because Emma's father hated change, Mr. Knightley came to live with them at Hartfield.

## Level B2

At almost twenty-one, Emma Woodhouse had beauty, intelligence and money, and very little had ever gone wrong in her life. She lived at Hartfield, the grandest house in Highbury, with her father, a gentle, nervous man who disliked every kind of change.

For sixteen years, Emma's governess had been more of a friend than a teacher. When this good woman married Mr. Weston, a cheerful neighbor, Emma was delighted and took full credit for the match. "I planned it four years ago," she announced, "and everybody said it would never happen."

Mr. Knightley of Donwell Abbey listened with a smile. Sixteen years older than Emma, he was almost the only person who ever pointed out her faults. "A lucky guess is not the same as success," he told her. "Leave people to manage their own lives." But Emma had already found her next project.

Harriet Smith was a pretty, good-natured girl of seventeen who boarded at the village school. Nobody knew who her parents were, and Emma decided they must be people of good family. She also decided that Harriet's friends, the Martins, were far beneath her. Robert Martin, who rented a farm from Mr. Knightley, was a hard-working young man and clearly in love with Harriet. When he wrote her a sincere, sensible letter asking her to marry him, Harriet brought it straight to Emma. Emma was careful not to give an order, but she made her opinion impossible to miss. In the end, Harriet wrote a polite refusal. Mr. Knightley was angrier than Emma had ever seen him. "You have done your friend no good at all," he said. "Martin is a far better man than you realize, and you are filling her head with foolish ideas."

Emma's real plan was Mr. Elton, the handsome young vicar. She painted a portrait of Harriet, and Mr. Elton praised it so warmly that he took it to London to be framed. Soon afterward he left a love riddle at Hartfield, and Emma was certain it was meant for Harriet. Mr. Knightley warned her that Mr. Elton would never marry a girl without money, but she simply laughed.

On Christmas Eve, the whole party dined at Mr. Weston's house. Snow began to fall, and as the guests hurried home, Emma found herself alone in a carriage with Mr. Elton. Before they had gone far, he seized her hand and declared his love, not for Harriet, but for Emma. When she mentioned her friend, he looked astonished. "Miss Smith?" he said. "Who could think of Miss Smith when Miss Woodhouse is near?" Emma refused him in the coldest possible words, and they finished the journey in furious silence. Within a few weeks Mr. Elton had gone away and found himself a wife. She was a loud, proud woman with a fortune, and she soon began telling all of Highbury what to do. Harriet's heart was broken, and Emma, for once, felt truly ashamed.

Frank Churchill, Mr. Weston's son, had been raised far away by his rich aunt and uncle. He turned out to be handsome, lively and charming, and he paid Emma so much attention that everyone expected a wedding. At the same time, Jane Fairfax came home to stay with her aunt, Miss Bates, and her old grandmother. Miss Bates was poor, no longer young, and so kind and talkative that people sometimes smiled behind her back. Jane herself was elegant, gifted and very reserved, and Emma found her cold. Then a beautiful piano arrived for Jane from an unknown sender, and the whole village talked of nothing else.

Frank and Emma laughed and joked together constantly, yet Emma slowly understood that she was not in love with him. At a ball at the village inn, Mr. Elton publicly refused to dance with Harriet, while his wife smiled. Mr. Knightley, who rarely danced, crossed the room at once and led Harriet to the floor himself. It was a small act of kindness, but Harriet never forgot it. Some weeks later Harriet confessed that she cared for a man far above her, and Emma was sure she meant Frank.

One warm summer day, a party set out to enjoy the famous view from Box Hill. To liven things up, Frank proposed a game. "Each of you must say one very clever thing, two fairly clever things, or three very dull ones," he said. "Then I am perfectly safe," cried Miss Bates. "I am sure to say three dull things as soon as I open my mouth!" Emma could not resist. "I'm afraid there is a problem," she said. "You must stop after three." For a moment Miss Bates did not understand. Then she blushed deeply and said, in a small voice, that she would try to keep quiet.

When the carriages came round, Mr. Knightley took Emma aside. "How could you be so unfeeling to a woman like her?" he asked. "She is poor, she has lost the easy life she once had, and she will have even less as she grows older. She has known you since you were a baby, and you laughed at her in front of everyone." All the way home tears ran down her face. Early the next morning she called on Miss Bates to make what amends she could.

Not long afterward, news came that Frank's aunt had died. Then the real secret came out. Frank and Jane had been engaged since the previous autumn, and he had flirted with Emma only to hide it. Emma was embarrassed, but her first thought was for Harriet, whose heart she expected to be broken a second time. Harriet, however, was quite calm. "Mr. Churchill?" she said. "I never thought of him. It is Mr. Knightley I care for, and I think he may care for me." At these words, Emma saw the truth about herself as clearly as if a curtain had been pulled back. She loved Mr. Knightley, and she could not bear the thought of him marrying anyone but her.

A few days later Mr. Knightley found her walking in the garden at Hartfield. He had come to comfort her about Frank, and he was relieved to hear that she had never cared for him. Then, slowly and with difficulty, he spoke of his own feelings. "I am no good at fine speeches, Emma," he said. "But you know me, and you know what I feel." Emma's answer was everything he had hoped for.

Robert Martin asked her again, and she accepted him with real joy. Her father was discovered to be an honest tradesman, and nobody minded in the least. Because Emma's father could not bear to lose his daughter, Mr. Knightley offered to leave Donwell and live at Hartfield. Harriet and Robert married in September, and Emma and Mr. Knightley a month later, among the friends who loved them best.

## Level C1

Emma Woodhouse, at the age of almost twenty-one, possessed beauty, wit and a handsome fortune, and she had passed through life so smoothly that nothing had ever seriously troubled her. She was mistress of Hartfield, the principal house in Highbury, where she cared for her father, a kindly, anxious gentleman who regarded every change as a disaster.

The first change of that autumn was the marriage of Emma's governess, a gentle woman who had lived with the family for sixteen years and had long been more friend than teacher. She married Mr. Weston, a good-natured gentleman of the neighborhood, and moved into his house half a mile away. Emma missed her, but she consoled herself with a far more agreeable thought. "It was I who made that match," she declared. "Everyone said Mr. Weston would never marry again, and I alone saw that he would."

Mr. Knightley, who had walked over from Donwell Abbey that evening, was not impressed. Some sixteen years older than Emma and a man of plain sense and plainer speech, he was nearly the only person in Highbury who ever told her she was wrong. "Real success takes more than guessing," he observed dryly. "You made a lucky guess, and a lucky guess is all it was." Emma only laughed, and announced that she now intended to find a wife for the young vicar, Mr. Elton.

The instrument of Emma's next scheme was Harriet Smith, a pretty, soft-hearted girl of seventeen who lodged at the village school and whose parents were entirely unknown. Emma resolved that a girl so charming must have a gentleman for a father, and she set about improving her accordingly. Unfortunately, Harriet already had admirers of a humbler kind. She had spent a happy summer with the Martins, a respectable farming family, and Robert Martin, who rented a farm from Mr. Knightley, had quietly fallen in love with her. When his proposal arrived in a letter so honest and well written that even Emma was surprised by it, Harriet carried it to Hartfield in a flutter. Emma never actually forbade her to accept, but every smile and every pause made her wishes plain, and Harriet wrote her refusal at Emma's elbow. Mr. Knightley, learning of it, was more seriously displeased than Emma had ever known him. "You have taught her to despise the one man who would have made her happy," he said, "and all for the sake of a fancy of your own."

The fancy, of course, was Mr. Elton. Emma drew Harriet's likeness, and the vicar admired it with such enthusiasm that he begged to carry it to London for framing. He then left at Hartfield a carefully composed riddle on the word "courtship", which Emma copied out for Harriet with deep satisfaction. Mr. Knightley's warning that Mr. Elton was not the man to marry without money was dismissed with a smile.

The truth emerged on Christmas Eve. Mr. Weston had given a dinner, snow had begun to fall, and in the general hurry to get home Emma found herself sharing a carriage with Mr. Elton. Scarcely had the door been shut when he caught her hand and poured out a declaration of love, addressed, to her dismay, entirely to herself. "Miss Smith!" he exclaimed, when she reminded him of her friend. "I have never given Miss Smith a thought. Every attention I have paid in this house has been for you." Emma rejected him with icy finality, and they completed the journey in a silence that neither of them attempted to break. Within a few weeks he had left Highbury, and within a few months he returned with a bride, a rich, self-satisfied woman who began at once to improve the village. Breaking the news to Harriet was the hardest task Emma had ever set herself, and her friend's tears were a reproach she could not answer.

The new year brought two arrivals. Frank Churchill, Mr. Weston's son, had been raised by a wealthy aunt and uncle, and his visits to Highbury had been promised and postponed for years. When at last he appeared, he proved handsome, quick and delightful, and he devoted himself to Emma with an eagerness that set every tongue in the village wagging. At almost the same moment, Jane Fairfax came to stay with her aunt, Miss Bates, and her aged grandmother. Miss Bates had once known comfortable days, and though her circumstances had narrowed, her goodwill had not; she talked without pause, praised everyone, and asked for nothing. Jane was as elegant and accomplished as her aunt was simple, but she was so guarded that Emma, who ought to have been her friend, found her impossible to like. When a fine piano arrived for Jane with no name attached, the village could speak of nothing else, and Emma amused Frank with idle guesses.

As the weeks passed, Emma discovered that she enjoyed Frank's company but could live very well without it. At a ball at the Crown Inn, Mr. Elton made a public show of refusing to dance with Harriet, exchanging a smile with his wife as he did so. It was Mr. Knightley, who seldom danced at all, who crossed the room and offered Harriet his arm, and Emma watched the pair with a gratitude she did not stop to examine. Not long afterward, Harriet shyly confessed to an attachment to a gentleman far above her in station, and Emma, certain that she meant Frank, begged her not to say his name.

The party to Box Hill took place on a fine day in June, yet from the first it refused to be a success. The company divided into sulky groups, and Frank, in an odd and feverish mood, flattered Emma so loudly that she began to answer in kind. At length, he announced a game: each person was to produce one thing very clever, two things moderately clever, or three things very dull indeed. "Oh, then I need not be uneasy," cried Miss Bates, beaming round at everyone. "Three dull things will be no trouble to me at all, I am sure." "There is one obstacle, I fear," said Emma sweetly. "You will have to count them, and stop at three." Miss Bates did not grasp it at once; when she did, the color rose in her cheeks. She murmured that she supposed she must be very tiresome, since dear Miss Woodhouse had said so.

Mr. Knightley waited until the carriages were ready and then spoke to Emma where no one could hear. "How could you turn your cleverness against a woman of her age and character, and in her circumstances? A rich woman might laugh off such a joke. But she is poor; she has fallen far from the life she knew as a girl, and old age will only make her poorer. She saw you grow up, and now you humble her before the very people who take their manners from you." She wept the whole way home, and at an early hour the next morning she was sitting in Miss Bates's small parlor, doing her best, with real humility, to make amends.

Shortly afterward, word came that Frank's aunt had died, and soon a long-kept secret came to light. Frank and Jane Fairfax had been engaged since the previous autumn, and every compliment he had paid Emma had been a screen for his real attachment. Emma felt foolish, and a little ill-used, but her chief anxiety was for Harriet, who must surely be heartbroken a second time. Harriet, however, received the news with perfect composure. "Mr. Churchill?" she said. "Oh no, it was never him. It was the one who rescued me at the ball, when Mr. Elton was so unkind. And from some things he has said, I have begun to hope that he does not think me beneath him." In a single moment the truth broke upon Emma with painful clearness: the thought of Mr. Knightley married to Harriet, or to anyone else, was unbearable, because she wanted him for herself.

The days that followed were the most miserable of her life, filled with the recollection of every blunder she had made. Then one gray afternoon Mr. Knightley, returning from London, joined her on the garden path at Hartfield, plainly expecting to console a broken heart. When she told him, with some embarrassment, that she had never truly cared for Frank, his face changed. "I have not the gift of fine words, Emma," he said at last. "For years I have scolded you and found fault with you, and you have always listened with patience. Listen once more, and tell me whether I may hope." What she replied is of less importance than the fact that it was everything he had wished to hear.

Harriet's fortunes righted themselves with surprising speed. Robert Martin renewed his offer, and this time she accepted him without a moment's doubt and with considerable joy. Her parentage, when finally revealed, turned out to be that of a respectable tradesman, which would once have shocked Emma and now troubled nobody. As Mr. Woodhouse could not be reconciled to losing his daughter, Mr. Knightley proposed, to everyone's astonishment, to leave Donwell and make his home at Hartfield. Harriet and Robert were married in September, and in October Emma and Mr. Knightley followed, quietly and without show, among friends who wished them nothing but happiness.
