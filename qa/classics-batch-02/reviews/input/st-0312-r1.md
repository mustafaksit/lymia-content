# Flatland

Summary: A flat square from a flat world meets a strange visitor who speaks of a third dimension.

## Level A1

Square lived in Flatland. Flatland was a flat world. It had no up and no down. The people there were flat shapes. Square had four sides and four corners. He was calm, and he loved numbers.

One night, Square had a strange dream. In the dream, he was in Lineland. Lineland was only one long line. The people there were short lines and points. They could only move forward and back. The king of Lineland was very proud. "My line is the world," said the king. "No, the world is much bigger," said Square. The king did not believe him. He got angry, and Square woke up.

The next night, Square sat at home. Suddenly, he heard a voice. Nobody was in the room. Then a small point appeared. It grew into a big circle. "I am Sphere," said the visitor. "I come from Spaceland."

Square did not understand. "There is no up!" said Square. Sphere tried to explain. Square still did not believe him. So Sphere lifted Square out of Flatland.

Now Square saw a new world. He looked down at Flatland from above. He saw inside every house and every box. Sphere was round and solid, not flat. "This is Spaceland!" cried Square. He was very happy.

Then Sphere showed him Pointland. A tiny point lived there alone. "I am everything," said the point. But Square wanted to learn more. "Is there a world above Spaceland?" he asked. Sphere got angry at this question. He sent Square back down to Flatland.

Square woke up in his own house. He wanted to tell everyone about Spaceland. He talked about up and down. But nobody believed him. The leaders of Flatland were angry. They put Square in prison.

Square stayed in prison for seven years. He was alone, but he had hope. He wrote a book about Spaceland. Maybe someone can read it one day.

## Level A2

Square lived in Flatland, a world as flat as paper. Nothing in Flatland could go up or down. Things moved only north, south, east and west. The people were flat shapes, like lines, squares and circles. From the side, every person looked like a simple line. Square was a careful man, and he loved numbers.

Near the end of the year, he had a strange dream. In the dream, he stood in Lineland. This world was just one long, straight line. Its people were short lines and points, all in a row. They could only move forward and back, never to the side. The king of Lineland was a long line in the middle. "Where are you?" asked the king. "I hear your voice, but I can't see you."

Square tried to explain his flat world. He moved into the line and out again. The king only saw a short line, and then nothing. "You are a liar," said the king. "Your world is too small," answered Square. The king grew angry, and his people moved toward Square. Just then, Square woke up.

The next evening, Square sat alone in his room. Suddenly he heard a voice, but nobody was there. Then a point appeared in front of him. The point grew into a small circle, and then a large one. "I am Sphere," said the visitor. "I come from Spaceland, a world of three dimensions."

Square did not understand these words. "There is no up," said Square. "There is only north, south, east and west." Sphere tried to explain many times. He rose slowly, and the circle grew smaller and smaller. Then it disappeared, and a moment later it came back. But Square still did not believe him. At last, Sphere lifted him out of Flatland.

For a moment, Square felt sick and afraid. Then he opened his eyes and saw a new world. He looked down at Flatland from above. He saw inside every house and every closed box. Next to him, Sphere was round and solid, not flat. "Now I understand!" cried Square. "This is the third dimension!" His heart was full of joy.

Then Sphere showed him Pointland, a world with no size at all. A single point lived there and talked only to itself. "I am everything," said the point. "Nothing else is real." But Square wanted to learn more. "Is there a fourth dimension, above Spaceland?" he asked. Sphere became angry at this question. Suddenly, Square fell back down into Flatland.

Square woke up in his own house. He wanted to share his news with everyone. He spoke about up and down, about solid shapes and Spaceland. But nobody believed him. The leaders of Flatland did not like his strange ideas. They sent Square to prison.

Square stayed there for seven years. Nobody came to visit him, and the days were long. Still, he never stopped believing in Spaceland. He wrote a book about his adventure. Maybe one day, someone can read it and understand.

## Level B1

Square lived in Flatland, a world that was completely flat. Imagine a huge sheet of paper with shapes that slide across it. In Flatland, nobody could go up or down, because there was no up or down. The people were lines, squares and other flat shapes with many sides. When two people met, each one saw the other only as a line. They had to feel each other's corners to know who they were. Square was a calm, careful man who loved numbers.

On the last night of the year, Square had a strange dream. He found himself in Lineland, a world that was only one straight line. Its people were short lines and points, and they all lived in a row. They could move forward and back, but never to the side. In the middle stood the king, who was the longest line of all. "Who is talking to me?" asked the king. "I can hear you, but I can't see you."

Square tried to explain where he came from. "My world is wide, and it lies all around your line," he said. To prove it, he moved into the line and out again. The king saw only a short line that appeared and then disappeared. "That is a trick," said the king angrily. "Your world is too small for you to see the truth," replied Square. The king's people began to move toward Square, and at that moment he woke up.

The next day, Square was teaching his young grandson about shapes. "A point that moves makes a line," explained Square. "And a line that moves to the side makes a square." The boy thought for a moment. "Then a square that moves upward must make something new," he said. Square laughed at him. "That is nonsense, because there is no upward," he told the boy. He sent his grandson to bed and sat alone, still thinking about it.

Late that evening, Square heard a voice in his locked room. Then he saw a small point, which slowly grew into a circle. "I am Sphere," said the visitor. "I come from Spaceland, the land of three dimensions." Square was confused, and a little afraid. "How did you get into my room?" he asked. "I came from above," said Sphere. "Above? There is no above!" cried Square.

Sphere tried hard to explain. He said that he was really many circles, one on top of another. To show this, he rose slowly out of Flatland. Square watched the circle get smaller and smaller, until it was gone. Then Sphere came back, but Square still refused to believe him. In the end, Sphere decided there was only one way to teach him. He lifted Square right out of Flatland.

At first, everything was dark and strange, and Square felt sick. Then he opened his eyes and saw Spaceland. Below him lay Flatland, and he could see inside every house. He could see the rooms, the people, and even the inside of closed boxes. Sphere was beside him, round and solid, and now Square could see his true shape. "I understand now!" said Square. "This is the third dimension, and it is wonderful!"

Soon, however, Square wanted to learn even more. "You came from above my world," he said to Sphere. "So is there another world above yours, with a fourth dimension?" Sphere did not like this question at all. To teach him a lesson, he showed Square Pointland, a world with no size. A tiny point lived there alone and spoke only to itself. "I am everything, and everything is me," said the point happily. "Do you see?" said Sphere. "That creature will never want to learn anything new." But Square kept asking about higher worlds. At last Sphere lost his patience, and Square fell back down into Flatland.

Square woke up at home and remembered everything. He wanted to tell everyone the wonderful truth about Spaceland. First he tried with his grandson, but the boy did not understand. Then he spoke to other people in the city. They laughed at him, and some of them became angry. The leaders of Flatland did not want anyone to talk about a third dimension. They arrested Square and sent him to prison.

Square stayed in prison for seven long years. Nobody believed his story, but he never stopped believing it himself. He wrote everything down in a book about his adventure. He hoped that one day, someone would read it. Maybe that reader will understand what Square saw in Spaceland.

## Level B2

Square lived in Flatland, a world with length and width but no height at all. Picture a great table top on which shapes slide about, never rising above it or sinking below it. That was his whole universe, and nobody there had ever imagined anything else. Its people were lines, triangles, squares and figures with many sides. Because their eyes lay in the same flat plane, every person looked to them like a simple straight line. To know a stranger, they had to feel the corners of his shape with care. Square himself was a quiet, sensible man who loved mathematics and trusted only what he could prove.

On the last night of the year nineteen ninety-nine, Square fell asleep and had a strange dream. He found himself looking at Lineland, a world that consisted of a single straight line. Its people were short lines and points, crowded together in a row. None of them could ever pass another, so they spent their whole lives beside the same companions. They knew each other only by their voices, since none of them could see more than a single point. In the middle stood the king, the longest line in his kingdom, who was very proud of his tiny realm.

When Square spoke to him, the king was amazed to hear a voice from nowhere. Square tried to explain that his own world stretched out to the side, in a direction the king had never imagined. "There is no side," said the king firmly. "There is only forward and back." To prove his point, Square passed slowly through the line. The king saw nothing but a short line appearing out of nowhere and then vanishing again. "You are a liar and a trickster," shouted the king. "You are blind to everything outside your own line," replied Square, losing his temper. The king called his people, and as they moved toward Square in a fury, the dream ended.

The next day, Square gave his young grandson a lesson in geometry. He explained that a point moving three inches makes a line of three inches. Then the line, moving sideways three inches, makes a square of nine. The boy listened carefully and suddenly had an idea of his own. "Then a square moving upward must make a shape of twenty-seven," he said. Square told him sharply that "upward" meant nothing and sent him off to bed.

That evening, alone in his room, Square kept thinking about the boy's foolish words. Suddenly, a voice spoke right beside him, although the door was shut and nobody had entered. In front of him, a point appeared and slowly grew into a circle. "I am Sphere," said the visitor, "and I have come from Spaceland, where there are three dimensions." Square was not impressed in the least. "You are simply a circle who has found a clever way into my house," he said.

Sphere tried patiently to explain. He said that he was made of countless circles, one on top of another. Square, he went on, could only ever see one slice of him at a time. To show this, Sphere rose slowly out of Flatland. Square watched the circle shrink to a point and vanish completely. When Sphere came back, he even touched Square from the inside, which no one in Flatland could ever do. Square felt the touch but still refused to believe. At last Sphere decided that words were useless.

With a great effort, Sphere lifted Square out of Flatland. For a moment, Square felt terribly dizzy and saw nothing at all. Then a new world opened before his eyes. Below him lay his city, and he could see inside every house and every closed chest. He could even see his own family moving through the rooms, unaware of him. Beside him was Sphere, no longer a circle but a perfect, solid ball. "I see it now," whispered Square. "This is the third dimension, and it is beautiful."

Sphere showed him other wonders of Spaceland, including a cube, a solid shape with six square faces. Square understood at once that the cube was what his grandson had imagined. His mind, now wide awake, began to race ahead. "If a square moves upward to make a cube, a cube must move to make something greater still," he said. "Surely there is a fourth dimension, above even your world." Sphere was shocked and annoyed by the idea.

To calm him down, Sphere showed him Pointland, the lowest world of all. There a single point lived alone, with no length, width or height. It believed that it was the only thing in existence and talked happily to itself. When Square tried to speak to it, the point thought it was hearing its own clever thoughts. "You see how foolish it is to be content in a small world," said Sphere. "Then do not stop me from seeking a bigger one," replied Square. He went on begging to see the fourth dimension, until Sphere finally lost patience. In a moment, Square was falling, and then he was back in Flatland.

Square woke up in his own room and remembered everything. He tried to teach his grandson about the third dimension, but the boy no longer cared about his own idea. Then Square began speaking about Spaceland to anyone who would listen. People laughed at him, and some said that he had lost his mind.

The Council of Flatland, however, did not laugh. Its leaders knew about the visits from Spaceland, and they had a secret law against anyone who spoke of them. Square was arrested and sent to prison.

Seven years have passed since then, and he still has no one to share his secret with. Even his own memories of Spaceland sometimes seem dim and uncertain. Still, he writes them down, hoping that his pages will someday find a reader who understands.

## Level C1

Square lived in Flatland, a world that possessed length and breadth but not the faintest trace of height. Imagine an enormous table top across which shapes glide about, never rising above its surface and never sinking beneath it, and you have pictured his entire universe. Its inhabitants were lines, triangles, squares and figures with ever more sides, all moving freely to the north, south, east and west, yet never once up or down. Since their eyes lay in the very plane they lived in, every neighbor appeared to them as nothing more than a straight line, brighter or dimmer according to its distance. To be certain of a stranger's shape, a Flatlander had to feel his corners, a custom that every child learned long before learning to count. Square himself was a sober, careful man, fond of mathematics and deeply suspicious of anything he could not prove.

On the last night of the year nineteen ninety-nine, he fell asleep and found himself, in a dream, gazing at a world even poorer than his own. This was Lineland, a realm consisting of a single straight line, along which its people were strung like beads on a thread. Short lines and tiny points lived there side by side, and since none of them could ever pass another, each spent a whole lifetime beside the same two companions. Seeing nothing but a single point in front of them, they recognized one another by voice alone. At the center of this narrow kingdom stood its monarch, the longest line of all, utterly convinced that his line was the whole of creation.

Square greeted the king, who was astonished to hear a voice that seemed to come from inside his own body. Patiently, Square explained that his world stretched out sideways, in a direction the king had never so much as imagined. "There is no sideways," declared the king. "There is only forward and back, and anyone who says otherwise is mad." Hoping that a demonstration might succeed where words had failed, Square passed slowly through the line. To the king, all that happened was that a short line appeared from nowhere, lingered for a moment and then vanished. "A cheap trick," he sneered, "performed by a liar who wishes to unsettle my people." Losing his temper, Square told him that he was blind to everything beyond his own little line. The king summoned his subjects, who began to advance on the stranger in a fury, and at that moment the dream dissolved.

The following day, Square gave his young grandson a lesson in geometry. A point moving three inches, he explained, produces a line of three inches, and that line, carried three inches sideways, produces a square of nine. The boy, who was quick for his age, considered this in silence before offering a thought of his own. "Then a square moving upward ought to make a shape of twenty-seven," he said. Square, irritated rather than impressed, informed him that "upward" was a word without meaning and sent him to bed.

That evening, sitting alone, Square found that he could not shake off the child's absurd suggestion. All at once a voice spoke close beside him, though the door was locked and no one had come in. Before his eyes a point appeared, swelling into a small circle and then into a large one. "I am Sphere," the visitor announced, "a native of Spaceland, and once in every thousand years I visit your country in search of a willing mind." Square, for his part, saw only a circle that had found some clever way into a locked room, and he said so.

Sphere set about explaining himself with remarkable patience. He was, he said, built of countless circles laid one upon another, and what Square saw was merely the slice where he met the plane of Flatland. To prove it, he rose slowly upward, and Square watched in confusion as the circle shrank to a point and disappeared altogether. Returning, Sphere even touched him from within, something no Flatlander could possibly have done. Square felt the touch distinctly, yet he clung to his old certainties all the more stubbornly. Realizing at last that argument was hopeless, Sphere resolved to show rather than to tell.

With a tremendous effort, he lifted Square clean out of the plane. There followed a moment of darkness and dizziness so intense that Square believed he had lost his senses. Then his eyes opened upon Spaceland, and nothing in his life had prepared him for the sight. Spread out below him lay his own city, every house open to his gaze, every locked cabinet and closed chest revealing what it held. He could even watch his own family moving from room to room, quite unaware of the eye that observed them from above. Beside him floated Sphere, no longer a circle at all but a solid, perfectly rounded body whose shape he could at last perceive. "I understand," Square whispered, overwhelmed. "This is the third dimension, and it is more beautiful than anything I have ever known."

Sphere guided him through further wonders of Spaceland, among them a cube, a solid figure bounded by six square faces. Instantly Square recognized it as the very shape his grandson had dreamed of, and his mind, once so cautious, began to race. "If a square moving upward makes a cube," he reasoned aloud, "then surely a cube, moving in some further direction, must make something greater still." "Show me that fourth dimension," he begged his teacher, "for it must lie somewhere beyond even your world." Sphere, who had spent centuries trying to make others see a little further, was now dismayed to find a pupil who wished to see too far.

By way of a lesson, Sphere carried him down to Pointland, the humblest world of all, a place without length, breadth or height. There a single point lived in perfect solitude, chattering contentedly to itself and wholly persuaded that it was everything that existed. When Square called out to it, the point took his words for its own brilliant thoughts and praised itself all the more. "Observe how happy ignorance can be," said Sphere, "and how little it wishes to be disturbed." "Then do not let me become like that creature," Square replied, and he pleaded once again to be shown the fourth dimension. His teacher's patience finally ran out, and in an instant Square was plunging downward, back into the flat world he had come from.

He awoke in his own room with every detail still sharp in his memory. His first thought was to share the good news, beginning with his grandson, but the boy had lost all interest in his own idea and only stared at him blankly. Undeterred, Square began speaking of Spaceland to anyone who would listen, and soon his neighbors were whispering that his mind had given way.

It was the Council of Flatland, however, that took him most seriously. Its leaders had long known of the visits from above, and they had quietly passed a law against anyone who spread such dangerous ideas. Square was arrested, tried and sent to prison, where he has remained ever since.

Seven years have passed, and he has found no one in all that time who will believe him. Even his own memories of Spaceland have begun to fade, so that on some days he can hardly picture the shape of a cube. Yet he goes on writing, in the stubborn hope that his pages may one day reach a reader whose mind is open enough to look upward.
