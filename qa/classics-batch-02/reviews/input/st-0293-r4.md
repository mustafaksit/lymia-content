# The Adventure of the Norwood Builder

Summary: A frightened young lawyer asks Sherlock Holmes for help when an old builder suddenly disappears.

## Level A1

Sherlock Holmes and Watson sat at Baker Street. Suddenly, a young man ran into the room. His name was McFarlane, and he looked afraid. "Please help me, Holmes!" he cried. "The police want me."

Just then, Lestrade came in. He was a police inspector. "Tell your story first," Holmes said to McFarlane.

"Yesterday, an old builder visited me," said McFarlane. "His name was Oldacre. He lived in Norwood. He knew my mother long ago. He gave me some papers about his money. He left all his money to me!"

"In the evening, I went to his house. We looked at papers until late. I forgot my walking stick there. Then I slept at a hotel near Norwood. This morning, I read the newspaper. There was a fire at Oldacre's wood yard. Now the old man is gone. The police think I hurt him!"

"Come with me, McFarlane," said Lestrade. "We found your stick in Oldacre's room." Lestrade took McFarlane away. Holmes went to Norwood and looked at everything. He found nothing new. That night, Holmes was not happy.

The next morning, Lestrade was very happy. "Come and see this!" said Lestrade. There was a thumbprint on the wall. It was McFarlane's thumbprint. Holmes looked at it carefully. "It was not here yesterday," Holmes told Watson.

Holmes measured the halls. The upstairs hall was shorter than downstairs. Holmes smiled and asked for some straw. He put it in the upstairs hall. He lit it, and smoke went up. "Fire! Fire!" everyone shouted.

Suddenly, a door opened in the wall. A little old man ran out. It was Jonas Oldacre, and he was fine! "It was a joke," said Oldacre.

"Oldacre hid in a secret room," said Holmes. "That night, McFarlane touched wax on some papers. His thumbprint stayed in the wax. Oldacre used it on the wall later. Long ago, McFarlane's mother did not marry him. So he wanted to hurt her son." Lestrade took Oldacre away, and McFarlane was free.

## Level A2

One morning, Holmes and Watson sat in their rooms at Baker Street. Suddenly, the door flew open, and a young man rushed in. "My name is Hector McFarlane," he said. "Please help me, Holmes. The police want me for a terrible crime."

Just then, Inspector Lestrade walked in. "I am here for Hector McFarlane," said Lestrade. "Let him tell his story first," said Holmes.

"Yesterday, an old builder came to my office in London. His name was Jonas Oldacre, and he lived in Norwood. I did not know him, but he knew my parents long ago. He showed me some untidy papers about his money. In the papers, he left all his money to me! He asked me to come to his house that evening."

"I arrived at his house at nine o'clock. His housekeeper gave us supper. Then we looked at old papers until late at night. At last, Oldacre let me out into the garden. I could not find my walking stick. Oldacre promised to keep it for me. It was too late to go home. So I slept at a small hotel near Norwood."

"This morning, I read the newspaper on the train. There was a big fire at Oldacre's wood yard in the night. The old man is missing, and his safe is open. The police found my stick in his room. They also found burnt bones in the ashes. Now they think Oldacre is dead, and they blame me!"

Lestrade took McFarlane away to the police station. Holmes studied the old man's untidy papers. "Oldacre wrote these on a train," he told Watson. "Some words are neat, and some words are shaky. He did not really care about these papers."

First, Holmes visited McFarlane's mother at her home in Blackheath. "Long ago, I almost married Jonas Oldacre," she said. "But he was a cruel man, so I left him." Then Holmes went to Norwood and looked at the house all afternoon. He found nothing new, and the housekeeper told him nothing.

Early the next morning, Holmes and Watson hurried back to Norwood. Lestrade showed them a thumbprint on the wall near the front door. "It is McFarlane's thumbprint," he said happily. "This mark was not here yesterday," Holmes whispered to Watson.

Holmes went upstairs and measured the long hall. The hall upstairs was shorter than the hall downstairs. Holmes asked for some straw and three policemen. He put the straw at the end of the upstairs hall. Then he lit it, and gray smoke rose up. "Fire! Fire!" they all cried. Suddenly, a hidden door opened in the wall. A thin old man ran out of a secret room. It was Jonas Oldacre, alive and well! "It was only a joke," said the old man.

"Oldacre hid there, and his housekeeper helped him," said Holmes. "The bones in the fire came from an animal. That evening, McFarlane pressed his thumb into wax on a letter. Oldacre kept the wax and copied the mark on the wall. He wanted to hurt McFarlane's mother through her son." That day, McFarlane went home a free man.

## Level B1

It was a quiet morning at Baker Street when the door suddenly flew open. A young man rushed into the room with a newspaper in his shaking hand. "I am Hector McFarlane," he cried. "I am the unluckiest man in London, and the police are looking for me!"

Before McFarlane could explain, they heard heavy steps on the stairs. Inspector Lestrade of the London police came in with two policemen. "Hector McFarlane, I am here to arrest you," said Lestrade. "Let him tell us his story first," said Holmes.

"Yesterday afternoon, a stranger came to my office in London," McFarlane began. "He was an old builder from Norwood, and his name was Jonas Oldacre. He knew my parents many years ago. He had no family, he said, and he wanted to leave everything to me. He gave me his will in untidy handwriting and asked me to copy it neatly. He also invited me to his house that evening. But he asked me to say nothing to my parents yet."

"I sent a telegram home and told my parents I was going to be late. At nine o'clock I reached his house, and his housekeeper served us supper. Afterwards, we worked in his study, where a heavy safe stood in the corner. It was nearly midnight when he let me out through a long window. My walking stick was missing, but he promised to keep it for me. It was too late to go home to Blackheath. So I spent the night at an inn near Norwood."

"This morning, I saw his name in the newspaper on the train. There was a big fire in his wood yard last night. Oldacre is missing, his safe is open, and his papers are all over the floor. The police found my stick in his study and some burnt bones in the ashes. They believe that the old man is dead and that I am guilty!"

Before he left with his prisoner, Lestrade explained his idea. "The young man learned about the money," he said. "So he went back that same night and did his wicked deed."

"Why did he choose the one night when everyone knew about his visit?" Holmes asked. "And why did he leave his own stick behind?" Then Holmes studied the will carefully. "Some parts are clear, and some are very shaky," he told Watson. "Oldacre wrote it on a train. So the will did not really matter much to him." He spent the rest of that day on the case. First he visited McFarlane's mother, a small woman with bright blue eyes, in Blackheath. "Many years ago, I was going to marry Jonas Oldacre," she said. "Then I learned what a cruel man he was, and I married someone kinder."

Next, Holmes went to Norwood, but he found no useful clue there. The housekeeper said that she heard nothing during the night. The only strange thing was Oldacre's bank book. It showed large payments to a man nobody knew. "Oldacre was not as rich as people believed," Holmes told Watson that evening. "Still, I fear that this time Lestrade has all the facts on his side."

Early the next morning, a telegram arrived from Lestrade. Holmes and Watson hurried to Norwood, where Lestrade was waiting with a proud smile. He showed them a clear thumbprint on the white wall of the front hall. "We took McFarlane's thumbprint in wax this morning," he said. "It is exactly the same." Holmes bent over the mark in silence. When Lestrade turned away, Holmes whispered to Watson. "I examined this wall yesterday, and there was nothing on it."

Holmes went outside and walked around the house. Then he went upstairs and walked along the top hall, counting his steps. "Lestrade, please bring me three policemen and some straw," he said. Holmes piled the straw at the end of the upstairs hall and lit it. As gray smoke rose up, he asked everyone to shout "Fire!" together. At once, a hidden door in the wall flew open. A little old man with sharp eyes ran out of a secret room. It was Jonas Oldacre, alive and unhurt. "It was only a joke," said the old man.

"It was no joke," said Holmes. "The upstairs hall was shorter than the hall below. So there had to be a secret room. Oldacre hid there with food and a bed, and his housekeeper helped him. That evening, McFarlane sealed some papers with wax and pressed his thumb into it. Oldacre kept the wax and used it to make the mark on the wall. The bones in the ashes came from an animal. He wanted revenge on the woman who refused to marry him. He also wanted to escape his debts. The stranger in the bank book was Oldacre himself, with a new name." McFarlane was free that day, and Lestrade took the old builder away instead.

## Level B2

Holmes was complaining that London had become dull when the doorbell rang wildly and quick feet raced up the stairs. A moment later, a pale young man burst into the room with a crumpled newspaper in his hand. "Forgive me," he gasped. "My name is Hector McFarlane, and I am the unhappiest man in London." Holmes waved him into a chair. "You are an unmarried lawyer with a weak chest, but otherwise I know nothing about you," he said calmly.

"The police are following me," said McFarlane. "They believe Jonas Oldacre of Norwood is dead, and they say I am to blame." Just then, Inspector Lestrade appeared in the doorway with two policemen. "Hector McFarlane, I arrest you in connection with the disappearance of Jonas Oldacre," Lestrade announced. At Holmes's request, however, he agreed to wait while the young man told his story.

"Until yesterday, I had never heard of Jonas Oldacre," McFarlane began. "At three o'clock he walked into my office in the city and introduced himself as a builder. Then he laid some untidy pages on my desk and asked me to turn them into a proper will. To my amazement, he was leaving everything he owned to me. He explained that he had no family of his own and had known my parents in his youth."

"He asked me to come to Norwood that evening to sort out some other papers. He also begged me to say nothing to my parents until everything was settled, because he wanted to surprise them. I sent a telegram home to say I would be late and reached his house at nine. His housekeeper served us supper, and then we went into his study, where a heavy safe stood in the corner. We went through old documents until nearly midnight. Then he let me out through the long glass window that opened onto the garden. I could not find my walking stick, but he told me he would keep it safely until I returned. It was too late to get back to Blackheath, so I spent the night at an inn near Norwood."

"On the train this morning, I read about a strange night in Norwood. Shortly after midnight, a fire had broken out in Oldacre's wood yard. When the flames were finally put out, the old man was nowhere to be found. His bed had not been slept in, his safe was open, and papers were scattered across his study. My walking stick was lying in the room, and some burnt bones were discovered among the ashes. The police have decided that Oldacre is dead and that I am responsible."

The two policemen led McFarlane downstairs, but Lestrade stayed behind for a moment. "It's all perfectly clear," he said. "The young man learns he will be rich when the old man is gone. So he goes back to Norwood and does the wicked deed that very night." "On the very night when everyone knows he is in the house?" asked Holmes. "And then he kindly leaves his own stick behind for the police?" Holmes suggested that a passing tramp might have seen the open safe through the window, but Lestrade only laughed and left.

Holmes picked up the untidy pages McFarlane had left on the table. "Look, Watson," he said. "Some lines are clear, some are almost impossible to read, and a few are very shaky. This was written on a train: the neat parts at stations, the messy ones while the train was moving. A man who writes his will so carelessly cannot think that it matters very much."

Holmes spent the rest of that day on the case and came home tired and gloomy in the evening. He had first visited McFarlane's parents in Blackheath. The mother, a small woman with fierce blue eyes, refused to believe a word against her son. "I was once engaged to Jonas Oldacre," she admitted. "Thank heaven I discovered how cruel and spiteful he was before the wedding." In Norwood, Holmes found nothing new, and the housekeeper insisted that she had slept through the whole night. The only odd thing was Oldacre's bank book, which showed large payments to a man nobody had ever heard of. "Oldacre was not nearly as rich as people thought," said Holmes. "Still, Watson, for once the facts are all on Lestrade's side."

Early next morning a telegram arrived from Norwood. It said McFarlane's guilt was now certain. When Holmes and Watson reached the house, Lestrade led them into the front hall with a broad smile. On the plain white wall was a clear thumbprint. "We took an impression of the young man's thumb in wax this morning, and the two are identical," Lestrade said proudly. Holmes bent over the mark and studied it in silence. Once the inspector had gone, Holmes turned to Watson with shining eyes. "I examined this very wall yesterday, and there was no mark on it," he whispered.

Holmes then walked around the house, taking careful measurements. Next, he climbed to the top floor and paced the length of the upstairs corridor. After that, he asked Lestrade for three of his policemen and a large bundle of straw. At the end of the corridor, Holmes made a pile of straw and set fire to it. As thick smoke drifted along the passage, he asked everyone to shout "Fire!" as loudly as they could. The cry had hardly left their lips when a hidden door in the end wall flew open. Out ran a small, thin old man with a sharp face, straight into the arms of the police. It was Jonas Oldacre, very much alive.

"What have you been doing all this time?" shouted Lestrade, red with anger. "It was only a little joke, my dear sir," the old man muttered with an uneasy laugh. "Joke or not, it nearly sent an innocent man to prison," said Holmes. "The upstairs corridor is six feet shorter than the one below it, so there had to be a hiding place. Oldacre built it himself, and his housekeeper brought him food. On the night of the visit, McFarlane sealed a packet of papers with wax and pressed his thumb into it. Oldacre kept that wax and used it to put the print on the wall last night. As for the bones in the ashes, they came from an animal. His aim was to punish the woman who had rejected him by ruining her son. He also planned to escape his debts and start again under a new name, the one in his bank book."

"You have saved me from a terrible mistake," Lestrade admitted. Holmes only smiled and told him to keep the credit for himself. That evening, McFarlane was a free man, and Jonas Oldacre was the one in a police cell.

## Level C1

Sherlock Holmes had just been grumbling to Watson that London had grown sadly uneventful when the doorbell rang with frantic violence and hurried feet came pounding up the stairs. A moment later the door was flung open, and a pale young man with frightened eyes stumbled into the room, clutching a crumpled newspaper. "You must forgive me," he gasped, looking from one of them to the other. "My name is Hector McFarlane, and at this moment I believe I am the most unfortunate man in London." Holmes, entirely unmoved, waved him into a chair and let his gaze travel over the visitor. "Beyond the obvious facts that you are a bachelor, a lawyer, and a sufferer from a weak chest, I know nothing whatever about you," he remarked.

The young man looked startled, but his own troubles left him no room for curiosity. "If you have seen the morning paper, you will know why I have come," he said. "The police believe that Jonas Oldacre of Norwood is dead, and they are convinced that I am the man responsible." He had barely finished speaking when a heavy tread sounded on the stairs, and Inspector Lestrade, flanked by two uniformed policemen, appeared in the doorway. "Hector McFarlane, I am arresting you in connection with the disappearance of Jonas Oldacre," he declared. Only at Holmes's request did the inspector consent to wait while the prisoner told his story, though he made it plain that he expected to learn nothing new.

"Until yesterday afternoon, the name of Jonas Oldacre meant nothing to me," McFarlane began. "At about three o'clock an elderly gentleman walked into my office in the city and introduced himself as a builder from Norwood. He laid a few untidy sheets of paper on my desk and asked me to draw them up as a proper will. Glancing through them, I saw to my utter astonishment that he intended to leave everything he possessed to me. He explained that he was a lonely bachelor with no relations worth mentioning, that he had known my parents long ago, and that he had heard nothing but good of me."

"He then asked me to come out to Norwood that same evening to settle some other papers. He also begged me to keep the matter from my parents until it was complete, since he wished it to be a pleasant surprise for them. I sent a telegram home to say that I should be late, and at nine o'clock I was at his door. His housekeeper gave us a light supper, after which he took me into his study, where a heavy safe stood in the corner. We sat over leases and deeds until close on midnight, and then he let me out through the long window that opened onto the lawn. My walking stick had vanished somewhere, but he assured me that he would look after it until my next visit. Since the hour was too late for me to return to Blackheath, I took a room at an inn close by."

"It was only this morning, glancing at the newspaper on my way into town, that I learned what had followed. Not long after midnight a fire had broken out in the wood yard behind Oldacre's house, and when it was finally put out, the owner was nowhere to be found. His bed had not been slept in, his safe stood open with papers strewn about the study, and my own stick lay on the floor. Among the ashes of the woodpile, the firemen had come upon some burnt bones. You may imagine what conclusion the police drew from all this."

When the two policemen had led their prisoner down the stairs, Lestrade lingered long enough to explain, with evident satisfaction, how simple the whole affair was. "Here is a young man who discovers that an old fellow's fortune will come to him the moment the old fellow is gone," he said. "What does he do but slip back the same night and carry out his wicked deed?" "On the one night when the housekeeper has seen him in the house, and leaving his own stick behind as a souvenir for the police?" Holmes asked mildly. He went on to suggest that a passing tramp, glimpsing the open safe through the window, might account for everything just as well. The inspector merely laughed and took his leave.

Holmes turned his attention instead to the rough draft that McFarlane had left behind, holding the pages up to the window and frowning over the uneven script. "Observe, Watson, how the writing changes from one line to the next," he said. "Here it is perfectly firm, there it is barely legible, and in places it wanders all over the page. This document was composed in a railway carriage, the clear lines at stations and the rest while the train was in motion. A man who disposes of his entire fortune on a suburban train journey can hardly regard the matter as serious."

The rest of that day Holmes devoted entirely to the case, and he returned to Baker Street in the evening looking more dispirited than Watson had seen him for a long time. He had begun in Blackheath, where McFarlane's mother, a small, fierce woman with brilliant blue eyes, would hear not a single word against her son. "I was engaged to Jonas Oldacre once," she told him, "and thank heaven I found out in time what a cruel and spiteful creature he was." Norwood had proved no more rewarding, for the house yielded nothing in the prisoner's favor. The housekeeper, a silent and sullen woman, claimed to have slept soundly through the night. The only curiosity was the old man's bank book, which recorded large sums paid over recent months to a gentleman whom nobody in the neighborhood had ever heard of. "Oldacre was a great deal poorer than the world supposed," Holmes said, sinking into his armchair. "Yet I must admit, Watson, that for once every fact in this business is on Lestrade's side."

Early next morning a telegram arrived from Norwood, informing Holmes that important fresh evidence had come to light and that the young man's guilt was now beyond question. At the house, Lestrade, glowing with triumph, led them into the front hall and pointed to the bare white wall beside the door. There, standing out with perfect clearness, was a single thumbprint. "We took an impression of McFarlane's thumb in wax this morning," he said, "and you may compare the two for yourself, since they are identical." Holmes bent over the mark in a silence that Lestrade plainly mistook for defeat. Yet the moment the inspector left the hall, Holmes turned to Watson with eyes that danced. "I examined that very wall yesterday with the greatest care," he murmured, "and there was nothing on it then."

For the next hour Holmes was as busy as a terrier, measuring the outside of the house with a tape. Then he climbed to the top floor and paced slowly along the corridor there. At last, he came down and, to the inspector's open irritation, requested three constables and a generous bundle of straw. The straw was heaped at the far end of the upstairs passage and set alight. As gray smoke began to curl along the ceiling, Holmes asked the whole party to raise the cry of "Fire!" all together. Scarcely had the shout faded when a section of the end wall swung outward, and a small, thin old man darted out of a hidden chamber. He ran straight into the waiting hands of the police. Jonas Oldacre, whose supposed end had caused such a stir, was very much alive.

"What on earth have you been doing all this time?" thundered Lestrade, his face crimson. The old man gave an uneasy, crooked little smile and insisted that it had all been nothing more than a practical joke. "A joke that came within a hair's breadth of destroying an innocent man," said Holmes coldly. "The upper corridor is six feet shorter than the one beneath it, and a builder who wants a hiding place is well placed to make one. His housekeeper, I imagine, kept him supplied with food. On the night of the visit, McFarlane sealed one of the packets of papers with wax and pressed his thumb into it. Our friend here kept that seal and used it to put the print on the wall during the night. As for the bones among the ashes, they belonged to some unlucky animal and to nobody else."

"His motive was a double one," Holmes went on. "He meant to take revenge on the woman who had refused him long ago by ruining her only son. At the same time, he hoped to slip away from his debts and begin afresh under the very name that appears in his bank book." Lestrade, who had listened in growing astonishment, admitted that Holmes had saved him from a disastrous blunder. Holmes, with a careless wave of the hand, told him that the newspapers were welcome to give the credit to the inspector. By evening Hector McFarlane was on his way home to Blackheath, and the Norwood builder was the one sitting in a police cell.
