# The Count of Monte Cristo: The Escape

Summary: A young sailor, locked away for something he did not do, meets a wise old prisoner.

## Level A1

Edmond Dantes was a young sailor. He worked on a ship, the Pharaon. Mr Morrel owned the ship. "You can be our captain," said Mr Morrel. He loved a kind girl, Mercedes. Soon they planned a wedding.

But two men did not like Edmond. Danglars wanted to be the captain. Fernand loved Mercedes too. They wrote a bad letter about Edmond. At the wedding party, soldiers came. They took Edmond away.

Villefort read the bad letter. But Edmond had another letter for Paris. It was for Villefort's father! Villefort was afraid for himself. He burned the letter. Then he sent Edmond to prison.

The prison was on an island. Edmond sat alone in a dark room. Years went by, and he was sad. One night, he heard a sound. Then a stone moved in the wall. An old man came out of a hole! His name was the Abbe Faria. "I am a prisoner too," said Faria.

Faria dug a tunnel for years. But the tunnel came to Edmond's room. The two men became good friends. He taught Edmond history and languages. He also helped Edmond understand the lie.

Then Faria became very ill. One night, he spoke softly to Edmond. "I have a secret, my son. There is a treasure on Monte Cristo. It is a small island. Now the treasure is yours." Later, Faria died quietly in bed.

The guards put Faria in a sack. Edmond had a brave idea. He carried his friend to his bed. He got into the sack with a knife. At night, the guards came back. They threw the sack into the sea!

Edmond cut the sack with his knife. He swam for a long time. In the morning, a ship found him. After fourteen years, Edmond was free!

Later, Edmond went to Monte Cristo. He looked under the rocks. He found a box of gold and jewels. "Faria was right!" said Edmond. He thanked his old friend in his heart.

## Level A2

In the spring of eighteen fifteen, a ship came home to Marseille. Its name was the Pharaon. The captain died at sea, so Edmond Dantes brought it home. Mr Morrel owned the ship, and he liked Edmond very much. "You are young, but you can be our next captain," he said. Edmond ran home to his old father. Then he went to Mercedes, and they planned their wedding.

But not everyone was happy for Edmond. Danglars worked on the Pharaon too, and he wanted to be captain. Fernand loved Mercedes, but she loved only Edmond. The two men wrote a secret letter to the king's lawyer. In it, they called Edmond a dangerous enemy of the king.

On the day of the wedding party, soldiers came through the door. They took Edmond away in front of everyone. Mercedes cried, and his old father could not speak.

A young lawyer, Villefort, asked Edmond many questions. Edmond answered honestly, and Villefort almost let him go. But Edmond had another letter in his pocket. The dying captain gave it to him for a man in Paris. It was for Villefort's own father! Villefort was afraid for his future. He burned the letter in the fire. Then he sent Edmond to a dark prison on a rocky island.

Edmond waited and waited, but nobody came for him. Years went by in a small, cold room. One night, he heard a soft scratching sound inside the wall. Days later, a stone moved, and an old man looked out. The old man was a priest, the Abbe Faria. "I went the wrong way with my tunnel," he said.

Faria and Edmond became close friends. The old priest taught him history, science and many languages. He also helped Edmond think about the bad letter. "Danglars and Fernand wrote it, and Villefort hid the truth," said Faria. At last, Edmond understood everything.

Then Faria became very ill, and he could not walk. Edmond stayed by his side and did not leave him. One night, Faria told him a great secret. "There is a treasure on the island of Monte Cristo. It is yours now, my son." Some days later, the old priest died quietly in his sleep.

The guards put Faria in a sack and left the room. Edmond had a brave plan. He carried his friend through the tunnel to his own bed. Then he took a small knife and got into the sack. At night, two guards carried the sack to the rocks. They threw it far out into the cold sea. Edmond cut the sack open and swam up to the air. In the morning, sailors on a small ship found him. After fourteen years in prison, Edmond Dantes was free.

Some months later, Edmond sailed to Monte Cristo with his new friends. He stayed on the island alone and looked under the rocks. He moved a big stone and found a cave. Inside was an old box full of gold and jewels. Edmond looked at the sea and thought of Faria. "Thank you, my friend," he said softly.

## Level B1

In the spring of eighteen fifteen, the ship Pharaon sailed into the port of Marseille. On the deck stood Edmond Dantes, a sailor of only nineteen. The captain died during the long voyage, so Edmond brought the ship home himself. Mr Morrel, who owned the Pharaon, was waiting for him at the port. "You did very well, and I want you as our next captain," said Mr Morrel. Edmond ran first to his old father, who lived alone in a small room. Then he hurried to Mercedes, the beautiful girl he loved. They were going to marry in a few days.

However, two men were watching Edmond with cold eyes. Danglars, who kept the money books on the ship, wanted the captain's job for himself. Fernand loved Mercedes, but she loved only Edmond. Danglars also knew something useful. During the voyage, Edmond stopped at a small island to help the dying captain. The old emperor of France was living there, far from his throne. On that island, someone gave Edmond a letter for a man in Paris. Danglars wrote a secret note to the king's lawyer, and Fernand took it to the post. The note said that Edmond was helping the emperor against the king.

On the day of the wedding party, everyone was eating and laughing. Suddenly there was a loud knock at the door. Soldiers came in and took Edmond away. Mercedes cried out, and his father turned white. Danglars smiled quietly, and Fernand looked at the floor.

The king's lawyer, Villefort, was a young and ambitious man. He asked Edmond many questions, and Edmond answered them all honestly. Villefort began to believe him and almost set him free. Then he read the name on the letter from the island. It was his own father's name! His father was a friend of the emperor, and this secret could destroy Villefort's career. He threw the letter into the fire and watched it burn. "Stay here for one more night, and then you can go home," Villefort told Edmond. That same night, soldiers took Edmond by boat to a prison on a rocky island.

Days became months, and months became years. Nobody answered Edmond's questions, and nobody came to visit him. He was slowly losing all hope. Then one night, he heard a quiet scratching sound inside the wall. Somebody was digging! Edmond started to dig from his side too. After many days, a stone fell, and an old man with a gray beard looked out. "I am the Abbe Faria," the old man said with a sad smile. Faria was a priest who was also a great scholar. He spent years on a tunnel to the outside. But he made a mistake and reached Edmond's room instead.

Over the next years, the two prisoners met every day in secret. Faria taught Edmond history, science, mathematics and several languages. He also helped him think carefully about the day of his arrest. "Who wanted your job, and who wanted your girl?" asked Faria. Slowly Edmond understood the truth about Danglars, Fernand and Villefort.

Then Faria fell seriously ill, and one side of his body stopped working. Edmond could escape alone now, but he refused to leave his friend. One evening, Faria showed him an old, half-burned paper. "This paper shows the way to a great treasure," he said. The treasure was on the island of Monte Cristo. At first, Edmond believed that his friend's mind was sick. But Faria explained everything, and Edmond began to believe him. "When you are free, the treasure will be yours," said Faria. Soon after, the old priest died quietly in his bed.

The guards put Faria into a sack and left it in his room until night. Then a brave idea came to him. He carried Faria through the tunnel and laid him in his own bed. After that, he took a small knife, climbed into the sack and waited. At night, two guards came and carried the sack outside. They swung it three times and threw it into the sea. Under the cold water, Edmond cut the sack open and swam up to the air. A storm was blowing, but he swam for hours to a small island. In the morning, the sailors of a small ship pulled him out of the water. He told them that he was a sailor from a lost boat. He was thirty-three now, after fourteen long years in prison.

Some months later, Edmond went to Monte Cristo and stayed there alone. He searched among the rocks for a long time. At last, he moved a large stone and found steps down into a cave. In the deepest part stood an old box. When he opened it, gold and jewels shone in the dark. "You were right, my dear friend," Edmond whispered. The poor sailor was gone, and in his place stood a rich, free man.

## Level B2

In the spring of eighteen fifteen, the merchant ship Pharaon sailed slowly into the harbor of Marseille. Its captain had died of a fever at sea, so the young mate, Edmond Dantes, had brought her safely home. Mr Morrel, the owner, was so pleased with the nineteen-year-old sailor that he promised him the captain's job. Edmond hurried home to embrace his elderly father and then went straight to the fishing village where Mercedes lived. They were to be married within days.

Not everyone shared his happiness, however. Danglars, who kept the ship's accounts, was full of jealousy and wanted the command for himself. Fernand, a young fisherman, had loved Mercedes for years, but she had always refused him. Meeting by chance near the harbor, the two men soon discovered that they shared a common enemy. On the dying captain's orders, Edmond had stopped on the way home at the island where the exiled emperor lived. There he had been given a letter to carry to a man in Paris. Danglars wrote an unsigned note with his left hand so that nobody would recognize the writing, and Fernand posted it. It accused Edmond of being a secret agent of the emperor.

On the day of the wedding, friends and neighbors gathered for a joyful meal before the ceremony. Just as the guests were rising to go to the town hall, a police officer and several soldiers appeared at the door. Edmond was arrested in front of everyone, while Mercedes cried out and his father reached for him in vain. Danglars watched with quiet satisfaction, and Fernand could not meet anyone's eyes.

The case went to Villefort, a cold and ambitious young lawyer who worked for the king. By chance, he was celebrating his own engagement that very day. As he questioned the prisoner, Villefort saw that Edmond was honest and probably innocent, and he prepared to release him. Then he asked to see the letter from the island and read the name of the man in Paris. It was his own father, a loyal friend of the emperor. If anyone discovered this connection, Villefort's career would be finished. He burned the letter in the candle flame and promised Edmond that he would soon be free. That same night, however, he had the young sailor rowed out to the fortress of If, a grim prison on a rock.

There, in a cell below the ground, Edmond waited for a trial that never came. He asked the guards again and again why he was there, but they only shrugged. The first year passed in anger, the second in prayer, and after that he sank into a deep, silent sadness. Then one night, lying awake, he heard a faint scraping sound inside the wall. Night after night it continued, and Edmond began to dig towards it with the handle of a broken jug.

At last, a stone gave way, and through the opening climbed a thin old man with a white beard. He was the Abbe Faria, a priest and scholar whom the guards believed to be mad. For years, he had been digging a tunnel towards the outer wall, but he had come out in his neighbor's cell instead. Faria laughed at his mistake, but for Edmond it was the best thing that had happened in many years.

The old man had made pens from fish bones, ink from soot, and paper from strips of his own shirts. Now he became Edmond's teacher, sharing with him history, mathematics, chemistry and several languages. Edmond learned quickly, and his mind grew sharp and strong. One day Faria asked him to describe every detail of the days before his arrest. Step by step, the old priest showed him the truth. Danglars had written the note, Fernand had posted it, and Villefort had hidden the facts to protect his father. Edmond sat in silence for a long time, understanding at last who his enemies were.

Some time later, Faria suffered a sudden attack of illness, and a few drops of his hidden medicine saved him. A second attack left him unable to use his right arm and leg. "Go without me," Faria urged him, but Edmond refused to abandon his friend. Touched by such loyalty, the old man revealed his great secret. He showed Edmond a half-burned page from an old book, which described the hiding place of a cardinal's treasure. That treasure, Faria said, lay on the small island of Monte Cristo. At first Edmond feared that his friend's mind had failed, but the more he listened, the more he believed. "When you are free, all of it will be yours," Faria told him. The third attack came one night, and the old priest died quietly before morning, with Edmond holding his hand.

The guards sewed Faria into a sack and left it on his bed until the evening. Alone in his cell, Edmond realized that the prison was offering him one single way out. He opened the sack, carried his friend through the tunnel and laid him in his own bed, facing the wall. Then he crawled back, took Faria's knife, climbed into the sack and sewed it closed from the inside. Late that night, two guards carried the heavy sack out into the wind and threw it from the top of the cliff. A weight on the sack pulled him down, but he cut himself free and swam up to the air.

A storm was coming, and the fortress stood black behind him. Edmond swam for hours until he reached a bare rocky island, where he spent the rest of the night. At dawn, a small sailing ship passed close by, and its crew pulled him from the water. He told them that his boat had been wrecked, and they gladly took him on as a sailor. When he first saw his face in a mirror, he hardly recognized the serious man of thirty-three who looked back. He had been a prisoner for fourteen years.

Some months later, Edmond persuaded his new companions to stop at Monte Cristo, a lonely island of rocks and wild goats. Pretending he had hurt himself in a fall, he stayed behind while the ship sailed away for a few days. Following Faria's directions, he found a stone that could be lifted, and beneath it a staircase leading down into a cave. In the deepest chamber he discovered an old chest bound in iron. When he raised the lid, gold coins, diamonds and pearls glittered in the faint light. Edmond fell to his knees and thanked heaven and his old friend. The poor sailor from the Pharaon had disappeared, and in his place stood a free man with a fortune beyond counting.

## Level C1

On a bright morning in the spring of eighteen fifteen, the Pharaon came sailing into the harbor of Marseille, watched by a small crowd on the quay. Something was wrong, for the ship moved with unusual caution. The figure giving orders on deck was not the captain at all, but a tall young man of nineteen. This was Edmond Dantes, the first mate, who explained to the ship's owner, Mr Morrel, that the captain had died of a fever during the voyage. Morrel, an honest merchant, listened to the young man's calm report and decided on the spot that the Pharaon would have a new captain. Edmond's first visit was to his old father, who had been living on very little in a narrow room at the top of a steep staircase. His second was to the fishing village beyond the town, where Mercedes, the girl he was about to marry, stood waiting for him at her window.

Happiness of that kind, however, has a way of attracting envy. Danglars, the ship's accountant, had never forgiven the young mate for rising above him. He could not bear the thought of taking orders from a man so much younger than himself. Fernand, a fisherman from the same village, had loved Mercedes for years and had been gently but firmly refused, since her heart had only ever belonged to Edmond. When the two men met by chance near the harbor, it took Danglars only a few minutes to see how useful Fernand's misery might be. On the dying captain's instructions, the ship had stopped at the small island where the fallen emperor lived in exile. There Edmond had been given a letter for Paris. Disguising his handwriting by writing with his left hand, Danglars composed an unsigned note informing the authorities that Edmond Dantes was a secret agent of the emperor. He then pretended to throw it away, knowing perfectly well that Fernand would pick it up and carry it to the post.

The wedding feast took place at an inn overlooking the sea, and the long table rang with laughter, songs and good wishes. Just as the guests were rising to accompany the young couple to the town hall, three heavy knocks sounded at the door, and a police officer entered to arrest the bridegroom. Mercedes held on to Edmond until the last moment, and his father stood as if turned to stone. Danglars observed the scene with secret pleasure, while Fernand slipped quietly away.

The man who would decide Edmond's fate was Villefort, the king's deputy prosecutor, who had been called away from his own engagement party to deal with the case. Cold and ambitious though he was, Villefort could see at once that the young sailor before him was honest, and he was on the point of releasing him. Only as a formality did he ask to see the letter Edmond had been carrying from the island. The name written on it drained the color from his face, for the man in Paris was none other than his own father, a known supporter of the emperor. Should that connection ever become known, his career would be over before it had properly begun. Villefort held the letter to the candle until nothing remained but ashes, then assured Edmond warmly that he would soon be home. That very night, the young sailor was rowed across the dark water to the fortress of If.

The fortress stood on a bare rock outside the harbor, and the men sent there were not expected to return. Edmond was locked in a cell below the level of the sea. There he waited for a trial that never came and asked questions that no one would answer. Through the first year he raged, through the second he prayed, and after that he sank into a silence so deep that even his guard stopped speaking to him. It was in this state, years into his imprisonment, that he heard one night a faint, regular scratching somewhere inside the wall.

Using the handle of a saucepan, Edmond began to dig from his own side, loosening the old mortar a little at a time. After many days, part of the floor gave way, and from the hole emerged a thin old man with a white beard and eyes that shone with intelligence. He introduced himself as the Abbe Faria, a priest and scholar. The guards considered him harmlessly mad because he kept offering the governor a fortune in exchange for his freedom. For years, he had been tunneling towards what he believed was the outer wall, only to discover, with a rueful laugh, that he had reached his neighbor's cell instead.

Faria had made pens from fish bones, ink from soot, and paper from strips of his own shirts, and on these he had written whole books from memory. In the hidden hours between the guard's visits, he taught Edmond history and mathematics, chemistry and philosophy, and the languages of half of Europe. One evening Faria asked him to describe, in the smallest detail, the days that had come before his arrest. Question by question, the old priest led him to a truth he had never allowed himself to suspect. Danglars had written the accusation, Fernand had delivered it, and Villefort had buried an innocent man to protect his own name.

Not long afterwards, Faria was seized by a sudden attack of the illness he had been expecting. Only a few drops of a hidden red medicine brought him back. A second attack left his right arm and leg useless, and with them any hope of escaping through the tunnel. "Go without me," he urged, but Edmond would not hear of leaving the man who had given him back his mind. Moved by such loyalty, the old priest at last shared the secret that had made the guards think him mad. He produced a half-burned page, discovered years earlier inside an old prayer book, on which a cardinal of long ago had written down a hiding place. There, on the small rocky island of Monte Cristo, the cardinal's family had buried a treasure that no one had ever found. Edmond's first fear was that his friend's illness had reached his mind, yet the more carefully Faria explained, the more impossible it became to doubt him. "When you are free, every coin of it belongs to you," the old man told him. The third attack came on a still night, and Edmond, holding his friend's hand, watched the old priest slip peacefully away before dawn.

In the morning, the guards sewed Faria into a rough sack and left it on his bed until nightfall. Sitting beside the sack, Edmond grasped that the prison was offering him a single, desperate door, and that he had the courage to walk through it. He opened the sack and carried his friend through the tunnel to his own cell. There he laid him in the bed, face turned towards the wall, and returned to take his place. With Faria's knife in his hand, he climbed into the sack and sewed it shut from inside, then lay perfectly still and waited. Towards midnight, two guards carried him out into the wind, complaining about the weight, and tied something heavy to his feet before climbing to the edge of the rocks. Edmond had imagined a grave of earth, and only when they swung him out over the darkness did he understand that the prison's only cemetery was the sea. The cold water closed over him and the weight dragged him down, but with the knife he freed himself and fought his way back up to the air.

He swam for hours until he reached a small, bare island, where he held on to the rocks and waited, shivering, for the day. At dawn a little sailing ship passed close by, and its crew, who earned their living by smuggling, pulled him aboard and believed his story of a shipwreck. When at last he saw his own reflection, he hardly recognized the serious man of thirty-three who looked back at him. He had entered the fortress of If at nineteen, and fourteen years had passed within its walls.

Months went by before he could persuade his new companions to stop at Monte Cristo, a lonely island of rocks, wild goats and silence. Pretending he had hurt himself badly in a fall, he begged them to leave him there and collect him a few days later. Alone at last, he followed Faria's directions across the stones until he found one that could be lifted, and beneath it a stairway descending into the rock. In the furthest chamber of the cave stood an old chest bound with iron. When he forced open the lid, gold coins, diamonds and pearls caught the faint light like fire. Edmond fell to his knees, not in greed but in gratitude, and spoke his old friend's name aloud. The young sailor from the Pharaon had been lost in the sea that night. The man who rose from his knees was free, and richer than any king.
