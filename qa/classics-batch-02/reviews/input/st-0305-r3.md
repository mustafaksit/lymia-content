# Black Beauty

Summary: A gentle black horse tells the story of his life with kind and unkind masters.

## Level A1

I am a horse. My name is Black Beauty. I am black, with one white foot. I have a white star on my face. My first home was a green farm. "Be good and gentle," said my mother.

Then Squire Gordon bought me. His home was Birtwick Park. John Manly took care of the horses. John had a young helper. Ginger was a tall red horse. Her first masters were cruel to her. So she was often cross. Merrylegs was a small, happy pony.

We went on a long trip. At night, we slept at an inn. There was smoke in the stable. I was afraid, and I did not move. Then the young helper covered my eyes. He led me out into the yard. He went back for Ginger too. "Good lad!" said my master. Later the young helper left. Joe Green came to Birtwick.

Later our mistress was very ill. The family went to a warm country. An earl bought Ginger and me. His home was Earlshall. His wife wanted our heads high. The groom used a tight bearing rein. It hurt our necks all day. Ginger fought against it.

A careless man rode me one night. I fell and hurt my knees. My knees got well, but they had marks. So the earl sold me.

Next I went to London. Jerry Barker drove a cab there. He was poor, but very kind. Jerry never worked on Sundays. We worked hard, but we were happy.

At a cab stand, I saw Ginger. She was thin and very tired. "My life is hard now," said Ginger. I never saw her again. I think her troubles are over.

Then Jerry got sick and left London. My next masters were hard. I grew weak. At last, three kind ladies bought me. Their groom looked at my white star. "This is Black Beauty!" he cried. It was Joe Green from Birtwick! Now I live in a quiet field. My troubles are over.

## Level A2

My name is Black Beauty, and I am a horse. I am black, with one white foot. I have a white star on my face. My first home was a quiet farm with a pond. "Always be good and kind," my mother told me. "Never kick or bite, even in play."

When I was four, Squire Gordon bought me. He lived at Birtwick Park with his family. John Manly, the coachman, was kind to every horse. My friends were Ginger, a tall red horse, and Merrylegs, a pony. "Why are you so cross?" I asked Ginger. "Men were cruel to me before," she said. "Here nobody hurts me, so I try to be calm."

Once, on a long journey, we stayed at an inn. In the night, I woke up and smelled smoke. I was very afraid, and I did not want to move. Then our young groom came in. He tied his scarf over my eyes. He led me out into the yard. Then he went back for Ginger. "You are a brave lad," said my master.

Later the young groom left, and Joe Green came. Joe was only a boy. One winter night, John woke me in a hurry. "Our mistress is very ill," he said. "Run for the doctor, Beauty, as fast as you can!" John rode me through the dark to the town. The doctor rode me back home. I was hot and wet. Joe gave me cold water and no warm blanket. That night I was very sick. John looked after me day and night, and I got better. Joe was very sorry, and he learned fast.

Then our mistress had to go to a warm country. Ginger and I went to a rich earl at Earlshall. His wife wanted her horses' heads very high. So the groom put a tight bearing rein on us. It hurt our necks and backs all day. Ginger fought against it, and she broke free.

One night a careless man rode me too fast. One of my shoes was loose. I fell on the stones and hurt my knees. My knees got better, but the marks stayed. So the earl sold me.

Later I came to London. My new master was Jerry Barker, a cab driver. Jerry was poor, but he was very kind. Jerry never worked on Sundays, because everyone needs rest.

At a cab stand, I saw a thin, tired horse. It was Ginger! "My masters work me very hard," she said quietly. "I am very, very tired." I never saw her again. I think her troubles are over now.

Then Jerry became ill and had to leave London. My next masters were hard, and I grew old and weak. At a horse fair, a kind farmer bought me. He let me rest. Then he sold me to three gentle ladies. Their young groom looked at my face and my white foot. "This is Black Beauty from Birtwick!" he cried. It was Joe Green, now a grown man! Now I live in a quiet field, and my troubles are over.

## Level B1

My name is Black Beauty, and this is the story of my life. I am a black horse with one white foot and a white star. I spent my early days on a pleasant farm with a clear pond. "Grow up gentle and good," my mother told me one day. "Never bite or kick, even in play."

When I was four, Squire Gordon bought me for his home at Birtwick Park. My new mistress gave me my name, because I was so black and handsome. The coachman was John Manly, who loved horses and understood them well. A cheerful young groom worked with him in the clean, peaceful stable.

In the next stalls lived Ginger, a tall chestnut mare, and Merrylegs, a little pony. Ginger, however, often laid back her ears and tried to bite. "The men who trained me were rough and cruel," she told me. "Nobody was ever kind to me, so I learned to fight." At Birtwick nobody treated her badly, and slowly she became calmer.

One stormy evening, John and my master were driving me home from town. The river was very high after the rain. When we came to the wooden bridge, I felt that something was wrong. I stopped and would not move, although John tried to make me go on. Just then a man ran out from the toll-house with a light. "Stop! Stop!" he shouted. "The middle of the bridge is gone!" My master thanked me again and again.

In the spring, we went on a long journey and stayed at an inn. Late at night, I woke up because the stable was full of smoke. Somebody was careless with a light in the hayloft above us. The horses were crying out, and I was too frightened to move. Then our young groom ran in and spoke to me calmly. He tied his scarf over my eyes and led me out into the yard. Then he ran back into the smoke and brought out Ginger. "You are a brave lad," said my master proudly.

Not long after that, the young groom left for a better job. His place went to Joe Green, a willing boy who was new to the work. One winter night, John woke me up suddenly. "Our mistress is very ill, Beauty," he said. "Run for the doctor as fast as you can." I galloped all the way to town with John on my back. The doctor's own horse was tired, so the doctor rode me home. When I reached Birtwick, I was hot and shaking. Joe gave me a bucket of cold water and no warm blanket. Soon I became very ill, and John nursed me for many days. Joe felt terrible about his mistake, and he learned to be careful.

Sadly, my mistress had to move to a warmer country. The family left Birtwick, and Merrylegs went to live with the vicar. An earl bought Ginger and me, and we went to Earlshall. His wife liked her horses to hold their heads very high. So the coachman put a tight bearing rein on us. It held our heads up for hours, and my neck and back ached. One day Ginger could not bear it any longer, and she kicked herself free. After that she never pulled the carriage again.

One night a careless groom rode me home fast over a stony road. One of my shoes was loose, but he did not notice. I stumbled and fell, and I hurt both my knees. They healed, but the marks stayed, and so the earl sold me.

Later I came to London and met Jerry Barker, who drove a cab. Jerry was poor, but he was honest, cheerful and kind. His wife and children loved me and often gave me apples. He never worked on Sundays, because he believed that everyone needed rest. The work was hard, but I was happy with him for three years.

One day, at a cab stand, I saw a worn-out chestnut horse. It was Ginger, and she knew me at once. "I am so tired, Beauty," she said. "My masters only want money from me, and I cannot fight any more." I never saw her again, and I believe her troubles are over now.

In the winter, Jerry became very ill, and he had to leave London. My next masters worked me hard, and I grew thin and weak. At a horse fair, a kind old farmer decided to help me. He gave me rest and good food until I was strong again. Then he sold me to three ladies who lived in a quiet country house. Their groom looked closely at my white star and my white foot. "Black Beauty, do you remember me?" he cried. It was Joe Green, now a grown man, and he hugged my neck. I will stay here for the rest of my life, and my troubles are over.

## Level B2

My name is Black Beauty, and this is the story of my life. I spent my first years on a pleasant farm, in a wide meadow with a clear pond and shady trees. I am black all over except for one white foot and a white star on my forehead. "You come from a good family, and you have never learned bad ways," my mother told me. "Grow up gentle and good, do your work willingly, and never bite or kick, even in play."

When I was four, I was sold to Squire Gordon, whose house stood in a large park called Birtwick. It was my new mistress who gave me my name, because she thought I looked so black and beautiful. The coachman, John Manly, was a quiet, steady man who understood horses better than anyone I have known. He was helped by a cheerful young groom, and together they kept the stable clean and peaceful.

In the next stalls lived Ginger, a tall chestnut mare, and Merrylegs, a fat little pony whom the children adored. Ginger laid back her ears and snapped at anyone who came near her. One evening, as we stood together in the paddock, she told me why. "No one was ever kind to me when I was young," she said bitterly. "My first masters used force instead of patience, so I learned to fight back." At Birtwick, however, nobody treated her harshly, and month by month she grew gentler.

One stormy evening in autumn, John and my master were driving me home from town. The rain had been falling for hours, and the river had risen high and fast. As we reached the wooden bridge, I felt that something was wrong, and I stopped still. John urged me forward, but I refused to move a single step. Just then the toll-keeper ran out of his little house, waving a lantern. "Stop! The middle of the bridge has been carried away by the flood!" he shouted. If I had gone on, we would all have fallen into the dark water. That night my master said that God had given animals a kind of knowledge that men often lacked.

The following spring, we made a long journey, and one night we rested at an inn. Shortly after midnight, I woke to a heavy, bitter smell, and the stable quickly filled with smoke. Somebody had been careless with a light in the hayloft, and the hay was on fire. The other horses were crying out, and I was so frightened that I could not move. Then our young groom came in, speaking to me as calmly as if nothing was the matter. He tied his scarf over my eyes, led me out into the yard, and hurried back for Ginger. A few moments later, part of the roof came crashing down behind them. "You have done a brave thing tonight, lad," said my master.

Soon afterwards the young groom left for a better position, and Joe Green, a boy of fourteen, took his place. One winter night, John woke me with a lantern in his hand. "Our mistress is very ill, Beauty," he said. "You must run for the doctor as fast as you can." I galloped all the way to the town with John on my back. The doctor's own horse was too tired, so the doctor rode me home himself. When we reached Birtwick, I was wet with sweat and trembling all over. John was still walking home, so young Joe was left to look after me. The poor boy meant well, but he gave me cold water and no warm blanket. Before morning, I was very ill, and for many days John nursed me with great care. Joe was deeply sorry, yet the mistake made him a careful and thoughtful groom.

Sadly, my mistress never grew strong again, and the doctor sent her to a warmer country. Merrylegs was given to the vicar, and Ginger and I were sold to an earl at Earlshall. The countess wanted her carriage horses to carry their heads high, whatever it cost them. So the coachman fastened tight bearing reins on us, which held our heads up hour after hour. My neck ached, and I could not lean into the collar to pull properly. One day, when the rein was shortened even more, Ginger reared and kicked until she was free. After that she was never put to the carriage again, but I had to bear the rein for four long months.

One night a careless groom rode me home far too fast over a road covered in sharp stones. One of my shoes was loose, but he did not notice it. At last, I stumbled and fell heavily onto my knees. My knees healed in time, but they were left with marks, and no fine family wants such a horse. So the earl sold me, and I never lived with Ginger again.

After passing through several hands, I came to London, where Jerry Barker bought me to pull his cab. Jerry was poor, but he was the most honest and cheerful man in the whole city. His wife and children treated me like one of the family. He refused to work on Sundays, because he believed that men and horses both needed rest. Although the work was hard, those were happy years.

One day at a cab stand, a worn-out chestnut horse was brought up beside me. Although her coat was dull and her eyes were sad, I knew her at once. "I am tired of this life, Beauty," said Ginger quietly. "Men have worked me as hard as they could, and I have no strength left to fight." I laid my nose against hers, but I could say nothing to comfort her. I never saw her again, and I believe her troubles are over now.

Then Jerry fell seriously ill after a bitter New Year's night of waiting in the cold. The doctor told him to leave London, and so we had to part. My next owners cared only for money, and under their heavy loads I grew thin and weak. One day my strength gave out completely, and I fell down in the street. A kind farmer bought me at a horse fair and fed me until I was strong again. In the spring he sold me to three ladies who lived quietly in the country. One morning their young groom suddenly stopped brushing me and stared at my forehead. "A white star and one white foot," he murmured. "Why, this must be Black Beauty from Birtwick!" It was Joe Green, grown into a man, and he could hardly speak for joy. I have lived here a long time now, and my troubles are all over.

## Level C1

My name is Black Beauty, and what follows is the story of my life. I spent my earliest days on a pleasant farm, in a wide meadow where a clear pond lay in the shade of tall trees. I am black all over, except for one white foot and a white star on my forehead. My mother, a wise and gentle mare, gave me the advice that I have tried to follow ever since, in good homes and in bad ones. "You come of a good family, and you have never learned rough ways," she said to me one morning. "Grow up gentle and good, do your work with a willing heart, and never bite or kick, not even in play."

When I was four years old, my master broke me in himself, with such patience that I hardly knew how much I was learning. Soon afterwards I was sold to Squire Gordon, whose fine old house stood in a large park called Birtwick. It was my new mistress who chose my name, declaring that no other would suit a horse so black and so beautiful. The coachman, John Manly, was a steady, soft-spoken man who understood horses better than anyone I have known. A cheerful young groom worked beside him.

In the stalls next to mine lived Ginger, a tall chestnut mare, and Merrylegs, a fat little pony who carried the children about with endless good temper. Ginger, on the other hand, laid back her ears and snapped at anyone who came near her. One evening in the paddock she told me how she had come to be so bitter. "Nobody was ever kind to me when I was young," she said, tossing her head. "My first masters believed in force rather than patience, so I decided that I would fight them whenever I could." At Birtwick, however, nobody treated her harshly, and little by little the anger went out of her.

One stormy evening in autumn, John and my master were driving me home from the town. Rain had been falling since morning, and the river beside the road was running so high that it roared against its banks. As we came onto the wooden bridge, something in the feel of the boards beneath my feet told me that all was not well, and I stopped. Neither John's voice nor my master's could make me take a single step further. At that moment the toll-keeper came running out of his little house, swinging a lantern and shouting into the wind. "Stop! Stop! The middle of the bridge has been carried away by the flood!" Had I gone on, the carriage and all of us in it would have gone down into that black and rushing water. That night, stroking my neck in the stable, my master told John that God had given animals a kind of knowledge which was often surer than the reasoning of men.

The following spring my master and mistress set out on a long journey, and one night we put up at a large inn. Shortly after midnight I woke to a heavy, bitter smell, and within minutes the stable was so full of smoke that I could barely see. Somebody had been careless with a light in the hayloft above us, and the dry hay had caught at once. All around me horses were stamping and crying out in fear, and I stood frozen, unable to move. It was our young groom who saved us, walking in as calmly as if he had come to bring our evening oats. He tied his own scarf over my eyes, led me gently out into the yard, and then plunged back into the smoke for Ginger. They had scarcely reached the open air when part of the roof came crashing down behind them. "You have done a brave thing tonight, lad," said my master, and I have never forgotten the courage of that young man.

Not long afterwards the young groom left for a better position, and Joe Green, a willing boy of fourteen, took his place. One winter night John woke me, and by the light of his lantern I saw that his face was white with worry. "Our mistress is very ill, Beauty," he said. "Now you must run for her life, as fast as you have ever run." I galloped the whole way to the town without once slackening my pace, and the doctor rode me back himself, since his own horse was worn out. By the time we reached Birtwick, I was hot and dripping with sweat, and John was still trudging home on foot along the dark road. Young Joe meant well, but he gave me a whole bucket of icy water and left me standing without a blanket. Before morning, I was shaking with a fever, and for many days John nursed me with endless care. Joe was so sorry that he could scarcely eat, yet the lesson made him one of the most careful grooms I ever knew.

My mistress lived through that winter, but she never grew strong again, and at last the doctor ordered her to leave England for a warmer country. Merrylegs was given to the vicar, and Ginger and I were sold together to an earl, whose great house was called Earlshall. The countess, it turned out, cared a great deal more for fashion than for the comfort of the creatures who drew her carriage. At her wish, the coachman fastened tight bearing reins on us that held our heads high hour after hour. With my head forced up, my neck and back ached constantly, and I could no longer throw my weight into the collar. One afternoon, when the rein was shortened yet again, Ginger reared and kicked so wildly that nobody could hold her, and she was never put to the carriage again. I had no such spirit left in me, and for four long months I bore that cruel rein alone.

Then one night a careless groom rode me home far too fast over a newly mended road, strewn with sharp loose stones. One of my shoes had worked loose, and though I tried to warn him by breaking my pace, he paid no attention. At last, I stumbled and fell heavily onto my knees. My knees healed in time, but they were left marked, and no fine family will keep a horse whose knees show that he has once been down. So the earl sold me, and from that day I never again shared a stable with Ginger.

After passing through several hands, I came at last to London, where Jerry Barker bought me to draw his cab. Jerry was poor, but he was the most honest and good-tempered man in the city, and he never forgot that a horse can feel tired, hungry or afraid. His wife and children treated me as one of the family, and the little ones often brought me apples. On Sundays Jerry would not take a single fare, even when it cost him dearly, for he held that men and horses alike had a right to rest. Hard as the work was, I look back on those three years as some of the happiest of my life.

One day, as we stood waiting at a cab stand, an old worn-out chestnut was brought up beside me, her coat dull and her neck thin. When she lifted her head and looked at me, I saw at once that it was Ginger. "I am tired of this life, Beauty," she told me, so quietly that I could barely hear her. "Men have worked me as hard as they could, and I have no strength left to fight them any more." I laid my nose against hers, but nothing I could say would comfort her. I never saw her again, and I can only hope that her troubles are over at last.

Then, after a bitter New Year's night of waiting outside in the cold, Jerry fell so seriously ill that the doctor forbade him ever to drive a cab again. His family moved away to the country, and I was sold to masters who cared for nothing but the money a horse could earn. Under their heavy loads I grew thin and weak, until one day my strength gave out completely, and I sank down in the street. At a horse fair soon afterwards, a kind old farmer looked past my dull coat and decided that I deserved a rest. He fed me well all winter, and by spring I was strong enough to be sold to three gentle ladies who lived in the country. Their young groom was brushing me one morning when he stopped, stared at my forehead, and then bent down to look at my foot. "A white star and one white foot," he murmured. "Why, this must be Black Beauty from Birtwick, my own old friend!" It was Joe Green, grown tall and broad, and he was so glad that for a while he could only laugh and stroke my neck. I have lived in this quiet place for a long time now, and I know in my heart that my troubles are all over.
