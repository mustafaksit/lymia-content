# Kidnapped

Summary: An orphan boy visits his rich uncle and soon finds himself a prisoner on a ship.

## Level A1

David Balfour lived in a small village. His mother and father died. "Go to the house of Shaws. Your uncle lives there," said a kind minister. So David walked there for two days.

The house was big, dark and cold. Uncle Ebenezer was old, thin and mean. "Come in, then," said Uncle Ebenezer.

One stormy night, Ebenezer gave David a key. "Get the chest from the tower," he said. The tower stairs were very dark. Suddenly, lightning lit up the sky. The stairs stopped, and there was nothing below! David went carefully back down. Now he knew the truth about his uncle.

The next day, they went to Queensferry. A ship, the Covenant, was there. "Come and meet Captain Hoseason," said the uncle. David went onto the ship. But the uncle went back to land alone. Sailors held David and locked him below. Now David was a prisoner at sea!

In thick fog, the ship hit a boat. A man, Alan Breck, jumped onto the ship. He had a belt full of gold. The captain wanted the gold. David heard the plan and told Alan. Alan and David went into a small room. The sailors attacked, but the friends held firm. At last the sailors gave up.

Soon the ship hit rocks near Mull. David swam to land alone. Later, he found Alan again. Soldiers wrongly blamed them for a crime. So the friends ran across the Highlands. They slept in the heather.

At last, they came near Edinburgh. A lawyer, Rankeillor, listened to David. "Your father was the older brother. So the house is yours," said Rankeillor. At night, Alan knocked on the uncle's door. David and the lawyer hid nearby. "Why did you pay Hoseason?" asked Alan. "To take the boy away," said Ebenezer. Then David and Rankeillor stepped out.

The uncle gave David money every year. David and Alan said goodbye on a hill. Then David walked into Edinburgh, a rich man.

## Level A2

When his father died, David Balfour was seventeen. His mother was dead too. The village minister gave him a letter. "Take this to the house of Shaws, near Edinburgh. Your uncle Ebenezer lives there," said the minister. So David walked there for two days.

The house was large, but half of it had no roof. No smoke came from the chimneys. An old man shouted at David from a window. This was Uncle Ebenezer, a thin, gray man with nervous eyes. He gave David a cold supper and locked every door. His uncle loved money more than people.

One stormy night, Ebenezer gave David a key. "Bring me the chest from the top of the tower," he said. There was no light, so David climbed slowly. Suddenly, a flash of lightning lit up the sky. The stairs ended in the air, high above the ground! David went carefully back down. Now he knew the truth. His uncle wanted to get rid of him.

The next morning, Ebenezer was suddenly friendly. "Come to Queensferry and meet my lawyer," he said. At Queensferry, they met Captain Hoseason of the Covenant. The captain invited David onto his ship. Soon David saw his uncle in a small boat. The boat went back to land without him. "Help!" cried David, but strong hands held him. The sailors locked him in the bottom of the ship. Later, David worked for the captain in the round-house.

One foggy night, the Covenant hit a small boat. Only one man jumped up onto the ship. He was Alan Breck, a brave man from the Highlands. He had a sword and a belt full of gold. The captain and his men wanted the gold. David heard their plan and told Alan.

Alan and David stayed in the round-house. The sailors attacked again and again, but they could not get in. At last, they gave up. "You are a brave boy, David," said Alan.

Days later, the ship hit rocks near Mull. David swam to a small island and stayed there, wet and hungry. Then the sea went down, and he walked across to Mull. Soon he found Alan again, but there was trouble. Soldiers wrongly blamed the two friends for a crime.

So Alan and David ran across the Highlands. They slept in the heather and hid on a rock all day. A chief, Cluny, hid them in a little house on a mountain. Sometimes the tired friends argued. But when David became ill, Alan helped him.

At last, they came back near Edinburgh. David told his story to the lawyer Rankeillor. "Your father was the older brother. So the house of Shaws belongs to you," said Rankeillor. That night, Alan knocked on the door of Shaws. David and Rankeillor hid in the dark. "Why did you pay Captain Hoseason?" asked Alan. "To take the boy far away across the sea," said Ebenezer. Then David and the lawyer stepped out.

Ebenezer promised David most of the money from the land. Alan and David said goodbye on a green hill. Then David walked slowly into Edinburgh.

## Level B1

David Balfour was seventeen when his father, a poor village schoolmaster, died. His mother was dead too, so David had no family at home. His father left a letter for the house of Shaws, near Edinburgh. "Your father's family lives there," said the village minister. So David walked across the country for two days, full of hope.

People on the road gave him strange looks when he asked the way. The house was huge, but one side of it had no roof. An old man looked down from a window and asked rudely what he wanted. This was his uncle, Ebenezer Balfour. He was thin and bent, and he never looked David in the eye. He ate thin porridge, used only one candle, and kept every door locked.

One black and stormy night, Ebenezer handed David a key. "Climb the tower stairs and bring me the chest at the top," he said. There was no lamp, so David felt his way up in the dark. Suddenly a flash of lightning showed him the truth. The stairs ended in empty air, high above the ground. David's heart was beating fast as he went back down, step by step. At the bottom he found his uncle, who was shaking with fear. David knew now that his uncle wanted him gone.

The next morning, a boy brought a message from Captain Hoseason of the Covenant. "We will go to Queensferry together," said Ebenezer, "and you can meet my lawyer, Rankeillor." At Queensferry, the captain invited David to see his ship. A moment later, David saw his uncle in a small boat, rowing back to the shore. "Help!" David shouted, but strong hands grabbed him, and everything went dark. He woke up in the bottom of the ship, which was sailing out to sea. The captain was planning to take him across the ocean and sell him as a worker. Later, David became the captain's boy in the round-house, a small cabin on deck.

One foggy night, the Covenant ran into a small boat and broke it. Only one man managed to climb aboard. He was Alan Breck, a proud man from the Highlands with a sword. Around his waist he wore a belt that was heavy with gold. The crew planned to steal the gold, but David heard them and warned Alan. "Then you will stand with me," said Alan, "and two of us can hold this room."

The sailors attacked the round-house that night, but Alan and David were ready. They held the door bravely until the sailors gave up. After the fight, Alan gave David a silver button from his coat. "Show this to my friends, and they will help you," he said.

A few days later, the ship hit hidden rocks near Mull. David swam to a small, empty island and stayed there for days. Cold and hungry, he could not see a way off. Then he discovered that at low tide he could walk across to Mull. With the silver button, he found help and traveled on to look for Alan.

When David met Alan again, there was terrible news. Someone had committed a crime nearby, and the soldiers believed that Alan and David were guilty. They were innocent, but nobody wanted to listen. So the two friends ran into the wild Highlands and slept in the heather by day. One hot day they lay on a big rock while soldiers were walking below them. Later, Cluny, a chief who was also hiding, gave them shelter in his secret house.

The journey was long, and one day the tired friends had a bitter quarrel. Soon after, David became very ill, and Alan helped him to a house. They forgave each other, and their friendship grew stronger.

At last, they came back toward Edinburgh, and David went to see the lawyer Rankeillor. "Your father was the older brother. So the house of Shaws and its land belong to you," said Rankeillor. That night, Alan knocked on the door of Shaws while the others hid in the dark. "Why did you pay Captain Hoseason?" asked Alan. "To carry the boy far across the sea," said Ebenezer. At that moment, David and Rankeillor stepped out of the darkness.

Ebenezer agreed to give David two thirds of the money from the land every year. On a green hill outside the city, the two friends said goodbye. Then David walked slowly into Edinburgh, a rich young man without his friend.

## Level B2

David Balfour was seventeen when his father, the schoolmaster of a small country village, died and left him almost nothing. His mother had died some years before, so there was no one left to keep him at home. His father left a letter for the house of Shaws, near Edinburgh. "Your father came from that house, though he never spoke of it. Take this letter there, and you may find your fortune," said the village minister. So David packed a small bundle and set off on foot, hopeful and a little proud.

The closer he came, the stranger the answers grew when he asked the way. One woman spoke of the place with open anger, and a man warned him to keep away from it. It was a great, gloomy building with one wing left unfinished, its windows dark and its chimneys cold. An old man leaned out of an upper window and demanded to know his business. This was Ebenezer Balfour, his father's brother, a bent, gray figure who could not look anyone in the eye. It soon became clear that Ebenezer cared for nothing in the world as much as his money.

One wild night, Ebenezer sent David to fetch a chest from the top of the tower. There was no candle, so David climbed in total darkness. Suddenly a flash of lightning lit up everything around him. The stairs simply stopped, and beyond the last step there was only empty space. Very slowly, David made his way back down, shaking with anger. His uncle, waiting below, almost fainted when he saw David come back. Now there was no doubt in David's mind that Ebenezer wished him harm.

The next morning, a cabin boy arrived with a letter from Captain Hoseason, master of the ship Covenant. "I have business with the captain at Queensferry," Ebenezer explained, "and afterwards we can visit my lawyer, Rankeillor." At Queensferry, Hoseason was all smiles and invited David aboard to see his ship. A moment later, he noticed his uncle in a small boat, being rowed back toward the shore. He cried out for help, but strong arms seized him, and the world went black. When he woke, he lay in the dark belly of the ship, which was already sailing out to sea.

In time, David discovered what his uncle had arranged. Hoseason had been paid to carry him across the ocean, where he would be sold to work on a farm for years. Later he became the captain's boy and was moved to the round-house, a small cabin on deck.

One night, while thick fog covered the sea, the Covenant struck a small boat and cut it in two. A single passenger managed to leap aboard. He was Alan Breck, a man from the Highlands with a fine coat, a sword and a quick, proud temper. Around his waist he wore a money belt, heavy with gold for his chief, who lived in exile across the sea. Hoseason agreed to put him ashore, but Hoseason and his officers secretly planned to take the gold. David went straight to Alan and warned him. "You are an honest lad," said Alan, "and if you stand with me, we will give them a fight they won't forget."

They barred the round-house and waited. When the crew charged the door, Alan defended it with his sword while David guarded the other side. The struggle was short and loud, and before long the sailors lost heart and retreated. Delighted with his young friend, Alan took a silver button from his coat and gave it to David. "Wherever you show this button in my country, my friends will help you," he said.

Soon afterwards, the ship struck a reef off the coast of Mull. David was thrown into the sea and struggled ashore alone. For several days he suffered on a small, rocky island, cold and miserable. Then he realized, with some embarrassment, that he could walk across to Mull whenever the tide was low. With the help of the silver button, he made his way across Mull and onto the mainland. There he found Alan again, but at a dangerous moment. A serious crime had just taken place nearby, and the soldiers were sure that Alan and David were responsible. Neither of them had done it, yet both knew that no court would believe them.

For many days they crossed the Highlands, hiding by day among rocks and heather. Once they lay all day on top of a great rock under a burning sun while soldiers moved about below. Hungry and worn out, they were finally taken in by Cluny, a chief who was also in hiding on a mountain. The long journey wore away their patience, and one day they had a bitter quarrel. When David fell seriously ill soon after, Alan forgot his pride and cared for him until he was well. Their friendship came out of the quarrel stronger than ever.

At last, they reached the shore across the water from Queensferry, but they could not cross unseen. A kind young woman at an inn took pity on them and rowed them over secretly at night. David went to the lawyer Rankeillor, who listened to the whole story with growing interest. Then Rankeillor explained a family secret. Long ago, David's father and Ebenezer had loved the same lady. When she chose David's father, the two brothers made a strange bargain, and Ebenezer kept the house and the land. But David's father was the elder brother, so by law the estate now belonged to David. "Your uncle knows this perfectly well," said the lawyer, "and that is why he wanted you out of the way."

That night, Alan knocked on the door of Shaws while the others hid nearby. Alan pretended that some friends of his had found David on the coast of Mull. "They are keeping him safe," said Alan, "but they want to know what you wish them to do with him." Ebenezer made it clear that he did not want the boy back. "Then tell me," said Alan, "how much did you pay Hoseason?" "Enough to carry the boy across the sea and leave him there," snapped Ebenezer. At that, Rankeillor stepped forward and greeted him politely, and the old man knew he was beaten.

Ebenezer was allowed to stay at Shaws, but he agreed to pay David two thirds of the yearly income from the estate. Afterwards, on a green hill outside Edinburgh, the two friends said goodbye. Alan had to hide until a ship could take him safely abroad. David walked into the city a rich young man, but the busy streets felt empty without his friend.

## Level C1

When his father, the schoolmaster of a quiet country parish, died in the early summer, David Balfour was left at seventeen with a few coins and a handful of books. His mother had died some years earlier, and the cottage would soon be needed by the next schoolmaster. It was the minister, an old friend of the family, who produced the letter that changed everything: a sealed packet, addressed in his father's hand to the house of Shaws, near Edinburgh. "Your father came from Shaws, though he never chose to speak of it, and this letter may well make your fortune," said the minister.

David set out on foot with high hopes, but every question he asked along the road seemed to darken them. The mere mention of Shaws made one woman's face go hard with old anger, and a passing stranger advised him to turn back. When at last the house rose before him, it looked less like a family home than an unfinished ruin, one wing open to the sky and the chimneys smokeless. An old man leaned out of an upper window and asked him, without a single word of welcome, what he wanted there. This was Ebenezer Balfour, his father's brother: stooped, gray and unshaven, with eyes that slid away whenever they met another's. Supper was a bowl of thin porridge, and every door in the place was locked at night.

For a day or so the old man tried, rather clumsily, to be agreeable, though David often turned to find him watching with an expression he could not read. Then, on a night of wild wind and rain, Ebenezer handed him a key and asked him, quite casually, to fetch a chest from the top of the tower. No light was offered, so David climbed in pitch darkness, one hand on the cold stone, feeling with his feet for each step. Then a flash of lightning showed him exactly where he stood: the steps ended abruptly in empty air, with nothing beyond but a long fall into the night. He came down far more slowly than he had gone up, and his fear gave way to a cold, steady anger. At the foot of the tower his uncle, seeing him return, sank down as pale as paper. Whatever Ebenezer had hoped for that night, David now knew him for an enemy.

The next morning brought a cabin boy with a letter from Captain Hoseason of the brig Covenant, which lay at Queensferry. Ebenezer, suddenly all good humor, proposed that they go there together, adding that they could call on his lawyer, Rankeillor, and put David's affairs in order. The captain received them warmly and, with a fine show of kindness, invited the young man to look over his ship. David had scarcely set foot on the deck when he saw his uncle in a small boat, being rowed briskly back to shore. He shouted for help, but strong hands seized him, and the world vanished into darkness. He woke in the black hold, sick and dizzy, with the whole ship rolling beneath him as she stood out to sea.

From the sailors' talk he slowly pieced together his uncle's bargain. Hoseason had been paid to carry him across the sea and sell him into years of labor, from which no inconvenient nephew would return to trouble the house of Shaws. When he was well enough to stand, he was brought up from the hold and set to work as the captain's boy, sleeping and serving in the round-house on deck.

One foggy night the Covenant ran down a small boat and cut her clean in two, and a single man managed to spring up and catch hold of the brig. He was Alan Breck, a small, proud gentleman of the Highlands in a fine coat with silver buttons, with a sword at his side and a temper as sharp as its edge. Around his waist was a belt full of gold, which he was carrying to his chief, who was living in exile across the sea. Hoseason promised to land him safely, yet within the hour David overheard the captain and his officers arranging to take the gold by force. To keep quiet would have been safer, but David went straight to the round-house and told Alan everything. "You are an honest lad, David Balfour, and if you will stand with me tonight, we shall give them more than they bargained for," said Alan with a slow smile.

They barred the doors and turned their small cabin into a fortress. When the crew rushed it, Alan held the main door with his sword while David watched the other, and what followed was loud, confused and soon over. Before long the sailors lost heart and drew back, and no further attack came that night. Alan was overjoyed, and in his pride he declared David as brave as any man in the Highlands. Taking one of the silver buttons from his coat, he gave it to David as a token of friendship. "Show this anywhere among my people, and you will find friends," he said.

A few days later the Covenant, sailing too close to a dangerous coast, struck a reef off the island of Mull. David was flung into the sea and, after a desperate struggle, dragged himself onto a small rocky island, with no sign of Alan or the ship anywhere. For several miserable days he lived there on shellfish and rainwater, until it dawned on him, to his considerable embarrassment, that at low tide he could simply walk across to Mull. With Alan's button as his passport, he found help among strangers and made his way across Mull toward the mainland. He met Alan again, but at the worst possible moment. A serious crime had been committed nearby, and the authorities, eager to blame someone, settled on Alan, with David named as his companion. Neither had any hand in it, yet both understood that in such troubled times their innocence would count for very little.

There followed weeks of flight across the Highlands: long night marches, cold beds in the heather, and days spent lying still while soldiers searched the valleys below. Once they spent an entire day pressed flat on top of a great rock under a merciless sun, scarcely daring to lift their heads. At last, they were sheltered by Cluny, a chief who was himself in hiding, in a strange little house built into the side of a mountain.

Hardship, however, has a way of testing friendships, and theirs began to wear thin. Small resentments grew into silences, and one wet evening the silences broke into a bitter quarrel in which both said things they did not mean. Soon afterwards David fell seriously ill, and Alan, forgetting every injury to his pride, half carried him to the nearest house and looked after him until he recovered. They never spoke of the quarrel again, but something between them had grown stronger.

When at last they reached the shore across the water from Queensferry, they found every crossing watched. It was a kind young woman at a small inn who, moved by their story, rowed them over herself under cover of darkness. In Queensferry, David presented himself, still in his travel-worn rags, at the door of the lawyer Rankeillor, who listened to the whole extraordinary tale with growing interest.

In return, the lawyer told David the secret of his family. Long ago, his father and Ebenezer had both loved the same lady. When she chose the elder brother, the two men made an odd bargain: David's father took the bride, and Ebenezer took the house and the land. The agreement had never had any force in law, however, so the estate now belonged by right to David himself. "That is why your uncle wished you far away, and that is why we must make him confess it," said Rankeillor.

The plan was simple. That night, while David, the lawyer and his clerk hid in the darkness, Alan hammered on the door of Shaws. In the manner of a rough gentleman from the hills, he announced that friends of his had found David on the coast of Mull. Ebenezer made it plain that he had no wish to see the boy again. "Then you will not mind telling me what you paid Hoseason, so that I know what my silence is worth," said Alan. Cornered and confused, Ebenezer admitted that he had paid the captain to carry his nephew across the sea and sell him there. At that moment Rankeillor stepped out of the shadows and wished him a very good evening, and the old man knew the game was over.

In the end Ebenezer was allowed to keep his gloomy house, but he agreed to pay David two thirds of its yearly income for as long as he lived. The next day, on a green hill outside Edinburgh, the two friends parted, Alan to hide until a ship could carry him abroad, David to the city and the bank. David walked on into Edinburgh among the noise of the crowds, a rich young man at last, and lonelier than he had ever been on the open hills.
