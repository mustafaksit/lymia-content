# Daddy-Long-Legs

Summary: A young orphan goes to college thanks to a secret helper and writes him a letter every month.

## Level A1

Jerusha Abbott was an orphan. She lived in the John Grier Home. Jerusha was the oldest child. She was seventeen years old.

One evening, Mrs. Lippett called her. "A trustee likes your essay," said Mrs. Lippett. "He wants to send you to college." Jerusha could not believe it. She saw the man only from behind. His long shadow moved on the wall. It had very long arms and legs. She called him Daddy-Long-Legs.

The trustee paid for everything. But he had one rule. Every month, Jerusha must write a letter. She must send it to Mr. John Smith. It was not his real name. He never wrote back to her.

At college, Jerusha chose a new name. "Call me Judy," she told the girls. Her new friends were Sallie and Julia. Sallie McBride was warm and funny. Julia Pendleton came from a rich family. Judy read many books. She wrote funny letters to Daddy-Long-Legs.

One spring day, Julia's uncle came. His name was Jervis Pendleton. He was tall and kind. Judy showed him the college. In summer, Judy went to Lock Willow. It was a farm in the green hills. Later, Jervis came to the farm too. They walked in the hills together.

Judy wanted to be a writer. She wrote many stories. At first, nobody wanted them. But she did not stop. At last, she sold a book. She felt very proud.

Then Jervis asked Judy to marry him. Judy loved him, but she said no. She had no family and no money. His family was rich and proud. Jervis went away, and Judy cried.

Judy wrote to Daddy-Long-Legs for help. This time, he answered her. "Come to New York," he wrote. Judy went to his big house. A tall man sat in a chair. It was Jervis! Jervis was Daddy-Long-Legs. Judy laughed and cried at once. This time, she said yes.

## Level A2

Jerusha Abbott lived in the John Grier Home for orphans. She had no mother and no father. At seventeen, she was the oldest girl there. All day she cared for the small children. The home was clean, but it was cold and sad.

Once a month, the trustees came to visit the home. On that day, everything had to look perfect. After one visit, Mrs. Lippett called Jerusha to her office. Jerusha felt afraid.

"One of the trustees read your school essay," said Mrs. Lippett. "It made him laugh. He wants to send you to college." Jerusha could not speak. In the hall, she saw the trustee only from behind. The lights of a car threw his shadow on the wall. It had very long arms and legs, like a spider. Jerusha called him Daddy-Long-Legs.

The trustee paid for everything. But he had one rule. Every month, Jerusha must write him a letter about her life. She must send her letters to Mr. John Smith. This was not his real name. He never answered, and he never told her his real name.

At college, Jerusha chose a new name for herself. "Please call me Judy," she told the other girls. Her first friends were Sallie McBride and Julia Pendleton. Sallie was warm and funny, and she had a big happy family. Julia came from a very rich and proud family. The other girls knew many famous books, but Judy did not. So she read late into the night.

Judy wrote long, funny letters to Daddy-Long-Legs. "Are you old?" she asked in one letter. He never answered, but she did not stop. To Judy, he was like a family.

One spring day, Julia's uncle visited the college. His name was Jervis Pendleton. He was tall, kind and not at all proud. Julia was busy, so Judy showed him around. They walked, talked and had tea together. Judy liked him very much.

In the summer, Daddy-Long-Legs sent Judy to a farm. Its name was Lock Willow, and it lay among green hills. Later, Jervis came to Lock Willow too. As a boy, he spent many summers on this farm. Judy and Jervis climbed the hills and talked for hours.

Judy wrote stories all through college. At first, nobody wanted them. Then one story won a prize. After college, she finished a book, and a publisher bought it. She sent her first money to Daddy-Long-Legs.

At Lock Willow, Jervis asked Judy to marry him. Judy loved him, but she said no. She could not tell him about the orphan home. His family was rich, and she had no family at all. Jervis went away, sad and hurt. Judy wrote to Daddy-Long-Legs and told him everything. For the first time, he answered. "Come and see me in New York," he wrote. At his house, a tall man sat in a big chair. It was Jervis. "I wanted to tell you so many times," said Jervis. Jervis and Daddy-Long-Legs were the same man. This time, Judy said yes.

## Level B1

Jerusha Abbott was the oldest orphan in the John Grier Home. She was seventeen and knew no other home. Every day she washed small faces, buttoned small coats and kept ninety-seven children in order. She loved stories and words more than anything, but she never had time for them.

On the first Wednesday of every month, the trustees came to look at the home. It was the worst day of the month for Jerusha. Everything had to shine, and every child had to smile. On one of these days, as the last visitors were leaving, Mrs. Lippett sent for her. In the hall, Jerusha passed a tall trustee who was just going out. The lights of a waiting car threw his shadow across the wall. The shadow had long, thin legs, like a daddy-long-legs spider. Jerusha almost laughed out loud.

"You wrote a funny essay about the trustees' visit," said Mrs. Lippett. "The gentleman who just left read it. He wants to send you to college, and he will pay for everything." Jerusha could hardly believe her ears.

The trustee had only one condition. Once a month, Jerusha must write him a letter about her studies and her life. The letters must go to Mr. John Smith. This was not his real name, because he wanted to stay unknown. He would never answer, and she must never ask who he was.

At college, Jerusha started a new life with a new name. "I'm Judy," she told everyone, because she never liked the name Jerusha. She soon made two friends. Sallie McBride was cheerful and kind, and her family was big and warm. Julia Pendleton belonged to one of the richest families in New York. Judy did not tell either of them about the orphan home.

College was harder than she expected. The other girls knew famous books, painters and songs that Judy did not know at all. So she read every evening until late at night, trying to catch up. She also wrote to her helper, and in her letters she called him Daddy-Long-Legs. Her letters were full of jokes, little drawings and questions. In one letter she asked, "Are you very old, and are you bald?" He never replied, but she kept writing. For a girl with no family, he became the closest thing to one.

In the spring of her first year, Julia's uncle came to visit. His name was Jervis Pendleton, and he was tall, thin and friendly. Julia had no time for him, so Judy showed him around the college. They walked across the grounds and had tea, and Judy found him easy to talk to. Unlike the rest of his family, he did not seem proud at all.

That summer, Judy had nowhere to go. Daddy-Long-Legs found her a place at Lock Willow, a farm among green hills. She loved the animals, the fresh air and the long, free days. In the attic she found some old books with the name of Jervis Pendleton inside. As a boy, he spent his summers on this very farm. The next summer, Judy wanted to join Sallie's family, but Daddy-Long-Legs said no. So she went back to Lock Willow, and soon Jervis arrived there too. They spent happy weeks climbing hills and talking about everything.

Judy studied hard and grew more confident every year. She wrote stories for the college magazine, and one of them won a prize. Later she won a scholarship, so Daddy-Long-Legs did not have to pay her fees. She wanted to pay back every cent one day. A publisher did not want her first novel, but she did not give up.

After college, she returned to Lock Willow to write a new book. She finished it there, and a publisher bought it. Jervis visited her at the farm, and one evening he asked her to marry him. Judy loved him with all her heart, but she said no. She could not tell him about the orphan home. How could a girl with no name and no family marry into the proud Pendleton family? Jervis left without understanding, and Judy felt terribly unhappy.

She wrote to Daddy-Long-Legs and told him everything. For the first time in four years, he wrote back. He asked her to come and see him in New York. Judy went, full of nerves. A servant led her to a room where a tall man sat in a big chair. It was Jervis. "I wanted to tell you so many times," Jervis said gently. He was Daddy-Long-Legs, and he knew her secret from the very beginning. This time, Judy said yes.

## Level B2

Jerusha Abbott had spent all seventeen years of her life in the John Grier Home, an orphan asylum. Every child there wore the same dull clothes. As the oldest girl, she was kept busy from morning till night, washing faces and minding ninety-seven children. Even her name was not really hers: the matron, Mrs. Lippett, had found it in a telephone book. Jerusha loved words and stories, but nobody had ever asked what she thought about anything.

The worst day of every month was the first Wednesday, when the trustees came to inspect the home. Floors were polished, faces were scrubbed, and the children were told to smile and say thank you. On one such Wednesday, as the visitors were driving away, Jerusha was told to go straight to the office. On her way she caught sight of the last trustee in the hall. She never saw his face, but the lights of a car threw his shadow on the wall. His arms and legs looked impossibly long. He looked exactly like a daddy-long-legs, and she had to bite her lip to keep from laughing.

Mrs. Lippett was waiting with extraordinary news. At school, Jerusha had written a cheeky essay about trustees' day, and that very trustee had read it. "He found it amusing, which is more than I did," said Mrs. Lippett. "He thinks you may have a gift for writing, so he has decided to send you to college. He has sent two of our boys before, but never a girl." The trustee would pay her fees and give her a monthly allowance, on one condition. Once a month she had to write him a letter describing her progress. She was to address them to Mr. John Smith, a name he had chosen to hide his own. She should never expect a reply.

At college, the first thing Jerusha did was choose a new name. She became Judy, a name that sounded like a girl who had a family and a past. She made friends with Sallie McBride, a cheerful girl with a large and loving family. She also grew close to Julia Pendleton, whose family was among the richest in New York. Judy told neither of them where she came from, because she was afraid of how they might look at her.

She soon discovered how much she did not know. The other girls talked about books, artists and history they had learned as children. Judy had never even heard of most of them. She kept quiet, borrowed the books, and read them secretly late into the night. Meanwhile, she wrote to her unknown helper, whom she now called Daddy-Long-Legs. Her letters were lively and funny, full of little drawings, complaints about exams and endless questions. She pictured him as an old man with white hair, or perhaps no hair at all. In one letter, she begged him to tell her which. Only his secretary ever replied, and only about money. Still, she kept writing, because he was the first person in the world she could call her own.

Once, when she fell ill and spent a week in bed, a box of flowers arrived from him. She was so touched that she wrote him the warmest letter of all.

In the spring of her first year, Julia's uncle, Jervis Pendleton, came to visit the college. Julia found him dull and had no time for him, so Judy offered to show him around. He was tall and thin, with a friendly face, and he listened to her with real interest. They had tea together, and Judy decided that he was the only member of the Pendleton family worth knowing.

That summer, Daddy-Long-Legs arranged for her to stay at Lock Willow, a farm among green hills. Judy loved its animals, fields and slow, quiet days. In the attic she discovered a pile of old books with Jervis Pendleton's name inside. As a boy, he had spent his summers there. The following year, Judy wanted to join Sallie's family on holiday, but Daddy-Long-Legs firmly refused. She was sent back to the farm instead. She was annoyed, until Jervis turned up at Lock Willow too. The two of them spent weeks walking in the hills and talking about books and life.

Year by year Judy grew more confident. One of her stories won the college prize, and later she won a scholarship that covered her fees. She insisted that Daddy-Long-Legs should stop paying for her lessons, because she wanted to owe him as little as possible. When Jervis offered to take her and some friends on a trip abroad, she refused that too. She had too much pride to accept such gifts. Her first novel was rejected by a publisher, but instead of giving up, she started another.

After graduation, Judy went back to Lock Willow to write. She finished her book there, and this time a publisher accepted it. She sent Daddy-Long-Legs her first earnings as the beginning of her repayment. Jervis came to the farm, and one evening he asked her to marry him. Judy loved him deeply, yet she refused. She could not bring herself to tell him she was an orphan from the John Grier Home. She believed his proud family would never accept her. Jervis left, puzzled and hurt, and Judy was more miserable than she had ever been.

At last, she poured out the whole story in a letter to Daddy-Long-Legs and asked for his advice. For the first time, he answered in his own hand and invited her to his house in New York. Judy arrived trembling with nerves. She was shown into a quiet room where a tall man sat in a deep chair by the fire. It was Jervis. "I wanted to tell you so many times," he said, "but I was afraid of losing you." Jervis Pendleton and Daddy-Long-Legs had been the same man all along. This time, when he asked, Judy said yes.

## Level C1

For all seventeen years of her life, Jerusha Abbott had belonged to the John Grier Home, an orphan asylum where the dresses were identical and so were the meals. The days, for the most part, were identical too. Being the oldest, she had long since stopped being a child and become an unpaid helper, scrubbing faces, darning stockings and herding ninety-seven small orphans through their tidy, joyless routine. Even her name was second-hand: the matron, Mrs. Lippett, had picked the surname from a telephone book. What Jerusha loved most, words and stories, were the very things the home had no time for.

The first Wednesday of each month was trustees' day, which meant polished floors, scrubbed faces, and ninety-seven children reciting their gratitude on cue. Jerusha dreaded it more than any other day. On one such afternoon, just as the last car was pulling away, a child came running to say that Mrs. Lippett wanted her in the office at once. Hurrying downstairs, Jerusha glimpsed the final trustee leaving through the hall, though not his face. The headlights of a waiting car flung his shadow across the wall, stretching his arms and legs into absurdly long, thin lines. It was, she thought, exactly like a daddy-long-legs, and she had to swallow a laugh before she reached the office door.

The news that awaited her was so unlikely that she asked Mrs. Lippett to repeat it. At school Jerusha had written an impudent essay about trustees' day, and the essay had somehow found its way into the hands of the very gentleman whose shadow she had just admired. "Most people would have been offended," said Mrs. Lippett, with a look that suggested she was one of them. "He, however, laughed and decided you had talent. He means to send you to college and make a writer of you." Hitherto, it emerged, he had helped only boys, having no particular fondness for girls, which made his choice all the stranger. He would pay her fees and allow her a generous sum each month, provided she wrote to him once a month about her studies. The letters were to be addressed to Mr. John Smith, a name as false as it was dull, and no answer should ever be expected.

The first thing Jerusha did at college was to discard her name, as though it were part of the orphan's uniform. She became Judy, which sounded to her like a girl who had sisters, holidays and a past worth mentioning. Her closest friends were Sallie McBride, warm and sunny, the product of a large and affectionate family, and Julia Pendleton. Julia's surname opened every door in New York, and her manner suggested that she knew it. To neither did Judy confess the truth, fearing that one word about the asylum would change the way they looked at her forever. One Christmas, Sallie took her home, and Judy sat for the first time at a crowded family table, hiding her envy as brothers and sisters teased one another.

What startled her most was the sheer size of her ignorance. Her classmates chatted easily about poets, painters and kings whom every ordinary child seemed to have met in the nursery. Judy, who had read almost nothing but schoolbooks, had never heard of half of them. Rather than admit it, she smiled, nodded, and spent her evenings devouring the books everyone else had read at ten.

Meanwhile, the monthly letters to Mr. John Smith turned into something far livelier than progress reports. She addressed them to Daddy-Long-Legs, filled the margins with little sketches, and told him about everything from failed exams to new hats. "Dear Daddy-Long-Legs," ran one letter, "the whole world seems to have read this book except me, so tonight I shall read it twice." She also teased him, wondering aloud whether he was a white-haired old gentleman or, worse, a perfectly bald one, and begging him to settle the question. No such answer ever came; only a secretary wrote back, and only on matters of money. Yet the silence never stopped her, because at last there was someone she could call her own. When she lay ill for a week in the college infirmary, a box of flowers arrived with his card. The letter she sent in return was the most tender she ever wrote.

It was in the spring of her first year that Julia's uncle, Jervis Pendleton, paid the college a visit. Julia, who considered him tiresome, was not at home, so Judy volunteered to show him round. He was tall and lean, with an easy laugh, and quite unlike the rest of his formidable family. Over tea, he listened to her opinions as if they mattered, and she came away convinced that he was the one Pendleton worth knowing.

That first summer, having nowhere else to go, Judy was sent by Daddy-Long-Legs to Lock Willow, a farm tucked among green hills. She fell in love with all of it, the cows, the orchards and the long, unhurried days. Her letters to Daddy-Long-Legs that summer were full of calves, haystacks and sunsets, as if she had invented the countryside herself. Rummaging in the attic, she found a stack of boyhood books inscribed with the name Jervis Pendleton, for he had spent his summers on that very farm as a child. A year later, when she longed to holiday with Sallie's family, Daddy-Long-Legs, through his secretary, forbade it and sent her back to Lock Willow instead. She was furious, until Jervis himself appeared at the farm, after which the summer passed in a blur of hill walks, long talks and shared books.

Steadily, almost without noticing, Judy changed. A story of hers won the college prize. Later, a scholarship relieved Daddy-Long-Legs of her fees, an arrangement she insisted upon, since she was determined to repay every cent he had spent. When Jervis proposed a trip abroad for her and her friends, pride made her refuse that as well. Her first novel came back from the publisher with a polite rejection, which she bore, after one gloomy evening, by starting another.

After graduation, she returned to Lock Willow to write, and there, at last, a publisher accepted her book. Her first earnings went straight to Daddy-Long-Legs as the beginning of her debt's repayment. Jervis came to the farm that summer, and one evening, in the quiet after supper, he asked her to marry him. Judy loved him with everything she had, and so, perversely, she refused. She gave him no reason, only a stiff little speech about being unsuitable, which satisfied neither of them. She could not bring herself to tell him that she was a foundling from the John Grier Home, nor could she picture his illustrious relations welcoming a girl without a name. He left bewildered and hurt, and she was left lonelier than she had been even in the asylum.

In despair, she wrote the whole story to Daddy-Long-Legs, the one confidant she had always had, and begged him for advice. For the first time in four years, he replied in his own handwriting, asking her to call on him in New York. Judy went with her heart in her mouth, and was shown into a quiet room where a tall man sat in a deep armchair by the fire. It was Jervis. "I meant to tell you a hundred times," he said quietly, "but every time I was afraid you would never forgive me." The shadow on the orphanage wall, the silent reader of all her letters and the man she loved had been one and the same from the very beginning. This time, when he asked, Judy did not refuse.
