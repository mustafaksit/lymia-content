# The Three Musketeers and the Queen's Diamonds

Summary: A poor young man from Gascony joins three musketeers to save the queen from the Cardinal's trap.

## Level A1

D'Artagnan was a young man from Gascony. He had an old horse and little money. He wanted to be a musketeer in Paris.

In Paris, he was always in a hurry. He ran into a musketeer called Athos. Next, he got lost in Porthos's long coat. Then he picked up Aramis's handkerchief. All three wanted to fight him. "Meet me at noon," said Athos. "Meet me at one," said Porthos. "Meet me at two," said Aramis.

At noon, Athos came with two friends. His friends were Porthos and Aramis! Then five of the Cardinal's guards came. "Stop! Come with us!" they shouted. The young man stood with the musketeers. "Now we are four," he said. They fought, and they won.

Now the four were best friends. Constance was a pretty young woman. She worked for the queen. The young man loved her at once.

The king gave the queen twelve diamond pins. She gave them to a friend in London. He was the duke of Buckingham. The Cardinal heard about the gift. He sent Milady to London. She took two pins from the duke.

The Cardinal asked the king for a ball. She had to wear her twelve pins there. But her pins were in London! Constance went to d'Artagnan for help. "I can go to London," he said.

His three friends rode with him. Porthos stayed behind after an argument. Then men attacked them, and Aramis stayed behind. At an inn, Athos had trouble too. Only the young man went on to London.

The duke looked at the pins. "Two pins are gone!" he cried. He remembered Milady at his ball. His men made two new pins fast.

D'Artagnan rode fast to Paris. He came on the day of the ball. Constance took the pins to the queen. The Cardinal gave two pins to the king. "Count the queen's pins," said the Cardinal. There were twelve! The Cardinal was very angry, but he smiled.

The queen gave the young man a ring. Soon he found his three friends again.

## Level A2

D'Artagnan was eighteen years old and came from Gascony. His father gave him an old yellow horse and a little money. So the young man rode to Paris. He wanted to join the king's musketeers.

In Paris, he went to Treville, the captain of the musketeers. "First you must learn," said Treville. The young man ran out in a great hurry. On the stairs, he ran into a musketeer named Athos. At the door, he got caught in the long coat of Porthos. Then he picked up a handkerchief from under Aramis's foot. The three musketeers were very angry with him. "Meet me at noon," said Athos. "Meet me at one," said Porthos. "Meet me at two," said Aramis.

At noon, Athos was there with two friends. They were Porthos and Aramis! Before the fight began, five of the Cardinal's guards arrived. "Stop and come with us!" cried the leader of the guards. The musketeers were only three, and the guards were five. The young man stood next to Athos. "Now we are four," he said. They fought hard, and they won.

The king was pleased, and he gave d'Artagnan some gold. From then on, the four were always together. Porthos found a servant for him, a young man called Planchet. D'Artagnan took a room in the house of Bonacieux. His landlord's young wife, Constance, worked for the queen. One night, the young man saved her from the Cardinal's men. He loved her from then on.

The king once gave the queen, Anne of Austria, twelve diamond pins. She gave them to her friend, the duke of Buckingham. The duke lived in London. It was a gift of friendship, but the Cardinal hated the queen. He had spies everywhere. He sent Milady, a clever lady, to London. At a ball, she secretly took two pins from the duke.

Then the Cardinal asked the king for a great ball. "Wear your diamond pins at the ball," the king told the queen. The queen went white, because her pins were in London. Constance came to the young man with a letter from the queen. "I can leave tonight," he said.

The four friends left Paris with their servants. At the first inn, Porthos stayed behind after an argument. Later, men attacked them on the road, and Aramis stayed behind. At another inn, men attacked Athos, and he could not go on. Only d'Artagnan and Planchet reached Calais.

They needed a paper from the Cardinal for the ship. A gentleman at the port had one. The young man fought him, won and took the paper.

In London, the duke opened his box and went pale. Only ten pins were there! "Milady was at my last ball," he said. He closed all the ports to stop any news. His jeweler made two new pins in two days.

The young man rode back day and night. He arrived in Paris on the day of the ball. Constance took the twelve pins to the queen.

At the ball, the Cardinal gave the king two pins. "Count the queen's pins," said the Cardinal. The king counted them, and there were twelve. The Cardinal's face went red, but he smiled. "These two pins are a gift for the queen," he said quickly.

Later, the queen gave the young man a beautiful ring. Soon after, he found his three friends again.

## Level B1

D'Artagnan was eighteen, poor and proud, and he came from Gascony. His father gave him an old yellow horse and a little money. The young man rode north to Paris with a big dream. He wanted to become one of the king's musketeers, the bravest soldiers in the land.

In Paris, he went straight to Treville, the captain of the musketeers. "You cannot join the musketeers yet," said Treville, "but you can learn." Then, in a great hurry, the young man ran out of the room. On the stairs he crashed into Athos, a tall, quiet musketeer. At the door, he ran straight into Porthos and got caught in his long coat. A little later, he picked up a lady's handkerchief from under Aramis's foot. All three musketeers felt insulted, and each one asked for a duel. Athos chose noon, Porthos chose one o'clock, and Aramis chose two.

At noon, Athos was waiting in a quiet place with two friends. They were Porthos and Aramis! Before anyone could start, five of the Cardinal's guards came round the corner. The king did not allow duels, and the Cardinal's guards loved to catch musketeers. "Put away your weapons and come with us!" shouted their leader. The young man was not a musketeer, but he chose his new friends. "We are four now," he told Athos. The fight was short, the four friends won it, and nobody was badly hurt.

The king liked brave men, and he gave d'Artagnan forty gold coins. From then on, the four young men went everywhere together. Porthos found a clever servant for him, a young man called Planchet. D'Artagnan rented rooms in the house of a shopkeeper called Bonacieux. Bonacieux had a pretty young wife named Constance, who worked for the queen. One evening the Cardinal's men tried to take her away, but the young man saved her. From that moment, he loved her with all his heart.

The queen, Anne of Austria, once received twelve diamond pins from the king. She gave these pins to her friend, the duke of Buckingham, as a gift. The duke lived in London, and the king did not trust him. The Cardinal was the queen's enemy, and his spies were everywhere. He sent a message to Milady, a beautiful lady who worked for him in London. At a ball, she stood close to the duke and secretly took two of the pins.

Then the Cardinal went to the king with a clever idea. "Give a ball," said the Cardinal, "and ask the queen to wear her diamond pins." The king agreed and chose a day for the ball. When the queen heard the news, she almost cried. Her pins were in London, and there was no time to get them back. "I know a young man who will go to London for you," Constance told her. That night Constance came to his room with a letter from the queen to the duke. "I will ride to London and bring back the pins," d'Artagnan promised.

Treville gave the four friends leave, and they left Paris with their servants. At the first inn, a stranger picked an argument, and Porthos stayed behind to answer him. Further on, men attacked the friends on the road, and Aramis could not continue. At another inn, the owner accused Athos of paying with false money. Several men rushed at him, and Athos stayed behind to hold them back. In the end, only d'Artagnan and Planchet reached Calais.

There they learned that no one could cross the sea without a paper from the Cardinal. A gentleman at the port was carrying such a paper. The young man challenged him, won the fight and took the paper. An hour later, he and Planchet were sailing across the sea.

In London, the duke read the queen's letter and opened his jewel box at once. "Two pins are missing!" he cried. Then he remembered Milady, who was standing close to him at his last ball. He closed every port in the country, so no news could reach the Cardinal. His jeweler worked day and night, and in two days he made two new pins. The duke put all twelve into the box and gave it to his young visitor.

With fresh horses along the road, he reached Paris on the day of the ball. Constance was waiting, and she carried the box to the queen in secret.

At the ball, the twelve diamond pins were shining on the queen's dress. The Cardinal gave the king a small box with two diamond pins inside. "Ask the queen where she lost these two," he whispered. The king looked at his wife and counted her pins. There were twelve! The Cardinal hid his anger behind a smile. "I wanted to give the queen two more pins as a gift," he said quietly.

Later Constance led d'Artagnan into a small room with a curtain. A white hand came out from behind the curtain and gave him a beautiful ring. A few days later, he rode back along the road and found his three friends safe. Together the four of them returned to Paris, laughing all the way.

## Level B2

In the spring of sixteen twenty-five, a young man rode into Paris on an old yellow horse. His name was d'Artagnan, he was eighteen years old, and he had very little money but a great deal of courage. His father had given him the horse, a few coins and one firm piece of advice: never let anyone insult him. The young man dreamed of joining the musketeers, the king's own soldiers, who were famous for their courage and their quarrels.

He went first to the house of Treville, the captain of the musketeers, who had once been a friend of his father. Treville received him kindly, but he explained that nobody could join the musketeers without first proving himself. Then he hurried out of the room. On the stairs he ran straight into Athos, a tall and noble musketeer, who was not pleased at all. At the street door he ran into Porthos and became tangled in his huge coat. Under the coat he noticed that Porthos's fine belt was gold only at the front, and Porthos saw him notice it. A little later he picked up a lady's handkerchief that Aramis was hiding under his foot. By the end of the morning, he had agreed to three duels: Athos at noon, Porthos at one and Aramis at two.

At noon, he arrived at a quiet field behind an old convent, where Athos was already waiting. To his surprise, the two friends Athos had brought with him were Porthos and Aramis. But before the first duel could begin, five of the Cardinal's guards appeared. Duels were forbidden by the king's law, and the guards ordered the musketeers to surrender. The musketeers refused, although they were only three against five. The guards told the young man to go home. Instead, he stepped forward and stood beside Athos. "With me, you are four," he said. The fight that followed was fast and fierce, but the four friends fought as one and won it. The guards gave up and went away, and no one was seriously hurt.

The king loved his musketeers, and he was secretly delighted to hear that the Cardinal's guards had lost. He gave d'Artagnan forty gold coins as a reward. From that day on, the young man and the three musketeers were always together. Porthos found him a sharp servant named Planchet, and the young man took rooms in the house of a shopkeeper, Bonacieux. Bonacieux's young wife, Constance, was the queen's most trusted maid. One evening the Cardinal's men tried to arrest Constance in her own house, and d'Artagnan drove them away. From that moment, he was deeply in love with her.

Soon Constance told him why the queen was in danger. Some time before, the king had given his wife, Anne of Austria, twelve beautiful diamond pins. The queen had given them as a gift to her friend, the duke of Buckingham, before he returned to London. It was an innocent gift of friendship, but the Cardinal was the queen's enemy. Cardinal Richelieu was the most powerful man in the kingdom, and his spies told him everything. He sent orders to Milady, a beautiful and dangerous woman who served him in London. At a grand ball, she came close to the duke and quietly took two pins from the ribbon on his shoulder.

Then the Cardinal made his move. He suggested to the king that a great ball should be held at the city hall of Paris. "Ask the queen to wear her diamond pins," he added softly, "since they are your gift to her." The king did not fully trust the queen, so he agreed at once and told her about the ball. Constance found her in tears and offered to help. "I know a brave young man who would ride to the end of the world for you," she said. That same night, d'Artagnan took a letter from the queen to the duke and some money for the journey.

Treville gave the four friends leave, and they set off with their servants before dawn. At an inn in the first town, a stranger picked a quarrel with Porthos, who stayed behind to settle it. Further on, a group of men attacked the riders on the road, and Aramis had to stay behind. At the next inn, the owner falsely accused Athos of paying with bad money, and armed men rushed in to seize him. Athos held them back so that his friend could escape, and d'Artagnan rode on with a heavy heart. Only he and Planchet reached Calais.

There they discovered that no ship could leave without a written permission signed by the Cardinal. By luck, a gentleman who was also traveling to London carried exactly such a paper. The young man stopped him on the road outside the town and challenged him. He won the duel and took the gentleman's paper. A few hours later, he and Planchet were crossing the sea.

In London, the duke received the queen's letter and led his visitor straight to a small, private room. He opened a jewel box, and suddenly his face went pale. "Two of the pins are gone!" he cried. After a moment he remembered his last ball and the beautiful lady who had stood so close to him. It must have been Milady. At once, he ordered every port in the country to be closed, so that no news of his loss could reach Paris. His best jeweler worked day and night in the palace. In two days, two new pins lay in the box, and no one could tell them from the others. The duke put the twelve pins into d'Artagnan's hands and arranged fresh horses for his journey home.

The young man rode almost without stopping, and he reached Paris on the very day of the ball. He gave the box to Constance, and she carried it to the queen just in time.

That evening the city hall glowed with thousands of candles, and all of Paris seemed to be there. The king arrived first, and then the queen entered, calm and smiling, with the twelve diamond pins on her shoulder. Later he handed the king a small box and whispered, "Ask the queen where she lost these two." The king went to his wife and counted the pins on her shoulder. There were twelve. For a moment the Cardinal's face showed his anger, but he quickly hid it behind a smile. "These two were meant as my own gift to the queen," he said, and the king believed him.

Late that night Constance led d'Artagnan to a small room with a curtain across one wall. A beautiful white hand appeared through the curtain, and he knew at once whose hand it was. He bowed deeply, and when the hand disappeared, a fine diamond ring lay in his palm. A few days later he rode back along the road to look for his friends. He found Porthos, Aramis and Athos one by one, all safe and cheerful, and the four of them returned to Paris together.

## Level C1

In April of sixteen twenty-five, the towns along the road to Paris were treated to a curious sight. A young man of eighteen rode through them on an elderly yellow horse, a beast so comical that shopkeepers came to their doors to laugh. His name was d'Artagnan, and he had come from Gascony with a light purse, a proud heart and his father's parting advice. "Swallow no insult from any man," the old gentleman had told him, "except the king and the Cardinal." What he wanted most in the world was to serve among the king's musketeers, the boldest and most quarrelsome soldiers in the kingdom.

Treville, the captain of the musketeers, had been a friend of d'Artagnan's father in their youth, and he received the young man warmly. Leaving in a great hurry, the young man made three mistakes in a single morning. On the stairs he collided with Athos, a musketeer of serious and noble bearing, who coldly demanded satisfaction. At the street door he blundered into Porthos and became tangled in the giant's enormous cloak. In his struggle to escape, he discovered the cloak's purpose: Porthos's magnificent belt was gold in front and plain leather behind, and its owner did not forgive the discovery. Finally, meaning only to be polite, he picked up a lady's embroidered handkerchief that Aramis had been carefully hiding under his boot, in full view of Aramis's friends. Before eleven o'clock the young man, not yet a day in Paris, had three duels to fight: Athos at noon, Porthos at one, and Aramis at two.

The meeting place was a bare field behind an old convent, and Athos was already there when d'Artagnan arrived. What followed was a lesson in how small Paris could be, for the two companions Athos had brought as witnesses turned out to be Porthos and Aramis. The young man bowed to all three with perfect courtesy, remarking that he was sorry to have only one life to offer three such gentlemen. Before the first duel could begin, however, five of the Cardinal's guards came round the corner of the convent. Their leader ordered the three friends to surrender, and he advised the stranger from Gascony to go home while he still could. He could walk away a free man, or he could throw in his lot with three strangers who, an hour earlier, had been planning to fight him. "Gentlemen," he said, "you are only three, but if you will have me, we shall be four." The fight that followed was brief, and when it was over, the guards marched away with their pride in pieces and nothing worse to show for it.

The king, who adored his musketeers and enjoyed nothing so much as a defeat for his minister's guards, sent for the young Gascon and gave him forty gold coins. From that day the four were inseparable: Athos, silent and noble; Porthos, loud and vain; Aramis, gentle and secretive; and d'Artagnan, the quickest mind among them. Porthos found the young man a servant, the clever and practical Planchet, and d'Artagnan rented rooms above the shop of a timid merchant named Bonacieux. Bonacieux's young wife, Constance, was the queen's most trusted maid. When the Cardinal's men tried to arrest her one evening in her own home, d'Artagnan drove them off, and he fell in love with her on the spot.

It was through Constance that he learned the secret on which the queen's good name now depended. Some months earlier, the king had given his wife, Anne of Austria, twelve diamond pins. She, rather unwisely, had passed them on to the duke of Buckingham as a token of friendship before he returned to London. Cardinal Richelieu, whose spies seemed to hear even the whispers behind closed doors, learned of the gift within days. He wrote at once to Milady, a beautiful woman whose charm was matched only by her lack of scruples, and who lived, conveniently, in London. At a ball some weeks later, she drifted close to the duke, admired his costume, and slipped away with two of the twelve pins.

Then the Cardinal set his trap. He suggested to the king, as if the idea had only just occurred to him, that the city of Paris should give a grand ball in its city hall. "It would be charming," he added, "if the queen wore the diamond pins you gave her." The king, already suspicious, seized on the suggestion and informed his wife that he expected to see the pins at the ball. It was Constance who found her in tears and who, without hesitation, offered the services of a young man she trusted completely. That night d'Artagnan received a letter in the queen's own hand, addressed to the duke, along with a bag of money for the road. He left the house without a word to Bonacieux, which was fortunate, because the timid merchant had already sold himself to the Cardinal.

Treville granted the four friends leave, and they rode out of Paris before dawn with their servants, each of them aware that the Cardinal would not let them pass unchallenged. At the first inn, a stranger provoked Porthos into a quarrel, and the big man stayed behind to settle it with his usual noise. A few hours later, a group of men working by the roadside suddenly attacked the riders, and Aramis, unable to continue, was left at a village inn to rest. At the next town, an innkeeper accused Athos of paying in false coins, and armed men burst in to seize him. Athos held them back with his customary calm and told d'Artagnan to ride on without him. So it was that only d'Artagnan and Planchet, exhausted and covered in dust, rode into Calais.

There a new obstacle awaited them, for no ship could sail without a written permission from the Cardinal himself. Fortune, which had been unkind all week, now produced a gentleman who was carrying precisely such a paper and who was in a great hurry to use it. D'Artagnan followed him out of town, provoked him into a duel, won it, and rode back to the harbor with the precious document in his pocket. By nightfall, he and Planchet were out on the open sea.

The duke of Buckingham received the queen's letter as though it were a holy relic, and he led his visitor at once to a private room where the jewel box was kept. He lifted the lid, admired the pins, and then, with a sudden cry, began to count them again. Two were missing. For a long moment he stood in silence, and then he remembered his last ball and the charming lady who had lingered so close to him. "Milady," he murmured, "the Cardinal's creature." Every port in the country was closed by his order, so that no messenger could carry the news to Paris. His jeweler was brought to the palace, provided with everything he needed, and told that he would not leave until the work was done. Two days later, two new pins lay beside the ten old ones, so perfectly matched that even the duke could not tell which were which. He placed the box in d'Artagnan's hands, and he arranged a chain of fresh horses along the road from the coast to Paris.

The young man rode as if the whole kingdom depended on him, which, in a sense, it did. He reached Paris on the morning of the ball, more asleep than awake, and placed the box in the hands of Constance, who carried it to the queen in secret.

That evening the city hall blazed with candles, and the whole court crowded into its great rooms to dance. When the queen appeared, calm and smiling, the diamond pins sparkled on her shoulder, and the Cardinal, who believed she could have no more than ten, allowed himself a small smile. Choosing his moment, he handed the king a little box containing two pins and murmured, "Ask the queen where she lost these two." The king walked straight to his wife and counted the stones on her shoulder with the care of a man counting money. There were twelve. The Cardinal was far too clever to let his disappointment show for more than a moment. "I wished to give the queen two more pins," he said smoothly, "but I did not dare to offer them myself." The queen thanked him with a smile that told him, more clearly than any words, which of them had won.

Late that night Constance led d'Artagnan along a dark corridor to a small room closed off by a heavy curtain. A white hand appeared through the curtain, and he understood at once whose hand it was. He bowed low over it, and when it withdrew, a diamond ring lay in his palm. A few days later, with Treville's blessing, he rode back along the road to Calais to look for the friends he had left behind. He found Porthos, Aramis and Athos one by one, each with a story to tell and all of them safe. Then the four friends rode home to Paris side by side, as noisy and as cheerful as if the whole affair had been a holiday.
