# The Monkey King Makes Havoc in Heaven

Summary: A clever monkey born from a stone learns great magic and turns Heaven upside down.

## Level A1

A big stone sat on a mountain. One morning, the stone broke open. A little stone monkey jumped out! He played with the other monkeys.

The monkeys found a waterfall. The monkeys asked, "Who can jump through?" The stone monkey jumped through it. Behind the water was a dry cave. The happy monkeys moved into Water Curtain Cave. They called their friend the Monkey King.

Many years went by. Then the Monkey King felt sad. "All of us grow old," he said. He wanted to live forever. So he sailed away to find a teacher.

His teacher was a wise man, Subhuti. Subhuti gave the monkey a new name. The name was Sun Wukong. Wukong learned seventy-two magic changes. He could become a tree or a bird. He could fly far on a cloud.

Next, Wukong visited the Dragon King. "Give me a weapon," said Wukong. There he found a huge iron staff. It grew big or small for him. Wukong kept it in his ear.

The Jade Emperor asked Wukong to Heaven. He gave Wukong a job with the horses. But it was the smallest job there! Angry Wukong went home. Heaven sent an army, but Wukong won.

Then Wukong looked after the magic peaches. These peaches gave a very long life. Wukong ate the best ones. He ate Lao Tzu's magic pills too. So the Jade Emperor asked Buddha for help.

Buddha held out his hand. "Jump out of my hand. Then Heaven is yours," said Buddha. Wukong laughed and jumped onto his cloud. He flew to the end of the world. He saw five tall pillars there. He wrote his name on one pillar.

"I win!" said Wukong. Buddha showed him one finger. Wukong's name was on it! He was still in Buddha's hand. Buddha turned his hand into a mountain. It came down on top of the monkey. This was Five Elements Mountain. Wukong stayed under it for five hundred years.

## Level A2

A magic stone sat on top of Flower Fruit Mountain. One day it broke open, and a stone monkey stepped out.

One day, the monkeys found a big waterfall. The stone monkey jumped through the falling water. Behind it, he found a dry cave with stone beds. He named it Water Curtain Cave, and the monkeys moved in. They bowed to him and called him the Monkey King.

Years later, at a big feast, he suddenly began to cry. "Some day we all grow old," said the Monkey King. He wanted to find a way to live forever. So he made a small boat and sailed across the sea.

After a long search, he found a wise teacher, Subhuti. Subhuti gave him a new name, Sun Wukong. Wukong learned seventy-two ways to change his shape. He also learned to ride on the clouds. One day, he turned into a pine tree to show off. The other students laughed, but Subhuti was angry. "Go home, and never tell anyone about me," said Subhuti.

Back home, Wukong wanted a good weapon. He swam down to the Dragon King's palace in the sea. There he found a huge iron pillar with gold at each end. "Smaller!" said Wukong, and the pillar became a staff. It grew as big as a mountain. It also became as small as a needle. Wukong kept it in his ear. The unhappy Dragon King complained to the Jade Emperor.

The Jade Emperor invited Wukong to Heaven and gave him a job. Wukong looked after the horses of Heaven. Then he learned the truth: it was the lowest job there. Wukong was angry, so he flew home. He called himself "the great sage, equal to Heaven." Heaven sent an army, but Wukong won every fight.

So the Jade Emperor gave him a new job. Now Wukong looked after the peach garden of Heaven. Just one of these peaches gave a very long life. Wukong secretly ate the biggest and best ones. He also found Lao Tzu's magic pills and ate them all.

Then the Jade Emperor sent his best fighter, Erlang. Wukong and Erlang changed into birds, fish, and many other things. At last, Lao Tzu dropped a magic ring on Wukong's head. Wukong fell over, and the soldiers caught him. But nothing in Heaven could hurt him, and soon he broke free.

The Jade Emperor asked Buddha for help. Buddha held out his hand. "Jump out of my hand. Then you can rule Heaven," said Buddha. Wukong laughed and jumped onto his cloud. He flew and flew until he saw five tall pillars. He wrote his name on the middle pillar and flew back.

"I went to the end of the world!" said Wukong. Buddha smiled and showed him one finger. There was Wukong's name, in his own writing. He never left Buddha's hand at all. Then Buddha turned his hand into a mountain. It came down over Wukong and held him fast. This was Five Elements Mountain. The Monkey King stayed under it for five hundred years.

## Level B1

On top of Flower Fruit Mountain sat a very old magic stone. One morning the stone cracked open, and out came a monkey made of stone. Soon he was playing with the other monkeys.

One hot afternoon the monkeys followed a stream to a great waterfall. "Whoever goes through that water and comes back can be our king!" they shouted. The stone monkey closed his eyes and jumped straight through. Behind the falling water was a dry, bright cave full of stone beds and bowls. He named it Water Curtain Cave and led all the monkeys inside. From that day on, they called him the Monkey King.

For hundreds of years the Monkey King lived happily with his people. But one day, at a feast, he began to cry. "Today we are happy, but some day we will all grow old," he said. An old monkey told him that a few wise men knew the secret of long life. So the Monkey King built a small boat and set out alone across the sea.

After nearly ten years, he found a teacher called Subhuti. Subhuti took him as a student and gave him a new name, Sun Wukong. In secret, the teacher showed him seventy-two ways to change his shape. He also taught him to ride the clouds, thousands of miles in a single jump. One evening, Wukong turned himself into a pine tree to impress the other students. Subhuti was not pleased at all. "Showing off brings nothing but trouble. Go home, and never tell anyone that I was your teacher," said Subhuti.

Back on his mountain, Wukong wanted a weapon that was worthy of his powers. He dived into the sea and visited the palace of the Dragon King. The Dragon King showed him one weapon after another, but they were all too light. At last the dragon queen remembered a huge iron pillar that kept the sea steady. When Wukong touched it, it shrank until it was the right size for his hand. Now he could make it as big as a mountain or as small as a needle. He put the tiny staff into his ear and went home laughing. Behind him, the angry Dragon King sent a complaint up to Heaven.

The Jade Emperor did not want a war, so he invited the monkey to Heaven. He gave Wukong a job with a fine title, and Wukong was delighted. For two weeks he cared for the horses of Heaven, and they grew fat. Then he discovered that his job was the lowest in Heaven. In a rage, he flew straight home. There he called himself "the great sage, equal to Heaven." Heaven sent an army to bring him back, but Wukong defeated every general.

So the Jade Emperor gave him the title he wanted, and a new job. Wukong was to look after the peach garden of Heaven. Just one of these peaches gave a very long life. Wukong ate the best of them in secret. Soon he learned about a great peach feast, but nobody had invited him. Angry, he went to the feast hall before the guests and ate the finest food. Then he wandered into Lao Tzu's house and swallowed all his magic pills. In deep trouble now, he hurried home.

This time Heaven sent its bravest fighter, Erlang, with a huge army. For days, Erlang and Wukong kept changing shape. When Wukong became a sparrow, Erlang became a hawk. When Wukong became a fish, Erlang turned into a hungry water bird. At last Wukong became a little temple, with his tail up like a flagpole. Then Lao Tzu dropped a magic ring from the clouds, and it knocked Wukong over. The soldiers caught him and took him back to Heaven. But after all those peaches and pills, nothing could hurt him. Soon he broke free and ran wild through the palace of Heaven.

The Jade Emperor sent a message to Buddha, who came at once. Buddha held out his right hand, which was no bigger than a leaf. "Jump out of my hand, and you may rule Heaven," said Buddha. Wukong laughed at such an easy bet. He jumped onto his cloud and flew farther than ever before. At last, he saw five tall pink pillars standing in the mist. "This must be the end of the world," he said to himself. On the middle pillar he wrote, "The great sage was here."

Wukong flew back and landed proudly in Buddha's hand. "I went to the end of the world and back," he said. "Look at my finger," Buddha replied gently. There, on the middle finger, were Wukong's own words. He had never left Buddha's hand at all. Then Buddha turned his hand into a great mountain with five peaks. It came down over the monkey and held him tight. Its name was Five Elements Mountain, and there the Monkey King stayed for five hundred years.

## Level B2

On the top of Flower Fruit Mountain, near the eastern sea, there stood a stone of great age. Since the beginning of the world it had been drinking in the light of the sun and the moon. One day it split open, and out came a stone egg, which the wind shaped into a monkey. When he opened his eyes, two beams of golden light rose up to the palace of Heaven. The Jade Emperor noticed, but he decided that a stone monkey was nothing to worry about.

The young monkey played with the others, eating wild fruit and sleeping in the trees. One hot day they followed a stream up to a roaring waterfall. "Whoever can pass through that water and come back safely shall be our king," the monkeys agreed. The stone monkey shut his eyes and leaped straight through. Behind the water he found a dry, peaceful cave, with stone beds, stone bowls and an iron bridge. He brought everyone inside, and they bowed to him as the Monkey King of Water Curtain Cave.

He ruled happily for three or four hundred years. Then, at one feast, he suddenly began to weep. "Today we answer to no one," he explained, "but one day old age will take us all." An old ape told him that a few wise beings had escaped old age, and that they lived among men. The next morning, the Monkey King built a raft of pine logs and sailed off alone to find them.

After nearly ten years of wandering, he reached the home of a master called Subhuti. The master accepted him as a student and gave him a new name, Sun Wukong. One night Subhuti secretly taught him the seventy-two ways of changing shape. He also learned the cloud somersault, a single jump that carried him thousands of miles. One day the other students begged him to show off, and he turned himself into a tall pine tree. They clapped and cheered, but the noise brought Subhuti out of his room. "A gift like yours makes others jealous, and showing it off only brings trouble. Go home now, and never say that I was your teacher," said Subhuti sternly.

Back on Flower Fruit Mountain, Wukong began to train his monkeys, but he still had no proper weapon. He dived into the eastern sea and called on the Dragon King in his underwater palace. The Dragon King offered him one heavy weapon after another, but each felt as light as a feather. At last the dragon queen remembered a great iron pillar that had once been used to measure the sea. It was as thick as a barrel and as tall as a tower, with a band of gold at each end. The moment Wukong touched it, the pillar began to glow and shrink until it was exactly the right size. From then on it obeyed his every word, growing as big as a mountain or as small as a needle. He tucked it into his ear and swam home in high spirits. Behind him, the Dragon King was already writing a long complaint to the Jade Emperor.

Rather than fight, the Jade Emperor invited the troublesome monkey to Heaven and gave him an official post. For half a month, Wukong cared for the heavenly horses so well that they grew fat. Then, at a party, his fellow officers let slip that his post was the lowest in Heaven. Furious, Wukong marched out through the southern gate and rode a cloud back home. There he raised a great banner that called him "the great sage, equal to Heaven." Heaven sent an army to fetch him, but Wukong defeated its generals one by one.

In the end, Heaven decided it was easier to give him the empty title, along with a new job. He was put in charge of the peach garden, whose fruit ripened only once in thousands of years and gave endless life. Whenever the gardeners looked away, Wukong ate the ripest ones. Then he learned that a great peach feast was being held, and that he was not on the guest list. Insulted, he put the servants to sleep with a spell and ate the finest dishes before the guests arrived. Wandering off afterwards, he found himself in Lao Tzu's house, where the old sage kept his pills of long life. He swallowed every one of them, and only then did he realize what he had done. Before anyone could catch him, he slipped out of Heaven and hurried back to his mountain.

This time a huge army came down from the sky, led by the brave warrior Erlang. Wukong and Erlang fought for days, and both kept changing shape in a strange game of chase. When Wukong became a sparrow, Erlang became a hawk. When the monkey became a fish, Erlang became a water bird. Finally, Wukong turned into a little temple, but he could not hide his tail, which stood up like a flagpole. Watching from a cloud, Lao Tzu dropped his magic ring, and it knocked the monkey over. The soldiers carried him back to Heaven. After all those peaches and pills, however, nothing could harm him. Lao Tzu shut him inside the great furnace where he made his pills, hoping to melt away his magic. After forty-nine days the lid was lifted, and Wukong sprang out unhurt, with eyes that now shone like gold. He pulled the staff from his ear and ran wild through the halls of Heaven.

In despair, the Jade Emperor sent for Buddha. Buddha listened calmly to the monkey's boasting and then offered him a contest. "If you can jump out of my right hand, you may rule Heaven," said Buddha. Wukong laughed at such an easy bet, since Buddha's hand was no bigger than a lotus leaf. He leaped onto his cloud and flew farther than ever before. At last, five pink pillars appeared in the mist. Sure that he had reached the edge of the world, he wrote on the middle pillar, "The great sage was here."

Then he flew back and landed proudly in Buddha's palm to claim his prize. "You never left my hand," said Buddha with a smile, and he lifted his hand for the monkey to see. There, on the middle finger, in Wukong's own writing, were the words from the edge of the world. The five pillars had been nothing but Buddha's five fingers. Before the monkey could leap away, Buddha turned his hand over and changed it into a mountain. This was Five Elements Mountain, and it came down over the Monkey King and held him fast. The gods held a great feast to thank Buddha, and Heaven was peaceful once more. As for Wukong, he stayed under the mountain for five hundred years.

## Level C1

On the summit of Flower Fruit Mountain, high above the eastern sea, there sat a stone that had been drinking in the light of the sun and moon since the world began. One morning it split open with a crack like thunder, and from the stone egg inside it the wind shaped a monkey, complete in every limb.

The stone monkey grew up among the other monkeys, eating wild fruit and sleeping wherever night happened to find him. One sweltering afternoon the troop followed a stream to its source and came upon a waterfall that hung from the cliff like a curtain of white silk. Whoever dared to pass through it and return unharmed, they agreed, would be their king. The stone monkey did not hesitate for a moment; he crouched, sprang, and vanished into the roar. Behind the falling water lay a dry and spacious cave, furnished as if someone had been expecting him, with stone beds, stone bowls and an iron bridge. He led the whole troop inside, and that day they bowed before him as the Monkey King of Water Curtain Cave.

Three or four hundred carefree years went by, until one evening, in the middle of a feast, the Monkey King burst into tears. "We bow to no king and fear no beast," he told them, "but old age is a master none of us can escape." An old ape spoke up: there were, he had heard, a few sages in the world who had learned the secret of escaping old age, and they lived among men. The next morning the Monkey King tied a raft together from dry pine trunks and pushed off alone into the great sea.

For nearly ten years he wandered through the lands of men, copying their manners and finding most of them too busy chasing money to care about living forever. At last a woodcutter pointed him to the cave of the master Subhuti. The old master studied the scruffy visitor, asked him where he came from, and gave him a name: Sun Wukong, the one awake to emptiness. For years Wukong swept floors and fetched water, until one day Subhuti tapped him three times on the head and walked off with his hands behind his back. Wukong alone understood the riddle, and in the third watch of the night he crept through the back door to the master's bed. There he was taught the secret of long life, the seventy-two transformations, and the cloud somersault, a single leap that could carry him thousands of miles. One afternoon, to win the applause of his fellow students, he turned himself into a pine tree in the courtyard. The cheering brought Subhuti out at once. "A gift put on display is a gift others will envy, and envy always ends in trouble. Go home, and should you ever come to grief, never let my name pass your lips," said Subhuti, and he sent the monkey away that same day.

Back home, Wukong began turning his monkeys into an army, but no weapon on earth was heavy enough for his own hands. So he plunged into the eastern sea and called on the Dragon King, who brought out one great weapon after another, only to watch the monkey twirl each one like a chopstick. It was the dragon queen who finally suggested the iron pillar once used to measure the depth of the sea. It was as thick as a barrel, with a band of gold at either end, and under Wukong's hand it began to glow and shrink. From that moment it answered to his voice, swelling to the height of a mountain or shrinking to an embroidery needle that he kept tucked into his ear. The Dragon King, robbed of his treasure and his peace, sat down to write a complaint to the Jade Emperor. Shortly afterwards Wukong paid a visit to the dim halls beneath the earth, where the register of every creature's years was kept. He took up a brush, struck out his own name and those of all his monkeys, and strolled home, leaving a second complaint to travel up to Heaven.

The Jade Emperor, preferring a tame monkey to a war, invited Wukong up to Heaven and gave him a post with a grand-sounding title. For a fortnight Wukong threw himself into the work, caring for the heavenly horses so devotedly that they grew sleek and fat. Then, at a little party, his fellow officers let slip that his post was the very lowest in Heaven. Wukong overturned his desk, took the staff from his ear, and stormed out through the southern gate, back to his mountain. There he hoisted a banner proclaiming himself "the great sage, equal to Heaven," and invited anyone who disagreed to come and say so. A heavenly king and his fierce young son duly arrived at the head of an army, and Wukong sent them all home with nothing worse than bruised pride.

Heaven decided that an empty title was cheaper than another war, so Wukong got his title and, to keep his idle hands busy, the care of the peach garden. These were no ordinary peaches: the finest ripened only once in nine thousand years, and a single one could make a mortal as lasting as the sky. Placing a monkey in charge of them was, as everyone later agreed, not the wisest decision ever made in Heaven. Whenever the gardeners turned their backs, Wukong shrank himself to the size of a thumb and ate his way through the ripest fruit. Then came news of a grand peach banquet given by the great lady of Heaven, to which every god and sage had been invited except the one who guarded the peaches. Stung in his pride, Wukong froze the servants in place with a spell and helped himself to the finest dishes before a single guest arrived. Wandering off with a full stomach and an empty head, he drifted into the house of Lao Tzu, where five jars of pills of long life stood waiting. He ate the lot like roasted beans, and only then did it dawn on him how much trouble he was in. Then he fled home to his mountain.

This time Heaven sent a hundred thousand soldiers, and when even they could not take the mountain, the brave warrior Erlang was called in to help. When Wukong became a sparrow, Erlang became a hawk; when the monkey dived into a stream as a fish, Erlang followed as a fishing bird. In the end Wukong turned himself into a little roadside temple, with his mouth as the door and his eyes as the windows. Unfortunately, he could find nowhere to put his tail, so it stood up behind the temple as a flagpole, which fooled nobody. While Erlang laughed, Lao Tzu, watching from a cloud, let fall his magic ring, which tapped the monkey smartly on the head and toppled him into the arms of the soldiers. Yet once he was back in Heaven, nothing could harm him, for his body was full of the peaches and the pills. Lao Tzu shut him inside the great furnace where he made his pills, hoping to melt the magic out of him. Forty-nine days later the lid was lifted, and out sprang Wukong, unhurt, with eyes that now shone like gold. Pulling the staff from his ear, he ran wild through the halls of Heaven.

In despair, the Jade Emperor sent for Buddha. Buddha came at once, listened patiently to the monkey's boasting, and asked him why he wanted the throne of Heaven. "Kings take turns," Wukong replied cheerfully, "and it is about time Heaven had a new one." Buddha proposed a wager instead. "Leap out of the palm of my right hand, and the throne is yours," he said, "but fail, and you go back to earth to learn some patience." Wukong could hardly believe his luck, for the hand before him was no larger than a lotus leaf, and he had crossed far greater distances before breakfast. He sprang onto his cloud and somersaulted away until the world blurred beneath him and, at last, five pink pillars rose out of the mist. This, surely, was the edge of everything. To leave proof of his visit, he wrote on the middle pillar, in large and confident letters, "The great sage, equal to Heaven, was here."

Then he somersaulted back and landed in Buddha's palm, ready to claim his throne. Buddha simply turned his hand, and there on the middle finger, in Wukong's own writing, was the line he had left at the edge of the world. The five great pillars had been nothing but Buddha's fingers, and in all that dizzy flight the monkey had never once left his palm. Before Wukong could leap away, Buddha turned his hand over and changed it into a mountain of five peaks, which came down over him and held him fast. This was Five Elements Mountain, and only the monkey's head and hands stayed free beneath it. As for Wukong, there he stayed, under the weight of the mountain, for five hundred years.
