# Pandora's Box

Summary: A curious girl cannot stop wondering about a mysterious box that she must never open.

## Level A1

Long ago, the world was a happy place. Nobody was sick, and nobody was sad. Children played in the sun all day. A boy called Epimetheus had a little house. Then Pandora came to live with him.

In the house, Pandora saw a big box. It was dark and very pretty. A gold string was around it. It had a big knot. "What is in the box?" asked Pandora. "I don't know," said Epimetheus. "A man called Quicksilver left it here. We must not open it."

Pandora thought about the box every day. "Where is it from?" she asked. Epimetheus was tired of her questions. "Please stop talking about that box!" he said.

One morning, Epimetheus went out to pick fruit. Pandora stayed at home with the box. She touched the gold string. Suddenly the knot came undone in her hands! Then she heard small voices. "Let us out, Pandora!" said the voices.

Just then, Epimetheus came back. He saw Pandora at the box. He wanted to see inside too. So he did not stop her. Pandora opened the box.

The room went dark. Small, ugly things flew out of the box. They looked like bugs with dark wings. They were the Troubles. One of them stung Epimetheus. "Oh, it hurts!" cried Epimetheus. Another one stung Pandora on her head.

The Troubles flew out of the window. They went all over the world. Now people were sad and angry. Children said unkind words to their friends. Pandora sat on the floor and cried. "It is all my fault," said Pandora.

Then a soft voice came from the box. "Let me out, please!" "Can we open it?" asked Epimetheus. "Yes, let's open it," said Pandora. They opened the box together.

A smiling little girl flew out. Her wings were like a rainbow. "My name is Hope," she said. She touched their stings. The pain went away. "I always stay with you," said Hope. And Hope is still in the world today.

## Level A2

Long ago, the world was a very happy place. Nobody was ever sick, and nobody grew old. Food grew on the trees, so nobody had to work. Children played and sang all day, and they never fought.

Epimetheus was a boy with no mother or father. He lived alone in a little house. Then a girl called Pandora came to live with him. She came from a far country to be his playmate.

Inside the house, Pandora saw a large box. It was dark and shiny, with beautiful faces on every side. On the top, one face had flowers around its head. A golden cord went around the box in a tight knot. "Epimetheus, what is in this box?" asked Pandora. "It is a secret," said Epimetheus. "Then where did it come from?" asked Pandora.

"A man left it at our door before you came," said Epimetheus. "His name is Quicksilver. He laughs a lot, and he wears a cap with little wings. He told me to keep the box safe. We must not open it."

After that, Pandora asked about the box every day. Epimetheus grew tired of her questions. "Let's go and play outside with the other children," he said. But Pandora did not want to play.

One morning, Epimetheus went to pick figs and grapes with his friends. Pandora stayed home alone. She sat by the box and looked at the faces. The face on the top seemed to smile at her. "I only want to look at the knot," she said to herself. She touched the golden cord, and suddenly the knot came undone! "Oh no, I can't tie it again!" cried Pandora. Then she heard tiny voices inside the box. "Let us out, dear Pandora!" they called.

At that moment, Epimetheus came in quietly. He saw Pandora with her hands on the box. He wanted to see inside too, so he said nothing. Pandora lifted the top of the box.

At once, a cloud covered the sun, and the room went dark. Ugly little things with dark wings flew out of the box. They looked like bugs, and they had stings. These were the Troubles of the world. One stung Epimetheus, and he cried out. Another stung Pandora on her forehead.

The Troubles flew out of the open window. Soon the children outside began to fight and cry. The flowers lost their color. Now there was sadness, sickness and anger in the world. Pandora sat on the floor and cried. Epimetheus sat in a corner and did not speak to her.

Then they heard a soft tap from inside the box. "Please let me out!" called a sweet little voice. "Can we open it again?" asked Epimetheus. "Yes, let's open it," said Pandora. Together they lifted the top. A bright, smiling girl flew out, with wings like a rainbow. "My name is Hope," she said. She touched their stings, and the pain went away. "I always stay with you, even in hard times," said Hope. And she is still with us today.

## Level B1

Long ago, when the world was still young, nobody was ever sick or unhappy. Fruit grew on every tree, so nobody needed to work for food. The children of that time spent their days playing and singing in the sunshine. They never fought, and they never cried.

One of these children was a boy called Epimetheus, who had no mother or father. He lived alone in a little cottage until a girl called Pandora arrived. She came from a far country to live with him and be his playmate. The first thing she noticed in the cottage was a large box.

Its dark wood was smooth and shiny. Beautiful faces of men, women and children covered every side of it. In the middle of the top, one face wore a crown of flowers. Instead of a lock, a golden cord went around the box in a tight, clever knot. "Epimetheus, what is inside this box?" Pandora asked. "That's a secret," he answered. "You mustn't ask about it."

"Then tell me where it came from," said Pandora. "A man left it at the door just before you arrived," said Epimetheus. "He was laughing, and he wore a cap with two little wings. In his hand he had a stick with two snakes around it. His name is Quicksilver, and he told me to keep the box safe."

But Pandora couldn't stop thinking about the box. She talked about it in the morning, at lunch and at night. Soon Epimetheus was tired of hearing about it. "Let's go outside and play with the other children," he said. But Pandora always stayed behind with the box.

One morning, Epimetheus went with his friends to pick figs and grapes. Pandora stayed behind alone with the box. As she studied the carved faces, the face with the flowers seemed to smile at her. "I'll just look at the knot," she told herself. "I won't open anything." But as soon as she touched the golden cord, the knot came apart in her fingers. She tried to tie it again, but she couldn't remember how it went. Then she heard tiny voices whispering inside the box. "Let us out, dear Pandora," they begged. "We want to be your playmates!"

At that moment, Epimetheus came back quietly into the cottage. He saw Pandora with her hands on the box, but he didn't say a word. The truth was that he wanted to see inside it just as much as she did. So Pandora lifted the top of the box.

At once, a black cloud moved across the sun, and the room grew dark. A low, angry buzzing filled the air. Out of the box flew a crowd of ugly little creatures with dark wings. Each one had a sharp sting. They were the Troubles: sadness, sickness, anger and many others. One of them stung Epimetheus, who cried out in pain. Another landed on Pandora's forehead and stung her too.

Epimetheus ran to open the window to chase them out. The Troubles flew out into the wide world, where they have stayed ever since. Soon the children outside were quarrelling and crying for the first time. Even the flowers around the cottage began to lose their color. Pandora sat on the floor and cried. Epimetheus sat in a corner with his back to her.

After a while, they heard a gentle tapping from inside the box. "Let me out, please!" called a sweet little voice. "Shall we open it?" Epimetheus asked. "It can't make things any worse," said Pandora. So together they lifted the top once more.

Out flew a smiling little girl with wings that shone like a rainbow. She touched their stings with her fingers, and the pain went away at once. "My name is Hope," she told them. "I was in the box to help people with the Troubles. I will never leave you, even on the darkest days." And Hope has stayed with people all over the world ever since.

## Level B2

Long ago, when the world was as young as a child, there was no such thing as sadness. Nobody fell ill, nobody grew old, and nobody ever had to work, because ripe fruit hung on every tree. There was no need for doctors, since there was nothing in the world to cure. The children of those days spent their time playing and singing, and not one of them had ever quarrelled or cried.

Among these children lived a boy named Epimetheus, who had neither a father nor a mother. So that he would not be lonely, a girl called Pandora was sent from a far country to be his playmate. The very first thing she noticed when she stepped inside was a large and beautiful box.

It was made of a dark, rich wood that had been polished until it shone like a mirror. Faces of men, women, children and smiling creatures had been carved all over it, peeping out between leaves and flowers. In the middle of the lid there was one especially handsome face with a garland of flowers around its head. The box had no lock; instead, it was tied with a golden cord. The cord was twisted into a knot so clever that nobody could see where it began or ended. Pandora's fingers itched to touch it the moment she saw it.

"Epimetheus, what have you got in that box?" Pandora asked. "That's a secret, and you mustn't ask about it," he replied. "I don't know what's inside it myself."

"Then where did it come from?" she insisted. Epimetheus explained that it had been left at the door just before she arrived. The man who brought it had been laughing merrily, and he had worn a cap with two little wings. In his hand he had carried a strange stick with two snakes twisted around it. His name was Quicksilver, and he had asked Epimetheus to look after the box carefully.

Pandora, however, could not leave the subject alone. She talked about the box at breakfast, at dinner and at bedtime. She tried to guess what was inside: toys, perhaps, or sweets, or pretty new clothes. Whenever the box refused to tell her its secret, she called it ugly and boring. Epimetheus grew so tired of it that he began to spend more time outdoors. "Come and play with the others," he would say, but Pandora preferred to stay with the box.

One morning, Epimetheus went off with his friends to gather figs and grapes, and Pandora was left alone in the cottage. She knelt beside the box and studied the carved faces. It seemed to her that the face with the garland was smiling at her in a rather cheeky way. "I'll only look at the knot," she said to herself. "There's no harm in that." Yet the moment her fingers touched the golden cord, the knot fell apart as if by magic. She tried again and again to tie it, but she could not remember how it had gone.

Then, to her surprise, she heard small voices murmuring inside the box. "Let us out, dear Pandora," they whispered. "We want so much to play with you!" The voices sounded so friendly that it seemed unkind to keep them shut up in the dark.

At that very moment, Epimetheus stepped quietly through the door, carrying flowers he had picked to make her a garland. He saw what Pandora was doing, and he could easily have stopped her. But he was secretly just as curious as she was, so he said nothing at all. Pandora took a deep breath and lifted the lid.

Immediately, a black cloud passed over the sun, and the cottage grew dark. A low, angry buzzing filled the room, and a swarm of ugly little creatures rushed out of the box on dark wings. They were the Troubles of the world: sadness, sickness, anger, worry and a great many more, each armed with a sharp sting. One of them stung Epimetheus, who cried out in pain, and another settled on Pandora's forehead and stung her as well. Neither of them had ever felt pain before, and they did not know what to make of it.

Epimetheus threw open the window to drive them away, and the Troubles flew out into the wide world. Soon the children outside were quarrelling and crying for the first time in their lives. Before that day, nobody had ever shed a tear, but now tears seemed to be everywhere. Even the flowers around the cottage drooped and lost their color. Pandora sat on the floor sobbing, while Epimetheus sulked in a corner with his back turned to her. "It's all my fault," she kept saying, and this time he did not argue.

After a while, a gentle tapping came from inside the box. "Please let me out!" called a sweet little voice. "What if it's another Trouble?" asked Epimetheus doubtfully. "It can hardly make things worse," answered Pandora. Epimetheus had to agree, and the two of them lifted the lid together.

Out flew a bright, smiling little figure with wings that shone with all the colors of the rainbow. She touched the stings with her fingertips, and the pain disappeared at once. "Who are you?" asked Pandora, staring at her in wonder. "My name is Hope," the little figure told them. "I was packed into the box to make up for the Troubles, and I will never leave you. Even when you think I have gone, I will come back to you." From that day on, the Troubles have flown around the world, stinging people wherever they go. But whenever someone feels their sting, Hope is never far away, ready to make the pain easier to bear.

## Level C1

Long ago, when the world was as young as a newborn child, there was not a single grief to be found anywhere in it. Nobody fell ill and nobody grew old, and since figs and grapes hung ripe on every branch, nobody had ever heard of work. The children of that golden time filled their days with games and songs. Not one of them had ever quarrelled, and not one had ever crept off into a corner to sulk.

One of those children was a boy named Epimetheus, who had neither father nor mother to look after him. So that he should not be lonely, a little girl called Pandora was sent from a far country to share his cottage and be his playmate. Hardly had she crossed the threshold when her eyes fell on a large box, and from that moment she could think of very little else.

It was, to be fair, a remarkable object. The dark wood had been polished so carefully that Pandora could see her own face in it. All over its sides were carved figures of men, women, children and playful creatures, peeping out from among leaves and flowers. In the center of the lid was a face more striking than the rest, crowned with a garland of flowers. Its expression seemed to change each time she looked at it, now serious, now sly, now almost laughing. The box had no lock or key; it was bound instead with a golden cord, tied in a knot so intricate that nobody could tell where it began or where it ended.

"Epimetheus," Pandora asked on her very first day, "what is in that box?" "That, my dear Pandora, is a secret," he answered, "and you must be kind enough not to ask about it." Then, a little less grandly, he admitted that he did not know himself. This, as anyone might have predicted, only made her more curious than ever.

When she demanded to know where it had come from, he told her that it had been left at the door shortly before her arrival. The person who brought it had been laughing as if the whole world were a joke. On his head he had worn a cap with two small wings fluttering at the sides. In his hand was a curious staff with two snakes twisted around it, which he carried with an air of great importance. His name was Quicksilver, and he had asked Epimetheus, with a look half merry and half serious, to keep the box safe.

None of this satisfied Pandora in the least. She brought up the box at breakfast, returned to it over dinner and murmured about it as she fell asleep. Perhaps it held toys, she wondered aloud, or sweets, or pretty new clothes for her to wear to the children's games. Whenever it refused to give up its secret, she called it ugly and tiresome, though in truth she found it more fascinating every day. Epimetheus, who had never before been bored by anything, found himself growing weary of the subject and spending longer and longer hours out of doors. "Come and play with the others," he would urge her, but Pandora always found a reason to stay behind with the box.

One morning, Epimetheus set off with his friends to gather figs and grapes, leaving Pandora alone in the quiet cottage. Kneeling beside the box, she studied the carved faces. It struck her that the garlanded one in the middle wore a decidedly mischievous smile, as though it knew exactly what she was thinking. "I shall only look at the knot," she assured herself. "Surely there can be no harm in that." Yet the instant her fingers touched the golden cord, the knot came undone of its own accord, as neatly as if some unseen hand had untied it. Again and again she tried to put it back as it had been, but the pattern had vanished from her memory as completely as a dream at sunrise.

It was then that she heard it: a faint murmur of voices from inside the box, like the hum of insects on a summer afternoon. "Let us out, dear Pandora, pray let us out," they whispered sweetly. "We will be the very best of playfellows to you!" So friendly did they sound that keeping them shut up in the dark began to seem almost unkind.

At that unlucky moment, Epimetheus came back, his arms full of flowers he had gathered to make her a garland. He stopped in the doorway and saw at a glance what she was about to do. A single word from him would have been enough to stop her, but the plain truth is that he was quite as curious as she was, and so he held his tongue. Pandora, never guessing that he was watching, drew a long breath and lifted the lid.

Instantly a heavy cloud swept across the sun, and the bright little cottage sank into gloom. There arose a low, angry buzzing, and out of the box poured a swarm of ugly winged creatures, darting about the room on dusky wings. These were the Troubles, every one of them: sorrows and sicknesses, quarrels and worries, and a great many others besides, each with a sharp little sting. One of them settled on Epimetheus and stung him, so that he cried out in pain, while another alighted on Pandora's forehead and stung her before she could brush it away. Neither child had ever felt the smallest pain before, and for a moment they simply stared at each other in bewilderment.

Epimetheus flung open the window to chase them out, and they streamed into the wide world, where they have remained ever since. Before long, the children playing outside were squabbling and weeping for the first time in their lives. Even the flowers around the door began to droop, as though they too had felt something change. Pandora flung herself down on the floor in tears, while Epimetheus sat sulking in a corner with his back turned firmly towards her. "It was all my fault," Pandora kept repeating between her sobs, though Epimetheus, had he been honest, might have admitted that half the blame was his.

They had been sitting like that for some time when a soft tapping sounded from inside the box. "Please let me out!" called a voice so sweet and cheerful that it seemed to belong to another world. "What if it is only another Trouble?" muttered Epimetheus, who had quite forgotten that he was supposed to be sulking. "After all that has happened, it can hardly make matters worse," said Pandora. Epimetheus could not argue with that, and so, side by side, they raised the lid together.

Out fluttered a bright little figure with a sunny smile and wings that glimmered with every color of the rainbow. Without a word, she touched each sting with her fingertips, and the pain melted away as if it had never been. "And who, pray, are you?" asked Pandora, gazing at her through her tears. "My name is Hope," the little stranger replied. "I was packed into the box to make amends for that swarm of Troubles, and I promise that I will never desert you. There may be times when you think I have vanished altogether, but sooner or later you will see the glimmer of my wings again."

And so it has been ever since: the Troubles still fly about the world, stinging whomever they please, but wherever they go, Hope follows close behind.
