# King Midas and the Golden Touch

Summary: A rich king who loves gold more than anything receives a strange and dangerous gift.

## Level A1

King Midas lived in a big palace. He had a lot of gold. But he always wanted more. He had a little daughter, Marygold. She loved flowers, and she loved her father.

Every day, Midas sat in a dark room. He counted his gold there. "Gold is the best thing!" he said. One day, a stranger came in. His face was bright and kind. "You are very rich, King Midas. What do you want now?" asked the stranger. "I want a golden touch! My hand must make gold," said Midas. "It starts tomorrow," said the stranger.

The next morning, the sun came up. Midas touched his bed. It turned to gold! His clothes turned to gold too. Then he ran into the garden. He touched the red roses. Now they were gold. "How wonderful!" cried the king.

Midas sat down for breakfast. Marygold came in with tears in her eyes. "Father, my roses are hard and cold. They have no smell now," said Marygold. The king picked up some bread. It turned to gold. Even the water turned to gold. Poor Midas could not eat.

Marygold saw her sad father. She ran to him. She put her arms around him. At once, she stopped moving. His little girl was a golden statue now! Midas cried and cried.

Then the stranger came back. "Do you like your golden touch?" he asked. "No, I hate it!" cried Midas. "Gold or your daughter?" asked the stranger. "My daughter!" said Midas. "Then go to the river Pactolus. Wash there, and take water home. Pour it on Marygold," said the stranger.

Midas ran to the river and jumped in. His golden touch went away. He took a jar of water home. He poured the water over Marygold. She moved, and she laughed again! Then he poured water on the roses. They were red and soft again. After that day, Midas did not love gold. He loved his daughter most of all.

## Level A2

Long ago, there was a king called Midas. He was very rich, but he loved gold more than anything. He also loved his little daughter, Marygold. He wanted to give her a very big pile of gold.

Every day, the king went to a dark room. It was under his palace. He kept all his treasure there. He counted his coins and looked at his golden cups. "I am rich, but not rich enough," he said.

One afternoon, a young stranger stood in the room. The stranger's face was bright. "You have so much gold, King Midas. Are you happy now?" asked the stranger. "Not really," answered Midas. "I want a golden touch. Everything must turn to gold under my hand," he said. The stranger laughed. "Then you can have it. It starts tomorrow at sunrise," said the stranger. Then he was gone.

The next morning, Midas woke up very early. He touched his bed, and it turned to gold. His clothes became gold too. He put on his glasses, but they turned to gold too. Now he could not see through them! Still, Midas was happy. He ran into the garden and touched every rose. Soon all the roses were bright gold.

Then the king sat down to breakfast. Marygold came in with tears on her face. She had a golden rose in her hand. "Father, look at my rose! It is hard and ugly, and it has no smell," she cried. Midas lifted his cup of coffee. The coffee touched his lips and became hard gold. He tried some fish and a hot cake. They changed too. He was hungry, but he could not eat anything. "I have gold, but nothing to eat," he said sadly.

Marygold saw her father's sad face. She ran to him and put her arms around him. Midas kissed her hair, and at that moment she changed. Her face and her hair turned to gold. Marygold was a golden statue now. Midas cried out in fear.

Suddenly the stranger stood next to him again. "How do you like your golden touch?" asked the stranger. "I hate it!" cried Midas. The stranger asked him a few questions. Was gold better than a cup of cold water? Was it better than a piece of bread? Was it better than his own dear child? "No, no, no!" cried Midas. "Then go and jump into the river Pactolus. Fill a jar with its water and come home. Pour it on your daughter and your roses," said the stranger.

Midas ran to the river and jumped into the water. His touch was normal again. He filled a jar with water and ran back to the palace. He poured some water over Marygold's head. At once, her cheeks were pink again, and she laughed. She looked at her wet dress in surprise. Then Midas poured water on the roses. They became soft and red again.

Only one thing stayed the same. Marygold's hair kept a little golden color. After that day, Midas did not care about gold. The only gold he liked was the color of his daughter's hair.

## Level B1

Long ago, there lived a king called Midas. He was very rich, but he loved gold more than anything else in the world. The only thing he loved more was his little daughter, Marygold. He wanted to leave her the biggest pile of yellow coins in the whole world.

Midas once loved flowers, and he had a beautiful garden full of roses. Now he only thought about how much they would be worth as gold. Every day, he went down to a dark room under his palace to see his treasure. There he counted his coins and smiled at his golden cups. "Midas, you are a happy man," he whispered to himself. But deep inside, he was not happy, because he always wanted more.

One afternoon, while he was counting, a shadow fell across the gold. Midas looked up and saw a young stranger standing in the room. The stranger's face shone with a strange golden light, and his smile was friendly. "You are a rich man, friend Midas," said the stranger. "Yes, but it is not enough," said the king. "I wish everything I touched could turn to gold." The stranger looked at him for a long moment. "Are you sure? You will not be sorry?" he asked. "I am quite sure," said Midas. "Very well. Tomorrow, when the sun rises, you will have the Golden Touch," said the stranger. Then the young man was gone.

Midas hardly slept that night. When the first ray of sunlight came in, he touched the cover of his bed. At once, it turned to heavy, shining gold. His clothes became gold, and so did a book on his table. He put on his glasses to look more closely, but the glass turned to gold. Now he could not see through them at all. "It does not matter," thought Midas. "Marygold's eyes can see for me." He hurried into the garden and touched every rose until the whole garden was gold.

The king came in for breakfast feeling very pleased with himself. Soon Marygold came running in, and she was crying. "Father, something terrible happened to the roses! They are all hard and yellow, and they have no smell at all," she said. Midas felt a little ashamed, but he did not tell her the truth. Instead, he lifted his cup of coffee. As soon as it touched his lips, the coffee became a lump of gold. He tried a piece of fish, and it became a golden fish. A hot cake turned yellow and hard in his fingers. The richest king in the world was hungry, and he could not eat a thing.

Marygold saw how unhappy her father was. She jumped down from her chair and ran to him. She threw her arms around him, and he bent down to kiss her. At that moment, her sweet face changed. Her skin, her hair and even her tears turned to gold. Marygold was now a golden statue, standing quite still in his arms. Midas cried out, and his heart was full of sorrow.

Then he saw the young stranger at the door again. "Well, friend Midas, how do you like the Golden Touch?" asked the stranger. "I am the most unhappy man in the world," said Midas. "Let us see. What is worth more, the Golden Touch or a cup of cold water?" the stranger asked. "Water!" cried Midas. "And the Golden Touch or a piece of bread?" asked the stranger. "Bread is worth all the gold on earth!" said Midas. "And the Golden Touch or your own little Marygold?" asked the stranger. "My child!" cried the king. "I would not give one hair of her head for all the gold in the world." "You are wiser now," said the stranger. "Go and jump into the river Pactolus at the bottom of your garden. Then fill a jar with its water and pour it on everything you want to save."

Midas ran to the river and jumped in without even taking off his shoes. When he came out, his heart felt light and free again. He touched a small flower by the water, and it stayed a flower. He filled a jar and hurried home. He poured the water over the golden statue. Slowly, the color came back into Marygold's cheeks. She began to laugh, and she looked at her wet dress in surprise. She did not remember anything, and Midas did not tell her. Then he took her into the garden, and together they poured water on all the roses. Soon they were soft and red again, and they smelled sweet.

Only one thing stayed the same. Marygold's hair always kept a light golden color. Many years later, Midas liked to tell this story to his grandchildren. "I do not like the color of gold anymore," he told them. "I only like it in your hair."

## Level B2

Once upon a time there lived a very rich king named Midas, who loved gold more than anything else in the world. The only thing he valued even more was his little daughter, Marygold, a cheerful child with bright eyes. Yet even his love for her had become tied up with his love of gold. He believed that the best gift for her was the largest heap of yellow coins ever collected.

In his younger days, Midas had been fond of flowers, and he had planted a large garden full of roses. Now, when he looked at them, he only wondered how much they would be worth if each petal were made of gold. He spent most of his days in a dark room deep under the palace, where his treasure was kept. A single narrow window let in one beam of sunlight, and the king liked to count his coins in that light. He would pour them from bag to bag and look at his face in a golden cup. Then he would whisper, "Oh, Midas, what a lucky man you are!" Still, he never felt that he had enough.

One afternoon, as he was counting, a shadow fell across his treasure. Looking up, Midas saw a young man standing in the sunbeam. He had no idea how the stranger had entered, because the door had been locked. The young man's face seemed to glow with a soft golden light, and his smile was warm and friendly. "You are a wealthy man, friend Midas," said the stranger, looking around the room. "I have done fairly well," replied the king, "but it is very little compared with what I would like." "Then tell me what would satisfy you," said the stranger. Midas thought for a long time before he answered. "I wish that everything I touched would turn to gold." "Are you quite sure you will never be sorry?" he asked. "What could make me sorry?" said Midas. "I ask for nothing more to make me perfectly happy." "Then it shall be as you wish," said the stranger. "Tomorrow, at the first light of the sun, you will have the Golden Touch." A moment later, the room was bright, and the young man had disappeared.

Midas lay awake most of the night, waiting like a child before a holiday. As soon as the first ray of sunlight touched his window, he reached for the cover on his bed. Immediately it became heavy, shining cloth of gold. His clothes turned to gold as he put them on. A book on his table became a heavy golden block that nobody could open. He put on his glasses to see his treasures better, but they changed as well. Now he could not see through them at all. "It is only a small problem," he told himself. "Marygold's young eyes can see for me." Then he hurried into the garden and touched every rose bush, one after another, until the whole garden glittered.

By the time he came in to breakfast, the king was in a wonderful mood. A moment later, however, Marygold came into the room, crying as if her heart would break. In her hand she held one of the roses, which had become hard and yellow. "Father, what happened to our beautiful roses? They are all spoiled, and they don't smell sweet anymore!" she sobbed. Midas felt ashamed, but he did not tell her what he had done. Then he lifted his cup of coffee. The moment the coffee touched his lips, it turned into a lump of gold. He reached for a small fish on his plate, but it became a stiff golden fish, like a pretty ornament. A hot cake, an egg and a potato all changed in the same way. Midas looked at the rich breakfast and realized that he might be the hungriest king who ever lived.

Seeing her father so unhappy, Marygold forgot about her roses. She slipped from her chair, ran to him, and threw her arms lovingly around him. Midas bent down and kissed her, and at that very moment the change began. Her rosy face, her soft curls and even the tears on her cheeks became shining gold. Marygold stood in his arms as a golden statue, with a look of love and worry frozen on her face. The king cried out in sorrow, wishing he were the poorest man alive if only his child could come back.

When he looked up, the stranger was standing by the door. "Well, friend Midas, how are you enjoying the Golden Touch?" he asked. "I am the most miserable of men," the king answered. "Then let us see what you have learned," said the stranger. "Which is worth more, the Golden Touch or a cup of clear, cold water?" "Oh, the water!" said Midas. "I will never be able to drink again." "And the Golden Touch, or a simple piece of bread?" asked the stranger. "A piece of bread is worth all the gold on earth," Midas replied. "And the Golden Touch, or your own little Marygold, warm and loving, as she was an hour ago?" asked the stranger. "My child, my dear child!" cried Midas. "I would not have given one curl of her hair for all the gold in the world." "You are wiser than you were," said the stranger kindly. "Go and jump into the river Pactolus, which flows past the bottom of your garden. Fill a jar with its water and sprinkle it over anything you wish to change back."

He ran to the river and jumped into the water without even removing his shoes. As he climbed out, he felt as if a heavy weight had been lifted from his heart. To test his hand, he touched a small purple flower on the bank, and to his great joy it stayed a flower. He filled his jar and hurried back to the palace. With trembling hands, he sprinkled the water over the golden statue of his daughter. Little by little, the pink returned to her cheeks, and she began to laugh. She was very surprised to find her dress wet, and she had no memory of what had happened. Her father decided never to tell her. Together they went into the garden and sprinkled water on all the roses, until they were soft, red and sweet again.

Only two things remained to remind Midas of the Golden Touch. The sand of the river Pactolus sparkled like gold from that day on. Marygold's hair, too, kept a faint golden color that it had never had before. Years later, as an old man, Midas would sit with his grandchildren on his knees and tell them this story. "Since that morning," he would say, stroking their bright curls, "I have hated the color of gold, except in your hair."

## Level C1

There once lived a very rich king named Midas, and he had a little daughter whose name, as you will see, suited her rather too well: it was Marygold. King Midas loved gold more than anything else in the world, and if there was one thing he valued above it, that thing was his cheerful, golden-haired child. Yet the more he loved Marygold, the more he wanted gold, for the foolish man had persuaded himself that no gift could equal the largest heap of coins in the world.

In earlier and wiser days, Midas had been a great lover of flowers, and he had planted a rose garden that was the envy of every king in the land. Now, however, when he walked among the roses, he only calculated how much they would be worth if every petal were beaten into gold. Most of his hours were spent in a dark, damp room beneath the palace, which he called his treasure-room and where he allowed no one else to enter. A single narrow window let in one dusty beam of sunlight. In that thin light the king liked to pour coins from one bag into another and admire his face in a polished golden cup. "Oh, Midas, rich King Midas," he would murmur, "what a happy man you are!" It was a strange sort of happiness, though, for no matter how high the heap grew, he never once felt that it was high enough.

One afternoon, as he sat counting, a shadow fell across his treasure, and when he looked up he found a young man standing in the beam of light. How the stranger had come in, Midas could not tell, since he himself had turned the key in the lock. There was something about the visitor's face, a warm golden glow and a smile of quiet amusement, that made the king suspect he was no ordinary traveler. "You are a wealthy man, friend Midas," the stranger remarked, letting his eyes wander over the shining piles. "I have done fairly well," the king admitted, "but it is a mere trifle when you consider what I might have, if only I had more time." "So you are not satisfied?" said the stranger. "Then tell me what would content you."

Midas pondered the question for a long while, unwilling to waste such a chance on too modest a request. At last, he raised his head. "I wish," he said, "that everything I touch might be changed into gold." The stranger's smile broadened until it seemed to flood the gloomy room with sunshine. "The Golden Touch," he said. "Are you quite certain, friend Midas, that you will never regret it?" "What could I possibly regret?" the king replied. "I ask for nothing else to make me perfectly happy." "Then it shall be yours," said the stranger. "Tomorrow, with the first ray of the rising sun, you will find yourself gifted with the Golden Touch." There was a sudden flash of brightness, and when Midas opened his eyes again, he was alone.

He scarcely slept that night, tossing in his bed like a boy on the eve of a festival. The moment the first sunbeam crept through the window, he laid his hand on the cover of his bed, which at once grew heavy and stiff with glittering gold. Wild with delight, the king leapt up and rushed about the room, touching everything within reach. His clothes turned to gold as he pulled them on, and a book lying on his table became a solid golden block that no one would ever read again. When he put on his glasses to examine his new treasures more closely, the lenses too became gold, and he discovered that he could see nothing through them at all. "A small inconvenience," he told himself, refusing to be troubled. "Marygold's young eyes will serve me well enough." Then, unable to wait, he hurried into the garden and went from bush to bush, touching every rose until the whole garden blazed under the morning sky.

It was a very contented king who sat down to breakfast, but his good spirits did not last long. Marygold soon came in, weeping as though her heart would break, and in her hand was one of the roses she had loved, now hard, cold and yellow. "Oh, Father, look what has happened to all our beautiful roses! They are spoiled, every one of them, and they have no scent at all!" she sobbed. Ashamed, though unwilling to confess, Midas told her not to cry over such a trifle and promised her something far more precious in return. Then he raised his cup of coffee to his lips, and no sooner had it touched them than it hardened into a lump of solid gold. He reached for a little fish on his plate; it became a stiff golden ornament, every scale perfectly shaped and perfectly useless. A hot cake, an egg and a potato followed it, each turning to metal the instant his fingers closed around it. Gazing at a breakfast fit for an emperor, the richest king on earth began to understand that he might well be the hungriest man who had ever lived.

Seeing her father's distress, Marygold forgot her own sorrow at once. She slipped down from her chair, ran to his side, and threw her arms around him with all the tenderness of a loving child. Midas bent to kiss her, and in that same moment he felt the change take place beneath his lips. Her rosy cheeks, her soft brown curls and even the tears still shining on her face became gold. The little girl stood in his arms as a golden statue, her face fixed in an expression of love and anxious pity. Of all the sorrows that had ever come to a father, it seemed to Midas that his was the heaviest, for it was his own wish that had done this. He would gladly have become the poorest beggar in the kingdom if only the warmth could have returned to his daughter's cheeks.

When at last he raised his head, the young stranger was standing in the doorway, looking at him with a serious and gentle face. "Well, friend Midas," he said, "how do you find the Golden Touch?" "I am the most wretched of men," answered the king. "Indeed?" said the stranger. "Then let us see what you have learned. Which is worth more, the Golden Touch or a cup of clear, cold water?" "Oh, the water!" cried Midas. "It will never again wet my dry throat." "The Golden Touch, or a crust of bread?" the stranger went on. "A single crust is worth all the gold in the world," Midas answered. "The Golden Touch, or your own little Marygold, warm and soft and loving, as she was an hour ago?" the stranger asked at last. "My child, my dear child!" the king exclaimed, wringing his hands. "I would not have given one curl of her hair for the power to turn this whole earth into gold." "You are wiser than you were, King Midas," said the stranger, and his voice was kind. "Go, then, and plunge into the river Pactolus, which runs past the bottom of your garden. Fill a jar with its water and sprinkle it over whatever you wish to restore to its former state."

Midas did not wait to hear another word. He ran through the garden and leapt into the river, so eager that he never paused to take off his shoes. As he came up out of the water, he felt as though a heavy weight had been lifted from his chest. His heart, which had been growing hard and cold, seemed to become warm again. Near the bank grew a small purple flower, and when he touched it, trembling, it stayed exactly as it was, a modest living thing rather than a piece of treasure. Filling his jar, he hurried back to the palace and sprinkled the water, drop by drop, over the golden figure of his daughter. Slowly the color crept back into her cheeks, and she began to laugh, then to shiver, astonished to find her dress dripping wet. She remembered nothing of what had happened, and her father, perhaps wisely, never told her. Hand in hand, the two of them went out into the garden and sprinkled the river water over every rose bush, until the flowers were once again soft, red and fragrant.

Two reminders of the Golden Touch remained for the rest of the king's life. From that day on, the sand at the bottom of the Pactolus glittered like gold dust, and Marygold's brown curls kept a faint golden shine that they had never had before. Long afterwards, when Midas had grown old, he liked to take his grandchildren on his knees and tell them this story, running his fingers through their bright hair. "Ever since that morning," he would say, "I have disliked the color of gold, except when I see it in your curls."
