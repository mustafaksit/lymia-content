# Anne of Green Gables: Anne Arrives

Summary: A lonely, talkative orphan girl arrives at a quiet island farm where nobody expected her.

## Level A1

Matthew and Marilla lived at Green Gables. It was a farm in Avonlea. Matthew was old and very shy. "We need a boy," said Marilla. They asked for an orphan boy.

Matthew went to Bright River station. There was no boy there. A thin girl sat there alone. She had long red hair. "Are you Matthew Cuthbert?" asked the girl. "I am so happy to see you!"

Matthew could not leave her there. So the girl came home with him. On the road, she talked and talked. She loved the trees and the flowers. Matthew liked her very much.

"Where is the boy?" asked Marilla. "There was no boy," said Matthew. "You don't want me!" cried the girl. "I am not a boy!" "What is your name?" asked Marilla. "Anne Shirley," said the girl. "Please spell it with an E."

Marilla wanted to send Anne back. The next day, Anne told Marilla her story. Her mother and father died very young. Anne was only a baby then. Later she looked after other children. Then she went to an orphan home. Marilla felt very sorry for her. Matthew wanted Anne to stay. "You can stay with us," said Marilla. Anne cried, but now she cried with joy.

Two weeks later, Mrs. Rachel Lynde came. "She is so thin," said Mrs. Lynde. "And look at her red hair!" Anne got very angry. "I hate you!" she shouted.

"Say sorry to Mrs. Lynde," said Marilla. Then Matthew came up to her room. "Please say sorry, Anne," said Matthew softly. Anne loved Matthew, so she said yes. She went to Mrs. Lynde and said sorry. Mrs. Lynde smiled and forgave her.

Soon Anne met Diana Barry. Diana lived on the next farm. The two girls played in the garden. "Be my best friend, please," said Anne. "Yes!" said Diana with a laugh. They held hands and made a promise. Anne walked home with a happy heart. Green Gables was her home now.

One June day, Mrs. Rachel Lynde saw Matthew drive away. She hurried to Green Gables, and Marilla told her the news. "An orphan!" said Mrs. Lynde. "But you know nothing about him."

The next day, Marilla took Anne to Mrs. Spencer. Marilla wanted to send Anne back. On the way, Anne told her story. Her mother and father were teachers. They both got sick and died when Anne was a baby. Later she looked after other people's small children. Then she went to an orphan home. Marilla began to feel sorry for her.

At Mrs. Spencer's house, a hard, sharp woman wanted Anne for work. Anne looked at Marilla with frightened eyes. Marilla thought about Matthew and his kind face. So she took Anne back to Green Gables. "You can stay with us," she said the next day. Anne cried with joy.

Two weeks later, Mrs. Lynde came to see Anne. "She is very thin and plain," she said. "And her hair is as red as carrots!" Anne's face went red with anger. "I hate you!" she shouted. "You are a rude woman!" Then she ran upstairs to her room.

Marilla told Anne to say sorry, but Anne said no. The next evening, Matthew came quietly up to her room. "Please say sorry, Anne," he said softly. "It is so lonely downstairs without you." Anne loved Matthew, so she agreed. At Mrs. Lynde's house, she went down on her knees. "I am so sorry," she said. "Please forgive me." Mrs. Lynde forgave her at once.

Soon after, Anne met Diana Barry from the next farm. Diana had black hair and a happy smile. The two girls stood together in the garden. "Can you be my true friend, Diana?" asked Anne. "Yes, I can," said Diana with a laugh. They held hands and promised by the sun and moon.

Anne walked home with her heart full of joy. Anne was a part of Green Gables now. At last, the little orphan had a home.

## Level B1

Matthew and Marilla Cuthbert lived at Green Gables, an old farm near Avonlea. Matthew was sixty and very shy, and almost every woman frightened him. Marilla was tall and thin, with a strict face and a sensible mind. The farm work was getting too hard for Matthew, so they decided to adopt a boy.

One June afternoon, Mrs. Rachel Lynde saw Matthew driving away in his best suit. She could not rest until she knew why, so she walked up to Green Gables. "We're getting a little boy from an orphan home," Marilla told her calmly. Mrs. Lynde was shocked. "You don't know anything about this child," she warned.

At Bright River station, Matthew looked everywhere, but he could not find a boy. The only child there was a girl of about eleven, sitting on a pile of wood. Her thin face was covered with freckles, and her red hair hung in two braids. When she saw Matthew, she jumped up and held out her hand. "You must be Mr. Matthew Cuthbert of Green Gables," she said. "I was planning to climb that cherry tree and sleep there in the moonlight."

Matthew could not tell this hopeful child about the mistake. On the long drive home, she talked without stopping, and Matthew enjoyed every word. She loved the apple trees, which were white with flowers, and gave the road a name. "I never belonged to anybody before," she said happily.

Marilla came to the door as they drove up. "Matthew, who's that?" she asked. "Where is the boy?" "There wasn't any boy," said Matthew unhappily. The girl looked from one face to the other, and her eyes grew wide. "You don't want me because I'm not a boy!" she cried. Then she hid her face in her arms and cried and cried. "What's your name?" asked Marilla, a little more gently. "Anne Shirley," the girl answered. "But please spell it with an E."

The next afternoon, Marilla drove Anne to Mrs. Spencer's house to fix the mistake. On the way, Anne told her story. Her parents were teachers, and both died of a fever when she was a baby. After that, she lived with two poor families and looked after their small children. Finally, she went to an orphan home, which already had too many children. "Were those women good to you?" asked Marilla. "They wanted to be kind," Anne said quietly. Marilla said nothing, but she was thinking hard.

At Mrs. Spencer's house, a sharp-faced woman wanted a girl for her new baby. She looked Anne up and down, and Anne turned pale. Marilla thought of this hard woman, and then of Matthew's kind face. "I must talk to my brother first," she said, and she took Anne home again. The next day, Marilla told her that she could stay at Green Gables.

Two weeks later, Mrs. Lynde came to see the new girl for herself. "She's terribly thin and plain, Marilla," she said loudly. "And her hair is as red as carrots!" Anne's face went red, and she stamped her foot on the floor. "I hate you!" she shouted. "You are a rude, unkind woman!" Then she ran up the stairs and slammed her door.

Marilla told Anne that she must say sorry, but Anne refused. The next evening, Matthew crept up the stairs to her room. "Anne, please just say sorry," he whispered. "It's terribly lonely downstairs without you." Anne could not refuse Matthew. At Mrs. Lynde's house, she fell on her knees and made a long, dramatic apology. Mrs. Lynde was pleased, and she forgave Anne at once.

A few days later, Marilla took Anne to the farm next door. Mrs. Barry's daughter, Diana, was a pretty girl with black hair and a merry face. The two girls went out into the garden, among the tall old flowers. "Oh, Diana, can you be my best friend?" asked Anne shyly. Diana laughed. "Why, I think so," she said. They held hands and promised, by the sun and moon, to be true friends.

Anne ran home through the evening light, with her heart full of joy. That night, Marilla watched her at the supper table and thought about the last three weeks. She could no longer imagine Green Gables without Anne. The lonely orphan had a family at last.

## Level B2

Green Gables stood at the end of a long lane outside the village of Avonlea, on Prince Edward Island. Matthew and Marilla Cuthbert, a brother and sister who had never married, had lived there all their lives. Matthew was sixty, gentle and painfully shy, while Marilla was tall and strict, with a mouth that rarely smiled. As Matthew grew older, the farm work became too heavy for him, so they decided to adopt a boy who could help.

Mrs. Rachel Lynde, who lived at the bottom of the hill, prided herself on knowing everything that happened in Avonlea. One June afternoon she saw Matthew driving past in his best suit, and she hurried up to Green Gables. Marilla explained that Mrs. Spencer was bringing them a boy from an orphan home on the mainland. "You don't know a thing about this child," Mrs. Lynde declared. "Mark my words, nothing good will come of it."

Matthew reached Bright River station to find that the train had already gone. There was no boy on the platform, only a thin girl of about eleven sitting on a pile of shingles. She wore a tight, ugly dress, and two thick red braids hung down her back. Her face was small and covered with freckles, but her large gray-green eyes were full of life. "You must be Mr. Matthew Cuthbert of Green Gables," she said, holding out her hand. "If you hadn't come, I was going to spend the night up in that cherry tree, among the white flowers."

Matthew could not bring himself to disappoint her, so he decided that Marilla could explain. For the whole eight-mile drive the girl talked, and to his own great surprise, Matthew enjoyed every word. She told him that she loved imagining things, and that her red hair was the great sorrow of her life. Under a long arch of blossoming apple trees she fell silent with delight. Afterwards she gave the road a grand new name. "I'm so glad I'm going to belong to you," she said with a happy sigh. Matthew, who knew what was waiting at home, felt worse with every mile.

Marilla met them at the door, and her face changed when she saw the girl. "Matthew Cuthbert, who's that?" she demanded. "Where is the boy?" "There wasn't any boy," said Matthew helplessly. "There was only her." For a moment the child looked from one to the other, and the light slowly went out of her face. "You don't want me because I'm not a boy!" she cried. "Nobody has ever really wanted me." She sat down at the table, buried her face in her arms, and sobbed bitterly. "What's your name?" Marilla asked stiffly when the sobs had quieted a little. "Anne Shirley," said the girl. "But please spell it with an E. It looks so much more distinguished that way."

The next afternoon Marilla drove Anne to Mrs. Spencer's house to sort out the mistake. On the way, she asked Anne to tell her about herself. Anne's parents had both been teachers, and they had died of a fever when she was only three months old. After that, she was taken in by two poor families, and in both houses she spent her days caring for small children. At last, she was sent to the orphan home, where there were already too many children. "Were those women good to you?" Marilla asked. Anne's face went pink. "They meant to be kind, I'm sure," she said. "But they had so many troubles of their own." Marilla said nothing more, but pity was beginning to stir in her heart.

Mrs. Spencer was astonished to hear about the mistake, since the message she had received clearly asked for a girl. By chance, another neighbor was there, a sharp-eyed woman who was known for working her helpers far too hard. She needed a girl to look after her new baby, and she looked Anne over from head to foot. Anne turned white and looked at Marilla with silent, pleading eyes. Marilla thought of this hard woman, and then of Matthew's quiet face that morning. "I'll take her home and talk to my brother," she said firmly. The next evening, after listening to Matthew, she told Anne that she could stay at Green Gables.

Anne had been at Green Gables for two weeks when Mrs. Lynde came to inspect her. "She's terribly thin and plain, Marilla," said Mrs. Lynde. "Did anyone ever see such freckles, or hair as red as carrots?" In one jump Anne crossed the kitchen and stood in front of her, shaking with anger. "I hate you!" she cried, stamping her foot. "How would you like it if someone called you fat and dull? You are a rude, unkind woman, and I'll never forgive you!" Then she ran from the room, and the slam of her door echoed through the house.

Mrs. Lynde went home deeply offended, and Marilla told Anne that she must apologize. Anne refused, saying that she would rather stay in her room forever. The next evening, while Marilla was out in the yard, Matthew crept quietly up the stairs. "Anne, don't you think it might be best to just make peace?" he whispered. "It's dreadfully lonely downstairs without you." Because it was Matthew who asked, Anne agreed at once. At Mrs. Lynde's house she fell to her knees and poured out a long, dramatic apology. Marilla hardly knew whether to scold her or to laugh, because Anne seemed to enjoy every word of it. Mrs. Lynde, however, forgave her warmly and even said that red hair often darkened to a lovely auburn.

Not long afterward, Marilla took Anne to the Barry farm to borrow a skirt pattern. Diana Barry was a pretty girl of Anne's age, with black hair, rosy cheeks and a cheerful smile. While Marilla and Mrs. Barry talked indoors, the two girls stood shyly in the garden among the tall lilies. "Oh, Diana," said Anne at last, clasping her hands, "do you think you could like me enough to be my best friend?" Diana laughed. "I guess so," she said. "I'm awfully glad you've come to live at Green Gables." So they joined hands over the path and promised to be faithful friends for as long as the sun shone.

At supper Matthew gave Anne a small bag of chocolate sweets, and she asked whether she might give half to Diana. Marilla, watching her, realized with surprise that Anne had been with them only three weeks. Yet it already felt as though she had always lived there. The orphan from the station had found a home.

## Level C1

Anyone driving out of Avonlea, a small farming village on Prince Edward Island, might easily have missed the lane that wandered up to Green Gables, half hidden among its orchards. There Matthew Cuthbert and his sister Marilla, neither of whom had ever married, had spent most of their lives in a quiet and careful routine. Matthew, at sixty, was the shyest man in the district, a gentle soul who would take the long way round rather than speak to a stranger. Marilla was tall and angular, her gray hair screwed into a tight knot, and only a faint hint of humor about the mouth suggested that she was not made entirely of rules. With Matthew's heart growing weaker and the farm work heavier, the pair had at last agreed to adopt an orphan boy who could help in the fields.

Mrs. Rachel Lynde, whose house stood where the lane met the brook, was one of those capable women who manage their own affairs perfectly and still find time for everyone else's. So when, one bright June afternoon, she saw Matthew drive calmly over the hill in his best suit, she knew that something extraordinary must be happening. By tea time she was sitting in the Green Gables kitchen, where Marilla informed her, without the slightest drama, that Mrs. Spencer was bringing them a boy from the mainland. Mrs. Lynde delivered her verdict at once: taking in a stranger's child was a risky business, and her old friends, she warned, would live to regret it.

Matthew, meanwhile, arrived at Bright River to discover that the train had come and gone. The only passenger left behind was a girl of about eleven, perched on a pile of shingles at the far end of the platform. She wore a tight, ugly dress of yellowish gray, and beneath her faded hat hung two braids of unmistakably red hair. Her face was small, thin and freckled, and her eyes, which shifted from gray to green with every change of light, were bright with eager hope. "You must be Mr. Matthew Cuthbert of Green Gables," she said, with a gravity far beyond her years. "I had quite made up my mind that, if nobody came, I would climb that wild cherry tree and sleep among its blossoms under the moon."

Faced with this small, trusting stranger, Matthew found that he could no more tell her of the mistake than he could have flown. Throughout the eight-mile drive the child talked without pause, and Matthew, who dreaded almost every female creature, discovered to his astonishment that he was enjoying himself. She confided that she had never belonged to anybody, and that her red hair was the one sorrow she could not imagine away. Under a long arch of apple trees in full blossom she fell silent for the first time. Afterwards she insisted that so lovely a road deserved a far grander name than the plain one the neighbors gave it. Matthew, who knew what was waiting for them, felt more miserable with every turn of the wheels.

It was Marilla who opened the door, and her eyes went from the stiff little figure in the ugly dress to her brother's guilty face. "Matthew Cuthbert, who's that?" she exclaimed. "Where is the boy?" "There wasn't any boy," said Matthew miserably. "There was only her." Little by little, as the two of them argued, the child understood, and the radiance drained from her face as completely as light from a winter sky. "You don't want me because I'm not a boy!" she cried. "Nothing lovely ever lasts for me, and I was foolish to think this might." Then she flung herself into a chair, hid her face in her arms, and wept with a stormy abandon that left Marilla, who had no talent for comforting, entirely at a loss.

"Well, well, there's no need to cry like that," said Marilla at last, with more gentleness than she knew she possessed. "What's your name?" The girl admitted, after some hesitation, that she would much prefer a more romantic name, though her real one was plain Anne Shirley. "But please spell it with an E," she pleaded. "Without one, it looks so dreadfully ordinary on paper."

Next afternoon Marilla set off with Anne for Mrs. Spencer's house, fully intending to set the mistake right and come home alone. To stop the child from chattering about the sea and the sky, she asked for her history instead, and Anne, with unusual reluctance, gave it. Her parents had both been schoolteachers, poor as church mice, and both had died of a fever when their daughter was barely three months old. After that she lived with two poor families in turn, minding their small children, until she was sent to an orphan home that was already overcrowded. "Were those women good to you?" Marilla asked abruptly. A painful flush crept over Anne's face. "They wanted to be, I'm sure of it," she said slowly. "Only it's hard to be kind when you're always tired and worried about money."

Mrs. Spencer greeted them with cheerful astonishment; the message she had received, she insisted, had asked quite plainly for a girl. As chance would have it, another neighbor had called that afternoon, a sharp-eyed woman known all over the district for working her hired girls to exhaustion. She had a new baby that cried day and night, and on hearing about Anne she looked the child over like a farmer pricing a calf. Marilla glanced at Anne, sitting rigid and white on the edge of her chair, and something in that silent, hopeless face moved her more than all the tears of the night before. "I think I'd better take her home again and talk it over with my brother," she said with decision. The next evening, having heard Matthew's shy but stubborn opinion, Marilla told Anne that she might stay at Green Gables, provided she did her best to be good.

Mrs. Lynde, kept at home by a heavy cold, did not arrive to inspect the newcomer until Anne had been at Green Gables for two weeks. She took one long look at the child and turned to Marilla with the frankness on which she prided herself. "She's terribly thin and plain, Marilla," she announced. "Did anyone ever see such freckles? And hair as red as carrots!" In a single bound Anne crossed the kitchen and stood before her, scarlet with fury, her lips trembling and her small frame shaking from head to foot. "I hate you!" she cried, stamping her foot on the floor. "How would you like it if someone said you were fat and dull and hadn't an ounce of imagination? You are a rude, heartless woman, and I'll never forgive you, never!" Then she was gone, and the door of her room slammed overhead like a clap of thunder.

Mrs. Lynde departed in deep offense, and Marilla, though privately aware that Rachel had been unkind, sent Anne to her room until she was ready to apologize. Anne, with the stubbornness of the truly wronged, replied that she would sooner stay there for the rest of her life. The next evening, while his sister was out in the pasture, he crept up the back stairs, as nervous as a stranger in his own house, and tapped at the door. "Anne," he whispered, "couldn't you just say you're sorry and be done with it? The house is awful quiet without you downstairs." For Matthew, Anne would have done almost anything, and she agreed. On her knees on Mrs. Lynde's kitchen floor, she delivered an apology so lavish in tears and self-reproach that Marilla hardly knew whether to be satisfied or alarmed. The child, it was perfectly clear, was enjoying every moment of it. Mrs. Lynde, who had no ear for irony, was entirely won over, and she even offered the comforting opinion that red hair very often darkened into a handsome auburn with the years.

Some days later Marilla went over to the Barry farm to borrow a skirt pattern, and Anne went with her in a fever of hope and dread. Diana Barry turned out to be a pretty, black-haired girl of Anne's own age, with the merry expression of someone who had never in her life been unwanted. "Oh, Diana," Anne burst out at last, clasping her hands, "do you think you could like me a little, enough to be my best friend for always?" Diana laughed, as she generally did before saying anything. "Why, I guess so," she said frankly. "There's nobody near my age to play with, you know." So, standing in the Barry garden with their hands clasped across the path, Anne solemnly made them both vow to be faithful friends for as long as the sun and moon endured.

That evening at supper Matthew produced a small bag of chocolate sweets, half of which, Anne instantly decided, must go to Diana. Watching her across the table, Marilla was startled to realize that the child had been with them for only three weeks. It seemed, somehow, far longer, and Marilla could no longer quite picture the house as it had been before, so orderly and so silent. Without anyone quite deciding it, Green Gables had become Anne's home.
