# The Purloined Letter

Summary: A clever detective in Paris hunts for a stolen royal letter that the police cannot find.

## Level A1

Dupin lived in Paris. He was a very clever man. I was his friend. One evening, we sat in his small library. Then the Prefect came in. He was the head of the city police.

"I need help," said the Prefect. "The queen had a secret letter. She read it alone in her room. Then the king came in. She put the letter on the table. Then the Minister came in too. He put his own letter next to it. He took her letter with him. The queen saw it, but she said nothing. Now the Minister has the letter. He uses it against the queen. She wants it back."

The Prefect and his men looked everywhere. They went to the Minister's house every night. They looked in every chair and every book. They even looked in the Minister's pockets. But they found nothing.

"Look again," said Dupin. A month later, the Prefect came back. "The letter is not there," said the Prefect. "I can give fifty thousand francs for it." "Then pay me now," said Dupin. The Prefect wrote a check for him. Dupin took a letter from a drawer. It was the queen's letter! The Prefect took it and ran out.

"How did you find it?" I asked. "The police look in secret places," said Dupin. "But the Minister is very clever. He did not hide the letter. He left it out in the open."

"I went to his house," said Dupin. "I wore green glasses. A dirty, torn letter sat near the fire. I knew it at once. I left my gold box there on purpose. The next day, I went back for it. Suddenly a loud noise came from the street. I paid a man for that noise. The Minister ran to the window. Quickly, I took the real letter. I put a fake letter in its place."

Dupin smiled. "I wrote some words inside it. The Minister knows my writing very well."

## Level A2

Many years ago, I lived in Paris with my friend Dupin. Dupin was a quiet man with a sharp mind. One autumn evening, we sat together in his small library. Suddenly the door opened, and the Prefect walked in. He was the head of the Paris police.

"I need your help," said the Prefect. "A few months ago, the queen got a secret letter. She read it alone in her room. Then the king came in. She did not want him to see it. So she left it on the table. Then the Minister came in too. He saw the letter and understood everything at once. He put a letter of his own next to hers. When he left, he took her letter and left his own. The queen saw him, but she could say nothing. Now the Minister keeps the letter and uses it against her. She asked me for help. For three months, my men searched his house every night."

They opened every drawer and looked inside every chair. They pushed long needles into the cushions. They looked at every book, page by page. Twice, they stopped the Minister in the street and searched his pockets. But they did not find the letter.

"Search the house again," said Dupin. A month later, the Prefect came back. "The letter is still not there," said the Prefect. "I can pay fifty thousand francs of my own money for help."

"Then write me a check for that money," said Dupin. "Then you can have the letter." The Prefect wrote the check with shaking hands. Dupin took a letter from his desk and gave it to him. The Prefect looked at it and ran out without a word.

"How did you find it?" I asked. "The police search in only one way," said Dupin. "They look for secret places, like holes in chairs and floors. So the Minister did not hide the letter at all. He put it where everyone could see it."

"I went to visit the Minister at his house," said Dupin. "I wore green glasses and complained about my weak eyes. A cheap holder for cards hung near the fireplace. Inside it, I saw a dirty, torn letter. It had a big black seal with the Minister's own mark. It looked different from the queen's letter. But the size was right, and I knew the truth at once."

"I left my gold box there on purpose," said Dupin. "At home, I made a copy of the letter that night. The next morning, I went back for my box. Suddenly there was a loud noise in the street. I paid a man for that noise. The Minister ran to the window to look. Quickly, I took the real letter. I put my copy in its place."

"Why did you leave a copy?" I asked. "Because the Minister must not notice the change," said Dupin. "In his mind, he still has power over the queen. Inside the copy, I wrote a few lines from an old play. The Minister once did me a bad turn. He knows my handwriting very well."

## Level B1

Many years ago in Paris, I spent an autumn evening with my friend Auguste Dupin. We sat in his small library without saying a word. Then the door opened, and in came the Prefect of the Paris police. He was an amusing man, but not a very clever one.

"I am here about a very simple matter," said the Prefect. "It is so simple, in fact, that it confuses us all." "Perhaps the problem is that it is too simple," said Dupin. The Prefect laughed loudly at this idea.

Then he told us the story, but only after we promised to keep it secret. A few months earlier, the queen was reading a private letter in her rooms. Suddenly the king came in, and she did not want him to see it. She had no time to hide it, so she left it on the table. At that moment the Minister came in, and his sharp eyes saw the letter. He recognized the handwriting and understood her secret immediately. After some talk about business, he put a letter of his own next to hers. When he left, he took her letter and left his own behind. The queen saw everything, but she could not speak in front of the king.

"Now the Minister keeps the letter and uses it to control her," said the Prefect. "While he has it, he has great power over her. She asked me to find it, quietly."

"For three months, my men searched his house every night," the Prefect went on. "We looked into every drawer and every cupboard. We pushed long needles into the cushions and took the tops off the tables. We used a strong glass to examine every chair and every space between the bricks. We opened every book and turned every page. Twice, my men stopped him in the street like thieves and searched his pockets. Still, we found nothing."

"Then search the house again, very carefully," said Dupin.

A month later, the Prefect visited us again, looking more tired than ever. "I did everything again, and the letter is still not there," he said. "The reward is now twice as large. I will give fifty thousand francs of my own money to anyone who helps me." "Then write me a check for that amount," said Dupin calmly. "When you sign it, I will give you the letter." The Prefect stared at him, unable to speak. Then he wrote the check and signed it. Dupin opened a drawer, took out a letter and handed it to him. With shaking hands, the Prefect ran out of the house without a word.

When he left, Dupin explained everything to me. "The police work hard, but they think in only one way," he began. "They search for the letter in the places where they themselves hide things. They expect secret holes and hidden corners. The Minister is a poet and a mathematician, and he knows their methods well. So he did not hide the letter at all. He left it in plain view, where nobody looks."

"There is a game with a map," Dupin went on. "One player chooses a name, and the other must find it on the map. Beginners choose tiny names in small print. Good players choose big names, which stretch across the whole map. These names are so obvious that nobody sees them."

"So I visited the Minister one morning, wearing green glasses," said Dupin. "I complained about my weak eyes, but behind the glasses I looked everywhere. A cheap card rack hung from a little brass knob near the fireplace. In it, there was a dirty, torn letter with a large black seal. The address on it was the Minister's own, in small writing like a woman's. It looked careless and unimportant, but it was too careless for such a tidy man. Its edges looked rough and old, and suddenly I understood. He turned the letter inside out and gave it a new address and seal."

"I left my gold box on his table and went home," Dupin continued. "That night, I made a copy of the outside of the letter. The next morning, I went back for my box. While we were talking, there was a loud bang and shouting in the street below. I paid a man to make that noise. The Minister hurried to the window to look, and I quickly changed the letters. Now he has my copy, and he does not know it."

"Why leave a copy and not just take the letter?" I asked. "Because I wanted to leave his house safely," said Dupin with a smile. "Also, the Minister once did me a bad turn, and I promised to remember it. So inside the copy I wrote a few lines from an old French play. He knows my handwriting well."

## Level B2

In Paris, one windy autumn evening, I was sitting with my friend Auguste Dupin in his small library. We had been silent for an hour, each lost in his own thoughts, when the door opened. Our visitor was the Prefect of the Paris police, a man we found both amusing and a little tiresome. Dupin stood up to light a lamp, but sat down again when he heard that our guest wanted advice. "If it is a matter for thinking," he said, "we will examine it better in the dark."

"It is a very simple matter," said the Prefect, "and yet it puzzles us completely." "Perhaps it is the simplicity itself which misleads you," said Dupin. The Prefect laughed so hard at this idea that he nearly fell out of his chair.

After we had promised to keep the secret, he told us the story. A letter of great importance had been taken from the royal rooms. The thief was known, because he had been seen taking it. The queen had been reading the letter alone when the king walked in unexpectedly. Unable to hide it in a drawer, she left it on the table with only the address showing. Then the Minister came in, saw the letter, recognized the handwriting and guessed her secret. After talking about business for a quarter of an hour, he placed a letter of his own beside hers. When he left, he took hers and left his own on the table. The queen saw it all, but she could not say a word with the king beside her.

"The Minister now uses the letter to control her," said the Prefect. "As long as he keeps it, he holds great power over her. She has asked me to get it back quietly, without any public noise."

"Are you certain it is still in his house?" asked Dupin. "Quite certain, because he needs it ready to use at any moment," replied the Prefect. "For three months, while the Minister was away at night, my men searched every room of his house. We took the tops off the tables to look for hollow legs. We pushed long, fine needles into every cushion and examined every joint of every chair with a powerful glass. We measured each book, opened it, and turned every page. We lifted the carpets, studied the floorboards, and checked the moss between the bricks in the garden. Twice my men stopped him in the street, pretending to be robbers, and searched him from head to foot. We found nothing at all."

"Then I have only one piece of advice," said Dupin. "Search the house again, thoroughly."

About a month later, the Prefect returned, looking more troubled than ever. "I followed your advice, and the letter is still not there," he admitted. "The reward has now been doubled. I would gladly give fifty thousand francs of my own money to anyone who could help me." "In that case," said Dupin calmly, "you may write me a check for that amount. When you have signed it, I will hand you the letter." The Prefect stared at him in silence, as if he could not believe his ears. Then, with trembling fingers, he wrote out the check and signed it. Dupin unlocked a drawer, took out a letter and gave it to him. The Prefect read it with joy and rushed out of the house without a single word.

"The Paris police are skillful in their own way," Dupin explained when we were alone. "But they search only in the places where they themselves would hide something. They imagine secret holes in chair legs and hollow spaces in the walls. I once knew a schoolboy of eight who always won at a guessing game of even and odd. He studied the face of the other player and made his own face look the same. Then he waited to see what thoughts came into his mind. In this way, he could think exactly as his opponent thought. The police never try to think like the man they are hunting."

"The Minister is both a poet and a mathematician," Dupin continued, "and he knew they would search his house. He understood that the cleverest hiding place was no hiding place at all. Have you ever played the map game? One player names a word on a map, and the other must find it. A beginner chooses the smallest name he can find. An expert chooses a word in large letters which stretches across the whole map. Such words are so obvious that the eye passes over them, like the big signs above shops."

"So I called on the Minister one morning, wearing a pair of green glasses," said Dupin. "I complained of my weak eyes, and while we talked, I searched the room behind them. Near the fireplace, a cheap card rack hung from a little brass knob. In it lay a few visiting cards and a single letter, dirty, crumpled and almost torn in two. It had a large black seal with the Minister's own mark, and it was addressed to him in a small, feminine hand. It looked so worthless that no visitor would give it a second glance. Yet it was far too careless for a man with such tidy habits. Its edges were rubbed, and I knew at once that it had been turned inside out and sealed again."

"When I left, I deliberately forgot my gold box on his table," Dupin went on. "At home that evening, I carefully prepared a copy of the outside of the letter. The next morning I returned for my box, and we began talking again. Suddenly a loud bang came from the street below, followed by frightened shouting. The Minister rushed to the window to see what was happening. In that moment I took the letter from the rack and put my copy in its place. The noise had been made by a man I had paid, and he soon walked away quite calmly."

"But why did you leave a copy?" I asked. "If I had taken it openly, I might never have left his house," replied Dupin. "Besides, the Minister still believes he holds the queen in his power. He will go on acting as if he does, and so he will bring about his own fall. He once did me a bad turn, and I told him, quite cheerfully, that I would remember it. So inside the copy I wrote a few lines from an old French play about one dark plan answered by another. He knows my handwriting very well."

## Level C1

On a windy autumn evening in Paris, I sat with my friend Auguste Dupin in his small library at the back of the house, neither of us inclined to speak. The silence ended when the door swung open and admitted the Prefect of the Paris police, an old acquaintance whom we welcomed with as much amusement as respect. Dupin, who had risen to light the lamp, sat down again without doing so once he learned that our guest had come to consult him. "If the matter requires reflection," he remarked, "we shall examine it to better purpose in the dark."

"The business is extremely simple," the Prefect began, "and yet I thought Dupin might enjoy the details, because they are so very odd." "Perhaps it is precisely the simplicity of the thing that leads you astray," said Dupin. The Prefect laughed so heartily at this suggestion that I feared for the chair beneath him.

Having made us promise absolute secrecy, he explained that a document of the highest importance had been taken from the royal apartments. The queen, it seemed, had been reading the letter in private when the king entered without warning. Unable to slip it into a drawer, she had laid it on the table with only the address showing, trusting that it would escape notice. At that moment the Minister came in, and his quick eyes went straight to the paper, recognized the handwriting, and read the confusion in her face. For a quarter of an hour he talked about public business; then he drew a letter of his own from his pocket, pretended to read it, and set it down beside hers. When at last he took his leave, it was her letter that he carried away, while his own, of no value whatever, remained on the table. The queen watched the exchange with her own eyes, yet with the king standing at her elbow she dared not say a word.

"The Minister now holds a power over her that he uses freely for his own ends," the Prefect went on. "At last, in despair, she has placed the whole affair in my hands, and the letter must be recovered without a whisper of scandal."

"You are certain it is still in his house?" asked Dupin. "Absolutely, for its whole value lies in his being able to produce it at a moment's notice," said the Prefect. "Fortunately the Minister is often away at night, so for three months my men and I have searched his house from top to bottom. We removed the tops of the tables, in case a leg had been hollowed out to hold a rolled paper. We probed every cushion with long, fine needles, and examined every joint of every chair under a powerful microscope for the faintest trace of fresh glue. We opened every book, turned every page, and measured every cover to the smallest fraction of an inch. We lifted the carpets, studied the floorboards, and even inspected the moss between the bricks in the garden. Twice my agents stopped the Minister in the street, disguised as common robbers, and searched him to the last thread. And still we found nothing."

"What would you advise me to do, Dupin?" the Prefect sighed. "Search the house once more, as thoroughly as before," said Dupin. Before leaving, the Prefect read aloud from his notebook a careful description of the stolen letter, its outer appearance above all, and then went away gloomier than I had ever seen him.

About a month afterwards he called on us again, and after some ordinary conversation he confessed that the second search had been as fruitless as the first. "How large was the reward, did you say?" asked Dupin. "Very large indeed, and it has lately been doubled," said the Prefect. "I would gladly give fifty thousand francs of my own to anyone who could help me." "In that case," said Dupin, opening a drawer, "you may as well fill in a check for that sum. When you have signed it, I shall hand you the letter." The Prefect looked as though a thunderbolt had fallen at his feet. For some moments he sat speechless, staring at my friend; then, recovering a little, he seized a pen and, after several pauses, wrote and signed the check. Dupin examined it, unlocked his desk and handed the Prefect a letter. Our visitor clutched it with trembling hands, glanced at its contents, and hurried out of the house without a single word of thanks or farewell.

When he had gone, my friend agreed to explain. "The Paris police are able enough in their way," he said, "patient, ingenious and thorough in everything their work demands. Their failure lies not in the search but in the mind behind it, since they look only for the hiding places they themselves would have chosen. I once knew a schoolboy of eight who won every marble in his school at a simple guessing game of even and odd. His secret was to arrange his own face to match his opponent's expression, and then wait to see what thoughts arose in him to match it. The Prefect never troubles to measure the mind of the man he is pursuing; he assumes that every thief hides things as a policeman would."

"He considers the Minister a fool because the man has written poetry," Dupin went on. "But the Minister is a mathematician as well as a poet, and a bold courtier besides, and such a man would certainly foresee the searches of the police. His frequent nights away from home were no accident; they were invitations, offered so that the police might search freely and persuade themselves the letter was not there. Do you remember the game played with a map, in which one player names a word and the other must find it? The beginner chooses some tiny name in the finest print, but the expert prefers a word in great letters that stretches from one side of the map to the other. Such words, like the huge signs above shop doors, are so obvious that the eye slides past them without noticing."

"So I put on a pair of green glasses and called one fine morning at the Minister's house," Dupin continued. "I complained of my weak eyes, and behind the glasses I studied every corner of the room. At last my eyes came to rest on a cheap little holder for visiting cards, made of decorated cardboard, which dangled from a brass knob beneath the mantelpiece on a grubby ribbon."

"In it were a few visiting cards and a single letter," said Dupin, "soiled, crumpled, and torn almost in half. It bore a large black seal with the Minister's own initial, and it was addressed to the Minister himself in a small, feminine hand. This was utterly unlike the Prefect's description, which spoke of a small red seal and a bold, firm address, yet the size was exactly the same. That very difference, together with the dirt and the careless way the letter lay among the cards, so unlike the Minister's tidy habits, told me almost everything. Its rubbed edges showed that it had been turned inside out, like a glove, given a new address and sealed again."

"I took my leave, deliberately forgetting a gold box on the table," Dupin went on. "That evening I prepared a careful copy of the outside of the letter, imitating the seal with a piece of bread. The next morning I called to fetch my box. While we were talking, a loud bang sounded beneath the windows, followed by cries of alarm and the noise of a gathering crowd. The Minister hurried to the window and looked down into the street, and at that instant I took the letter and put my copy in its place. The disturbance had been arranged by a man in my pay, and when it proved to be nothing more than a harmless noise, the crowd let him go his way."

"But why replace it with a copy?" I asked. "Would it not have been simpler to seize it openly and go?" "The Minister is a determined man, and his house is full of servants loyal to him," replied Dupin. "Had I taken it openly, I might never have left that house at all. Besides, for eighteen months he has held the queen in his power, and now she holds him in hers. He does not know the letter has gone, and he will go on making demands as though he still had it, until he brings about his own political ruin."

"Did you leave the inside of the copy blank?" I asked. "That would have seemed an insult," said Dupin. "Once, years ago, the Minister did me an ill turn, and I told him, quite cheerfully, that I would remember it. Since he will surely wish to know who outwitted him, it seemed a pity to leave him without a clue. So in the middle of the blank sheet I copied two lines from an old French tragedy, about one dark scheme repaid by another, in a hand he knows very well."
