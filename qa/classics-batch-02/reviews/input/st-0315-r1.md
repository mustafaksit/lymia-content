# Theseus and the Minotaur

Summary: A young man finds his father's sword and sails to Crete to face a monster in a maze.

## Level A1

Theseus lived with his mother, Aethra, in Troezen. Theseus grew tall and very strong. "Where is my father?" he asked one day. Aethra took him to a big stone. "Lift this stone," said Aethra. Theseus tried, but the stone did not move.

A year later, Theseus came back. Now he lifted the stone. Under it lay a sword and gold shoes. "These are your father's things," said Aethra. "Your father is Aegeus, the king of Athens. Go to him."

Theseus walked to Athens. Bad men waited on the long road. Theseus beat them all. In Athens, he showed the king the sword. King Aegeus knew the sword at once. "My son!" cried Aegeus, and he hugged Theseus.

But Athens had a sad secret. Every year, a ship went to Crete. Seven boys and seven girls went on it. King Minos of Crete took them. They went into the Labyrinth. They never came back. A monster lived there, the Minotaur. It had the head of a bull.

"I can stop the Minotaur," said Theseus. The ship had black sails. The old king gave him white sails. "Put these up on the way home. White sails mean good news," said Aegeus.

In Crete, King Minos had a daughter, Ariadne. She liked the brave young man. She gave him a ball of thread. "Tie it to the door," said Ariadne. "Then you can find the way out."

Theseus went into the dark Labyrinth. The thread ran out behind him. He found the Minotaur and fought it. Theseus won the fight. Then he followed the thread back out. The young people from Athens were free.

The happy ship sailed home. But Theseus forgot about the white sails. The black sails stayed up. Old Aegeus waited on a high rock. He saw the black sails and lost hope. The old king died of sadness. Theseus became the new king of Athens. The sea got the old king's name. People call it the Aegean Sea.

## Level A2

Long ago, Theseus lived in Troezen with his mother, Aethra. He never saw his father, and he often asked about him. Aethra always answered, "Wait until you are strong." Theseus grew into a tall, strong young man.

When he was sixteen, Aethra took him up a hill. There was a huge stone at the top. "Lift this stone," she said. Theseus pushed and pulled, but the stone did not move. "Try again next year," said his mother. So he worked hard all year and grew even stronger.

The next year, he lifted the great stone with both hands. Under it lay an old bronze sword and gold sandals. "Your father left these here," said Aethra. "He is Aegeus, the king of Athens. Take them and go to him."

The road to Athens was long and dangerous. Wicked robbers lived along the way and stopped everyone. Theseus fought them one by one, and he won every fight. Soon people everywhere talked about the brave young man.

In Athens, Theseus went to the palace. He put the old sword in front of the king. Aegeus looked at it, and his eyes filled with tears. "This is my sword, and you are my son!" he cried.

But the people of Athens were not happy. Every year, a black ship sailed from Athens to Crete. On it were seven young men and seven young women. Minos, the strong king of Crete, wanted them. He put them in the Labyrinth, a building full of dark paths. In the middle lived the Minotaur. This monster had the head of a bull. Nobody ever came out of the Labyrinth again.

"I want to go with them," said Theseus. "I can fight the Minotaur." His father begged him to stay, but Theseus did not listen. At last the old king gave him a set of white sails. "Put these up on your way home," said Aegeus. "White sails can tell me the good news from far away."

In Crete, King Minos had a daughter, Ariadne. She saw Theseus and loved him at once. At night, she came to him in secret. She gave him a sword and a big ball of thread. "Tie the thread to the door and hold it," said Ariadne. "It can show you the way back."

The next day, Theseus walked into the Labyrinth. The thread rolled out behind him through the dark paths. At last, he found the Minotaur in the middle. They fought for a long time, and Theseus beat the monster. Then he followed the thread back to the light.

Theseus and the young people ran to the ship and sailed away. They were so happy, and they forgot about the sails. The black sails stayed up. Every day, old Aegeus stood on a high cliff by the sea. When he saw the black sails, his heart broke. The old king died of sadness that same day. Theseus came home to a quiet city, and he became king. From that day, people called the sea the Aegean Sea, after Aegeus.

## Level B1

Theseus grew up in Troezen, a small town by the sea, with his mother, Aethra. He never knew his father, and whenever he asked about him, his mother only smiled. "You will learn his name when you are strong enough," she told him. So Theseus ran, wrestled and climbed until he was the strongest young man in Troezen.

When he was sixteen, Aethra led him up a hill to a great flat stone. "Lift this stone, and you will find out who your father is," she said. Theseus pushed until his face turned red, but the stone stayed where it was. Aethra told him to wait another year and grow stronger. A year later, he lifted the stone and turned it over. Underneath lay a bronze sword and a pair of golden sandals.

"Your father hid these things here long ago," said Aethra. "He is Aegeus, the king of Athens. Put on the sandals, take the sword, and go to him." Aethra wanted him to go by sea, because the road was full of robbers. But Theseus chose the road, because he wanted to do brave deeds.

On the way to Athens, he met one wicked robber after another. Each of them liked to trouble strangers on the lonely mountain paths. Theseus fought them all and won every time, and the roads became safe again. By the time he reached Athens, people were already singing about him.

Old King Aegeus did not know this young stranger. At a feast in the palace, Theseus took out the bronze sword to cut his meat. The king saw the sword and jumped up from his seat. "Where did you get that?" he cried. When Theseus told him, Aegeus held him tight and wept for joy. At last, he had a son, and Athens had a prince.

Yet Athens hid a terrible sorrow. Long ago, King Minos of Crete beat Athens in a war. Since then, every year, Athens sent him seven young men and seven young women. Minos put them into the Labyrinth, a dark maze that Daedalus made for him. At its center lived the Minotaur, a monster with a man's body and a bull's head. None of the young people ever came out again.

When the black ship was ready to leave, Theseus stood up in front of the crowd. "I will go as one of the fourteen, and I will fight the Minotaur," he said. Aegeus begged him to stay, but Theseus did not change his mind. At last the old king gave his son a set of white sails. "Put these up when you come home," Aegeus told him. "Then I will know from far away that you are alive."

In Crete, the young people from Athens stood before King Minos. His daughter Ariadne was standing beside him, and she saw Theseus. She admired his courage, and soon she loved him. That night she came to him in secret with a sword and a ball of thread. "Tie the thread to the door, and hold the ball as you walk," she whispered. "The thread will lead you back to me."

In the morning, Theseus walked into the Labyrinth. He tied the thread to the door and let the ball unroll as he went. The paths turned left and right, up and down, until he could not see any light. At last, he heard a deep roar and found the Minotaur. They fought for a long time in the dark, and Theseus defeated the monster. Then he followed the thread, step by step, back to the door.

Before morning, Theseus, Ariadne and the young people ran to the ship and sailed away. On the way they stopped at the island of Naxos, and there Ariadne stayed behind. The others sailed on toward Athens, singing and laughing all the way. In all their joy, they forgot about the white sails.

Every day, old Aegeus stood on a high cliff and looked out to sea. At last, he saw the ship, and its sails were still black. He believed that his son was lost, and he died of sorrow there by the water. Theseus came home to a city in tears, and he became king of Athens. From that day on, people called the sea the Aegean, after his father.

## Level B2

In the little town of Troezen, beside the sea, a boy named Theseus grew up with his mother, Aethra. He had never seen his father. Whenever he asked about him, Aethra would only look away and say nothing. "When you are strong enough, you will know," she told him. So Theseus spent his days running, wrestling and hunting in the hills, until no young man in Troezen could match him.

When he was sixteen, his mother led him up the hillside to a great flat stone covered in moss. "If you can lift this stone, you will find out who your father is," she said. Theseus bent down and heaved with all his might, but the stone did not move at all. "Wait another year," said Aethra gently, and he went home ashamed. All that year he trained harder than ever. When he returned, he raised the stone and rolled it aside. Beneath it lay a bronze sword with a golden handle and a pair of golden sandals, both as bright as new.

"Your father, Aegeus, king of Athens, hid these here long ago," Aethra told him. "He asked me to send you to him once you were strong enough to lift the stone." Then she begged him to travel by ship, because the land road to Athens was full of robbers and wild beasts. Theseus refused, for he wanted to win fame along the way, as the great heroes had done before him.

The journey proved every bit as dangerous as his mother had feared. Cruel robbers waited at the narrow places of the road, and each of them had his own wicked trick for catching strangers. Theseus beat every one of them, and by the time he came down into Athens, the roads behind him were safe. His name had already reached the city before him.

King Aegeus, now old and anxious, did not recognize the tall young man who came to his palace. He invited the stranger to a feast, and there Theseus drew the bronze sword to cut his meat. The moment the king saw the golden handle, he jumped to his feet. "Where did you find that sword?" he cried. Theseus told him about the stone in Troezen. At once Aegeus threw his arms around him, weeping with joy. Before the whole court, the old king named him his son and the future ruler of Athens.

Yet Theseus soon discovered that the city carried a heavy grief. Years before, the son of King Minos of Crete had died while visiting Athens. In his anger, Minos had made war on the city. To keep the peace, Athens had promised to send him seven young men and seven young women every year. They were carried across the sea in a ship with black sails, and they were shut inside the Labyrinth. This maze had been built by the clever craftsman Daedalus, and nobody could find a way through it. In its heart lived the Minotaur, a monster with the body of a man and the head of a bull. No one who entered the Labyrinth ever came out again.

When the day came to choose the fourteen, the mothers of Athens wept in the streets. Theseus stepped forward and offered to go as one of them. He promised to put an end to this terrible custom forever. Aegeus pleaded with him, but he could not change his son's mind. At last the old king handed his son a set of white sails. "If you come home safely, raise these instead of the black ones," he said. "I will watch for them every day from the cliffs."

In Crete, the young prisoners stood before King Minos, and his daughter Ariadne watched them from beside the throne. She could not stop looking at Theseus, who stood straight and calm while the others trembled. Late that night, she slipped out of the palace and came to him in secret. She was carrying a sword and a ball of thread. "Tie the end of this thread to the door as you go in, and let it run out behind you," she whispered. "When your work is done, follow it back, and it will bring you out."

Theseus entered the Labyrinth with the thread unwinding in his hand. The passages twisted and turned and doubled back on themselves, and soon there was no light at all. He kept one hand on the cold wall and the other on the thread. He walked on through the darkness until he heard a roar and found the Minotaur in the very center of the maze. The struggle was long and hard, but in the end Theseus defeated the beast. Then, winding up the thread as he went, he found his way back to the door and out into the starlight.

Before dawn, Theseus led the young people of Athens down to the harbor. Ariadne fled with them, because she was afraid of her father's anger. They stopped on the island of Naxos on the way home, and there Ariadne stayed behind when the ship sailed on. The rest of the voyage was full of singing and celebration. In their happiness, everyone forgot the promise about the sails.

Day after day, old Aegeus had climbed the cliffs above the sea to watch for the ship. When at last it appeared on the horizon, its sails were still as black as on the day it had left. Believing that his son was dead, the old king died of grief on the cliff top. Theseus came home to a city in mourning and became king of Athens in his father's place. Ever since, the sea where Aegeus watched for his son has been called the Aegean.

## Level C1

Theseus spent his boyhood in Troezen, a quiet town on the shore across the water from Athens, raised by his mother, Aethra, and by her old father, who ruled the place. Of his own father he knew nothing at all, for whenever he asked, Aethra would turn her eyes toward the sea and say only that he would learn the truth in time. So the boy grew up restless and eager, racing the wind, wrestling with the shepherds, and listening by the fire to stories of heroes who had cleared the world of monsters.

When he was sixteen, his mother led him up through the woods to a great flat stone, half buried in moss and fallen leaves. "Lift this," she said, "and you will know whose son you are." He set his shoulder against it and strained until his arms shook, but the stone, which seemed rooted in the hill itself, did not stir. Aethra only smiled, a little sadly, and told him to come back in a year. He spent that year making himself stronger than any youth in Troezen, and when he returned, the stone rose under his hands and rolled over into the grass. In the hollow beneath it lay a bronze sword with a hilt of gold and a pair of golden sandals, untouched by rust, as though they had been waiting for him alone.

Theseus said nothing, turning the sword over and over in his hands. "These belonged to your father," said Aethra, kneeling beside him. "He is Aegeus, king of Athens, and before he went home he hid them here, telling me to send you to him on the day you could lift the stone." She begged him to make the crossing by ship, for the coast road was haunted by robbers who lay in wait for anyone foolish enough to travel it alone. Theseus would not hear of it; if he was truly the son of a king, he declared, he would walk to his father's door and make the road safe on the way.

And so he did, though the journey was every bit as perilous as his mother had warned. One after another, the robbers stepped out to meet him, each famous for some cruel trick he played on strangers, and each discovered that his trick did not work on Theseus. He overcame them all, and the shepherds and farmers who had lived in fear of them came out of their huts to cheer him as he passed. By the time he saw the rock of Athens rising above the plain, the story of the young stranger from the south had reached the city before him.

Aegeus had grown old and uneasy, troubled by quarrels at court and by a grief he never spoke of, and he did not know the young hero who came to his hall. Still, he gave the stranger a place at his table, and it was there, when the meat was brought in, that Theseus drew his bronze sword to carve it. The king stared at the golden hilt, rose trembling from his chair, and asked in a whisper where the weapon had come from. On hearing the answer, he took Theseus in his arms, weeping openly before the whole court, and named him prince of Athens and heir to the throne.

Joy did not last long in the palace, however, for the season of the tribute was approaching. Years earlier, the son of Minos, the mighty king of Crete, had died in Athens, and Minos, wild with sorrow, had brought his fleet against the city until it begged for peace. The price of that peace was a cruel one: each year Athens must send seven young men and seven young women to Crete in a ship with black sails. There they were led into the Labyrinth, the endless maze that the cunning craftsman Daedalus had built for the king. In its darkest heart dwelt the Minotaur, a creature with a man's body and a bull's head. Of all who had gone in, not one had ever come out again.

When the lots were drawn in the marketplace, and mothers cried out as the names were read, Theseus stood up before them all. The crowd fell silent. He would go of his own free will, he said, as one of the fourteen, and he would not come back until he had ended the tribute forever. Aegeus pleaded with him, reminding him that he had only just found his son, but Theseus was as immovable as the stone had once been. At last the old man gave way, and with shaking hands he gave the young prince a set of white sails. "If the gods bring you home, hoist these instead of the black ones," he said. "I shall sit on the cliff every day until I see them."

The voyage south took many days, and the black sails hung over the young people like a storm cloud. In Crete, the captives were brought before King Minos, a stern but not unjust ruler, and beside his throne stood his daughter Ariadne. The others wept and hung their heads, but Theseus looked the king in the eye, and it was this calm courage, more than his fine looks, that won Ariadne's heart. That night she crept through the sleeping palace to the place where the prisoners were kept, carrying a sword and a great ball of thread. "Fasten this to the door when you go in, and let it unwind behind you," she whispered. "However far the passages lead you, the thread will lead you home."

At dawn Theseus went into the Labyrinth, and he tied the end of the thread to the doorpost exactly as she had told him. The passages twisted, forked and doubled back, climbing and falling through the rock, and soon the last pale light behind him faded into complete darkness. He went on by touch alone, the thread slipping through his fingers, until a low bellowing echoed down the corridor. There, in the very center of the maze, the Minotaur was waiting for him. What followed was a long and fierce struggle in the dark, and when it was over, Theseus had defeated the monster. For a moment he leaned against the wall in the silence, breathing hard. Then, winding the thread back around his hand, he retraced every turn until he stepped out into the cool morning air.

Ariadne knew that her father would never forgive her, so she fled with Theseus and the young people of Athens, slipping out of the harbor before the city woke. They put in at the island of Naxos on the voyage home, and when the ship sailed on, Ariadne remained behind on that island. The rest of the journey passed in feasting and singing, and the young people, who had never expected to see their homes again, talked only of their waiting families. In all that gladness, not one of them remembered the white sails, which lay folded and forgotten below the deck.

Every day since the black ship had left, old Aegeus had climbed to the top of the cliff and sat gazing at the empty sea. At last a speck appeared on the horizon, grew into a ship, and came close enough for him to see its sails, still as black as on the morning it departed. Certain that his son was lost, the old king died of grief there on the cliff, his eyes still fixed on the water. So it was that Theseus, coming home in triumph, found the city in mourning, and the day of his greatest victory became the day he was crowned king. To this day, the sea that his father watched so faithfully bears his name, and sailors call it the Aegean.
