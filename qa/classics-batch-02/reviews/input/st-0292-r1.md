# The Hound of the Baskervilles

Summary: A country doctor brings Sherlock Holmes a strange old story about a giant hound on a lonely moor.

## Level A1

Sherlock Holmes was a famous detective. He lived in London with his friend Watson. One morning, a doctor came to Baker Street. His name was Mortimer, and he looked worried.

Mortimer told them an old family story. "A huge black hound lives on the moor. It follows the Baskerville family." "It is only an old tale," said Holmes.

Mortimer said, "Sir Charles Baskerville died. His weak heart stopped one night. Near him, I saw big footprints. They were the footprints of a giant hound!" Now Holmes listened carefully.

Sir Henry Baskerville got the big house. He came to Baker Street with a letter. It said, "Keep away from the moor." Holmes was busy in London. "Watson, please go with Sir Henry," said Holmes.

So Watson and Sir Henry went to Dartmoor. Baskerville Hall was dark and old. Barrymore and his wife looked after it. At night, Watson heard a woman crying. Their neighbor Stapleton loved butterflies. "The wet ground here is dangerous," said Stapleton.

One night, Watson saw Barrymore at a window. Barrymore held up a candle. Far away on the moor, a light answered. A man lived out there in secret. He was the brother of Barrymore's wife. He ran away from prison.

Later, Watson found a small stone hut. He waited inside in the dark. Then a man came to the door. It was Sherlock Holmes! "I was here all the time," said Holmes.

Holmes knew the answer now. Stapleton was secretly a Baskerville too. He wanted the big house and the money. He hid a huge dog on the moor. He put a strange paint on the dog. It glowed in the dark like a ghost.

Sir Henry walked home in the fog. Holmes and Watson waited behind some rocks. Suddenly, the glowing hound ran at Sir Henry. Holmes and Watson fired, and it fell. The hound did not get up again. Sir Henry was safe. Stapleton ran away into the wet moor. Nobody ever saw him again.

## Level A2

Sherlock Holmes was a famous detective in London. He lived in Baker Street with his friend Watson. One morning, a country doctor came from Dartmoor to see them. His name was Mortimer.

Mortimer read them an old story about the Baskerville family. Long ago, a cruel Baskerville did a wicked deed on the moor. Then a huge black hound came out of the night for him. From then on, the family feared the hound. "It is a fairy tale," said Holmes.

Mortimer said, "Three months ago, Sir Charles Baskerville died. He had a weak heart, and it stopped one night. On the ground near him, I saw footprints. They were the footprints of a gigantic hound!" Now Holmes sat up and listened carefully.

His nephew, Sir Henry, was the new owner of Baskerville Hall. Sir Henry came to London from his farm in a far country. Strange things happened to him there. A letter came to his hotel: "Keep away from the moor." Then someone took one of his boots. And a man with a black beard followed him. Holmes said, "I can't leave London now. Watson, please go to Dartmoor with Sir Henry."

Baskerville Hall stood alone in the middle of the moor. The butler, Barrymore, and his wife looked after it. At night, Watson heard a woman crying in the house. Soon Watson met Stapleton, a friendly man with a butterfly net. Stapleton lived at Merripit House with his sister. He showed Watson the Grimpen Mire, an area of soft ground. "Never walk there," said Stapleton.

One night, Watson saw Barrymore at a window with a candle. Barrymore held the candle up to the dark moor. Far away, a small light answered him. Then Barrymore's wife told the truth. She said, "The man out there is my brother. He escaped from prison, and he is hungry. We only give him food."

Soon Watson found a little stone hut on the moor. He waited inside in the dark. Then he heard steps and a calm voice. "Come out, Watson," said the voice. It was Sherlock Holmes! "I was on the moor all the time," said Holmes.

Holmes explained everything. Stapleton was secretly a Baskerville, and he wanted the family money. He kept a huge dog in a hidden place in the mire. A strange paint made the dog glow in the dark. The dog frightened Sir Charles, and his weak heart stopped. "Now Stapleton wants to frighten Sir Henry too," said Holmes.

Lestrade, a detective from London, came to help them. One evening, Sir Henry had dinner at Merripit House. Then he walked home across the moor in thick fog. Holmes, Watson and Lestrade waited behind the rocks. Suddenly, a giant hound jumped out of the fog. Its eyes and mouth shone like fire. Holmes and Watson fired, and the hound fell on the path. It did not move again. Sir Henry was very frightened, but he was safe.

Stapleton ran into the mire in the fog. In the morning, the detectives found Sir Henry's old boot there. But nobody ever found Stapleton.

## Level B1

Holmes and his friend Watson were having breakfast in Baker Street when a visitor arrived. He was a young country doctor called Mortimer, and he came from Dartmoor in Devon. He carried an old piece of paper, and he looked worried.

The paper told an old story about the Baskerville family. Long ago, a cruel Baskerville did a wicked deed out on the moor. That night, a huge black hound came out of the darkness for him. Since then, people said, the hound has followed the family. "An interesting fairy tale," said Holmes, and he lit his pipe.

Then Mortimer told them something more recent. Sir Charles Baskerville was a kind old man with a weak heart. Every evening, he walked on a path near his house. Three months ago, he did not come back from his walk. His heart had stopped, and his face showed great fear. Mortimer lowered his voice. "Mister Holmes, there were footprints near him. They were the footprints of a gigantic hound!"

Sir Charles had no children, so his nephew Henry got Baskerville Hall. Sir Henry was a cheerful young man who came from his farm across the ocean. In London, strange things happened to him at once. A letter came to his hotel with words cut from a newspaper. It said, "Keep away from the moor." A new brown boot and then an old black boot disappeared from his room. And a man with a black beard was following him in a cab. Holmes could not leave London, so he sent Watson to Dartmoor with Sir Henry. "Write to me every day," said Holmes.

Baskerville Hall was a dark old house in the middle of the empty moor. The butler, Barrymore, and his wife looked after it. On the first night, Watson heard a woman crying. In the morning, Barrymore's wife had red eyes. Soon Watson met a friendly neighbor called Stapleton, who chased butterflies with a net. Stapleton lived at Merripit House with a beautiful woman, and he called her his sister. He pointed at the Grimpen Mire, a huge area of soft green ground. Stapleton said, "Nobody can cross it safely. I know the secret paths, but one wrong step is enough." Later, his sister took Watson aside. "Go back to London at once!" she said. She thought he was Sir Henry.

One night, Watson saw Barrymore at a window, holding a candle up to the moor. Far away, a tiny light was answering. Barrymore's wife finally told the truth. The light belonged to her younger brother, who had escaped from prison. He was hiding on the moor, and she was giving him food. The same night, Watson saw a tall man standing on a rock against the moon. It was not the prisoner, so who was it?

Watson searched the moor and found an old stone hut. Inside, there was a blanket and some food. He sat down in the dark and waited. At last, he heard footsteps and then a calm, familiar voice. "Come out, my friend," said the voice. It was Sherlock Holmes! He had been living secretly on the moor all the time. He had also read all of Watson's letters.

Holmes explained the mystery. At Baskerville Hall, there was an old picture of the cruel Baskerville. Its face looked exactly like Stapleton's face. Stapleton was really a Baskerville, and he wanted the house and the money. The beautiful woman was not his sister; she was his wife. Stapleton kept a huge dog on an island in the mire. He covered it with a strange paint, so it glowed in the dark. One night, the glowing dog ran at Sir Charles, and his weak heart stopped. Now Stapleton wanted to frighten Sir Henry in the same way. The stolen boot gave the dog Sir Henry's smell.

Lestrade, a detective from London, came to help. On the last evening, Sir Henry had dinner at Merripit House. Then he walked home alone through thick fog. Holmes, Watson and Lestrade were waiting behind the rocks near the path. Suddenly, an enormous hound jumped out of the fog. Its eyes and mouth were shining like fire. It ran straight at Sir Henry, and he fell to the ground. Holmes and Watson fired, and the great hound fell down on the path. It never moved again. Sir Henry was badly frightened, but he was safe.

At Merripit House, they found Stapleton's wife locked in a room. She helped them at once. Stapleton himself ran into the Grimpen Mire in the dark. In the morning, the detectives followed his secret path and found Sir Henry's old boot. Then his footprints stopped, and nobody ever saw Stapleton again. Later, Sir Henry and Mortimer went on a long journey around the world.

## Level B2

On a gray autumn morning, Sherlock Holmes and his friend Watson received a visitor at their rooms in Baker Street. He was a tall, thin country doctor named Mortimer, and he had come all the way from Dartmoor, in Devon. From his pocket he took an old paper, more than a hundred and fifty years old, and he read it aloud. It told how a cruel member of the Baskerville family had once done a wicked deed on the moor. That same night, a great black hound had come out of the darkness for him. Ever since then, the family had lived under its shadow. Holmes listened with his eyes half closed. "Interesting," he said, "if you collect fairy tales."

Mortimer then told them about a newer event, and Holmes opened his eyes. Sir Charles Baskerville, the head of the family, had been a generous old man with a weak heart. Every evening, he walked along a dark path of tall trees beside his house. One night, three months earlier, he did not return, and his servants found him lying on the path. His heart had stopped, and his face was full of fear. Nobody saw anything strange, but Mortimer had looked closely at the ground. "There were footprints a little way off," he said in a low voice. "Mister Holmes, they were the footprints of a gigantic hound!"

Sir Charles had no children, so everything went to his nephew, Henry, who had been farming across the ocean. Sir Henry was a cheerful young man who did not believe in old stories, but London soon worried him. A letter reached his hotel, made of words cut out of a newspaper: "Keep away from the moor." Then a new brown boot vanished from his room, and the next day an old black one disappeared as well. Holmes and Watson also noticed a man with a black beard who followed Sir Henry through the streets in a cab. When Holmes ran after the cab, it raced away into the traffic. Holmes said he had too much work to leave London, so he asked Watson to go to Dartmoor with Sir Henry. "Never let him walk on the moor alone at night," Holmes warned, "and send me a report every day."

Baskerville Hall stood alone in the middle of the moor, a dark house surrounded by empty hills and strange old stones. It was looked after by the butler, Barrymore, and his wife. On the first night, Watson heard a woman crying bitterly, and in the morning Barrymore's wife had red, tired eyes. Soon Watson met their nearest neighbor, a cheerful naturalist called Stapleton, who carried a butterfly net wherever he went. Stapleton lived at Merripit House with a beautiful dark-haired woman whom he introduced as his sister. He showed Watson the Grimpen Mire, a wide stretch of bright green ground that looked solid but was soft and deep underneath. "Only I know the safe paths," he said proudly. "A stranger who tried to cross it would soon be in serious trouble." Later, while Stapleton was chasing a butterfly, his sister hurried up to Watson. "Go back to London tonight!" she whispered, believing he was Sir Henry, and she would not explain why.

The house had its own secrets. One night, Watson found Barrymore standing at a window and holding a candle up to the black moor. Far out in the darkness, a small light answered. At last, Barrymore's wife confessed the truth. Her younger brother had escaped from prison and was hiding on the moor. She had been sending him food and old clothes. Sir Henry felt sorry for her and agreed to say nothing. That same night, Watson saw a tall, thin figure standing on a high rock against the moon, quite still, before it disappeared. It was certainly not the prisoner, so who could it be?

Watson decided to find out. After a long search, he discovered an ancient stone hut on a hillside. Inside were a blanket, some tins of food and a note about his own movements. He sat in the shadows with his hand in his pocket and waited. Footsteps approached, and then a familiar voice spoke from outside. "Come out, Watson," it said. "You will find the evening much more pleasant out here." It was Sherlock Holmes. He had been living secretly on the moor for days. A hidden watcher, he explained, could see far more than a famous detective in a big house.

Holmes had solved the mystery, and he explained it carefully. In the hall at Baskerville, an old portrait showed the cruel Baskerville from the legend, and its face was exactly like Stapleton's. Stapleton was really a son of Sir Charles's younger brother, and after Sir Henry he would inherit everything. The beautiful woman was not his sister at all, but his wife. He had bought a huge, powerful dog in London and hidden it on an island deep in the Grimpen Mire. He painted its face and fur with a chemical that glowed in the dark. At night, it looked just like the hound from the old story. One evening he brought the dog to the path where Sir Charles walked. The shock was too much for the old man's weak heart. Now Stapleton planned to frighten Sir Henry in the same way, and the stolen boot had given the dog Sir Henry's smell.

The next day, Lestrade, an official detective from London, arrived to help. That evening, Sir Henry dined at Merripit House as Holmes had told him, and then he started to walk home alone. Holmes, Watson and Lestrade lay waiting behind the rocks near the path. Meanwhile, a thick white fog was rolling in from the mire. Suddenly, out of the fog came an enormous black hound, its eyes and mouth shining with a cold, ghostly fire. It raced past the three men and leapt toward Sir Henry, who fell to the ground in shock. Holmes and Watson fired together, and the great creature fell on the path and lay still. Sir Henry had fainted, but he was not hurt.

In Merripit House they found Stapleton's wife locked in an upstairs room, and she gladly told them about his hiding place. Stapleton himself had run into the mire in the darkness and fog. The next morning, they followed the small sticks that marked his secret path. There, half sunk in the mud, lay Sir Henry's old black boot. Beyond the boot, there were no more footprints, and Stapleton was never seen again. Sir Henry's nerves needed rest, so he and Mortimer set off on a long journey around the world. Back in Baker Street, Holmes went over the whole case with Watson. Then, with a smile, he suggested an evening at the opera.

## Level C1

It was a gray autumn morning in Baker Street, and Sherlock Holmes was amusing Watson with a walking stick. An unknown visitor had left it behind the evening before, and Holmes was deducing everything about its owner from the marks on its wood. The owner himself soon arrived: a tall, thin, eager young country doctor named Mortimer, who had come all the way from Dartmoor, in Devon. Folded in his pocket was an old paper, and he opened it with great care. It recorded a family legend, written down more than a century and a half before, about a cruel Baskerville who had once done a wicked deed on the moor. On that very night, the paper said, a great black hound had come out of the darkness for him. Ever since, the Baskervilles had been warned never to cross the moor after dark. Holmes heard it all with his eyes half closed. When Mortimer finished, he remarked dryly that the story would interest any collector of fairy tales.

Mortimer, however, had not come to London merely to read old papers aloud. Three months earlier, Sir Charles Baskerville, the generous old head of the family, had gone out for his usual evening walk. His path ran between tall, dark trees beside his house, and he had never come back from it. His servants had found him lying there; his weak heart had stopped, and his face still showed such fear that Mortimer could hardly recognize it. Nobody else had noticed anything unusual, but the doctor had examined the ground with care. "There were footprints a little distance away," he said, lowering his voice, "and they were not a man's, Mister Holmes; they were the footprints of a gigantic hound!"

The estate had passed to Sir Charles's nephew, Henry, who had spent years farming on the far side of the ocean. He was a cheerful, practical young man who believed in neither curses nor ghosts. London, however, gave him an unusual welcome. A letter reached his hotel, its message built from words cut out of a newspaper, warning him to keep away from the moor. A new brown boot vanished from his room, and the following day, rather oddly, an old black one disappeared instead. Holmes found the second theft far more interesting than the first. Then he and Watson noticed a man with a thick black beard following Sir Henry through the busy streets in a cab. When Holmes ran after it, the driver whipped up his horse and was lost in the traffic. Since his work kept him in London, Holmes asked Watson to travel down to Dartmoor as Sir Henry's companion. His orders were strict: Watson was to send detailed reports and never to let the young man walk on the moor alone at night.

Baskerville Hall rose out of the moor like a dark island, with endless brown hills around it and ancient stone circles on the slopes. Somewhere beyond the hills stood a prison, whose bells could be heard on still days. The house was kept by the butler, Barrymore, and his wife, a solid, serious woman. On Watson's first morning her eyes were red from weeping, though her husband insisted that nobody in the house had cried. Watson had heard the sobbing himself in the middle of the night, and he began to watch the butler more closely. Their nearest neighbor turned out to be a small, smiling naturalist called Stapleton, who seemed to spend his whole life chasing butterflies with a green net. He lived at Merripit House with a strikingly beautiful woman whom he introduced as his sister. With obvious pleasure, he showed Watson the great Grimpen Mire, whose bright green surface hid deep, soft ground. "I alone know the paths through it," he said with quiet pride, "and a stranger who tried them would soon be in very serious trouble." Later, while Stapleton was off chasing a rare butterfly, the lady hurried over to Watson. In a low, anxious voice, she urged him to return to London at once, for she had mistaken him for Sir Henry. When she realized her error, she refused to say another word on the subject.

The house had secrets of its own. Late one night, Watson caught Barrymore standing at a window, holding a candle up to the black moor and moving it slowly, as if signaling. Far out in the darkness, a tiny point of light was answering him. Pressed by Sir Henry, Barrymore's wife finally confessed that the man on the moor was her younger brother, an escaped prisoner. She had been leaving food and old clothes for him, because she could not bear to let him go hungry. Sir Henry, who was kinder than he looked, agreed to keep their secret. Yet that same night, as Watson walked back across the moor, he saw something which no confession explained. A tall, thin figure was standing perfectly still on a high rock, outlined against the rising moon, and then it vanished into the dark.

Determined to find this watcher, Watson searched the hillsides until he discovered an ancient stone hut. Inside lay a blanket, some tins of food and, to his astonishment, a note recording his own movements. He settled into the shadows with his hand in his pocket and waited for the owner to return. At last, footsteps approached, and then a voice he knew better than any other spoke from outside. It invited him to come out and enjoy the evening air, which was far more agreeable than a damp hut. It was Sherlock Holmes, who had been living secretly on the moor for days. An unseen watcher, he had reasoned, would learn far more than a famous detective sitting in a large house. Watson was hurt to discover that his careful letters had been sent on to the moor all along. His annoyance faded quickly, however, for Holmes had nearly finished the case.

The solution, as Holmes explained it, was cleverer and colder than any legend. In the hall at Baskerville hung a portrait of the cruel ancestor from the old story. When Holmes covered the painted hat and wig with his hand, the face which remained was unmistakably Stapleton's. The naturalist was in fact the son of Sir Charles's younger brother, and after Sir Henry he would inherit the house and the fortune. The lady at Merripit House was not his sister at all, but his wife. He had bought an enormous, powerful dog in London and hidden it on an island deep inside the mire. Then he had painted its face and coat with a chemical that glowed in the dark, so that it resembled the hound of the old tale. It was the sight of this glowing beast, brought to the path one evening, that had stopped Sir Charles's weak heart. Now Stapleton meant to frighten his cousin in the same way. The stolen old boot, Holmes added, had simply given the dog Sir Henry's scent.

Lestrade, an official detective from London, arrived by the afternoon train, and the trap was set. That evening, following Holmes's instructions, Sir Henry dined at Merripit House and then set off to walk home alone across the moor. Holmes, Watson and Lestrade lay hidden among the rocks beside the path. To Holmes's dismay, a thick white fog came creeping out of the mire and began to swallow the path, inch by inch. Soon Sir Henry's footsteps could be heard long before he could be seen. Then, out of the fog, burst an enormous black hound, its eyes and mouth glowing with a cold, unearthly fire. It raced past the three hidden men toward the young baronet, who fell to the ground in shock. Holmes and Watson fired together, and the great creature fell on the path and lay still. Sir Henry had fainted, but when he opened his eyes, he found his friends kneeling beside him, and he was unhurt.

At Merripit House they found Stapleton's wife locked in an upstairs room. Once she understood that her husband's plan had failed, she told them at once where his hiding place lay. Stapleton himself had fled into the mire in the darkness and the fog. The next morning, guided by the small sticks that marked his secret path, they found Sir Henry's old black boot half sunk in the mud. Stapleton had evidently carried it with him and then thrown it aside. Beyond the boot, however, the soft green ground kept its secret; there were no more footprints, and Stapleton was never seen again.

Sir Henry's nerves had been badly shaken, so he set off with the loyal Mortimer on a long voyage around the world. A month later, on a raw and foggy evening in Baker Street, Holmes went over the whole case with Watson. Then, with perfect calm, he proposed that they spend the evening at the opera.
