# The Blue Cross

Summary: A famous French detective chases a clever thief across London by following a trail of strange clues.

## Level A1

Valentin was a great detective from Paris. He came to London by train from Harwich. He wanted to catch Flambeau. Flambeau was a famous thief. He was very tall and very clever.

A small priest sat on the train. His name was Father Brown. He had a big umbrella and many parcels. "I have a silver cross!" said Father Brown. It had blue stones. Valentin felt sorry for the silly little man.

In London, Valentin had no clues. So he looked for strange things. In a small restaurant, the sugar was salt! The waiter told him about two priests. The short priest threw soup at the wall.

At a fruit shop, the signs were wrong. The orange sign was on the nuts. A priest knocked over the apples there.

Valentin took two policemen with him. They found a restaurant with a broken window. "A priest paid too much," said the waiter. Then the little priest broke the window. A priest left a parcel in a shop. The shop woman sent it to Westminster.

At last, they came to Hampstead. It was evening. Two priests sat on a bench. One was short, and one was very tall. Valentin and the policemen hid behind a tree.

The tall priest looked up at the stars. "Nothing makes sense up there," he said. "Reason is the same there," said Father Brown. Then the tall priest stood up. It was Flambeau! "Your cross is in my parcel," said Flambeau. Father Brown only smiled. "No, there is only paper in it. The cross is in the post," said Brown.

"How did you know me?" asked Flambeau. "You spoke against reason. A real priest never does that. I left all those strange clues. They brought the police here," said Father Brown.

Valentin and the policemen came out. They took Flambeau away. Valentin took off his hat to Brown. "He is the best of us," said Valentin. But Father Brown only looked for his umbrella.

## Level A2

Valentin was the head of the police in Paris. He was also the cleverest detective in France. One morning he came off a boat at Harwich. Then he took the train to London. He wanted to catch Flambeau, the most famous thief in the world. Flambeau was a master of disguise. But he could not hide one thing: he was very tall.

On the train, Valentin looked for tall men, but there were none. There was only a short priest from Essex with a round face. His name was Father Brown. He carried a big umbrella and lots of brown paper parcels. "One of my parcels has a silver cross in it. It has blue stones, so I must be careful!" he told everyone. To Valentin, the little priest seemed very silly.

In London, Valentin had no clues. So he decided to follow anything strange. He sat down in a small restaurant and ordered coffee. When he put sugar in his cup, it tasted of salt! The waiter told him about two priests at breakfast. The short one threw his soup at the wall.

Outside a fruit shop, Valentin saw two wrong signs. The orange sign was on the nuts. The nut sign was on the oranges. "A priest knocked over my apples this morning. Then he went up that street," said the angry shopkeeper.

Valentin asked two policemen to come with him. They rode on a bus and watched the streets. Soon Valentin saw a restaurant with a broken window. The waiter told them about two priests at lunch. The short one paid too much money. "That pays for the window," he said, and he broke the glass. Next they found a sweet shop. A priest left a small parcel there. He asked the woman to post it to Westminster.

At last the three men came to Hampstead. The sun went down, and the first stars came out. Two priests sat on a bench under a tree. One was short and round. The other was very tall. Valentin and the policemen hid behind the tree and listened.

The tall priest looked at the sky. "Out in the stars, maybe two and two make five," he said. "No, reason is the same everywhere," answered Father Brown. Suddenly the tall priest stood up, and his voice changed. "Your cross is already in my parcel," said Flambeau. Father Brown was not afraid. "I saw you change the parcels, so I changed them back. The real cross is in the post now," said Father Brown.

Flambeau looked at him with surprise. "How did you know me?" he asked. "You spoke against reason. A real priest never does that. So I left strange signs for the police. I put the salt in the sugar bowl and threw the soup. I broke the window too," said Father Brown.

Valentin and the policemen came out from behind the tree. Flambeau did not run. Valentin took off his hat. "Let us both bow to this little man. He is the master of us both," Valentin said to Flambeau. And Father Brown looked around for his umbrella.

## Level B1

Valentin, the head of the Paris police, was the most famous detective in Europe. One bright morning he landed at Harwich and took the train to London. He was following Flambeau, a thief who was famous in every country. Flambeau could change his face, his clothes and his voice. But he could not change his height, because he was a giant of a man.

On the train there were no tall men at all. At a small station in Essex, a short priest got on. He had a round, simple face, a huge umbrella and lots of brown paper parcels. His name was Father Brown, and he was going to a big meeting of priests. "I must be careful, because one of these parcels has a silver cross in it. It has blue stones too!" he told everyone on the train. Valentin felt sorry for the little man, who was telling his secret to the world.

In London, Valentin had no idea where to look. At such times he always followed the strangest thing he could find. He sat down in a small restaurant and put some sugar in his coffee. It was salt! The salt pot, of course, was full of sugar. The waiter remembered two priests who ate there that morning. "The short one threw his soup at the wall before he left," he said.

At a fruit shop further down the street, Valentin noticed two strange signs. The sign for the nuts was on the oranges. And the orange sign was on the nuts! "Two priests came past this morning," said the shopkeeper angrily. "The little one knocked all my apples into the road."

Now Valentin asked two London policemen to come with him. They took a bus going north and watched the streets carefully. Soon Valentin saw a restaurant with a broken window, and they jumped off. The waiter told them about a short priest at lunch. The priest paid far too much, and the waiter tried to give some money back. "No, that pays for the window," said the priest, and he broke it. Next, in a sweet shop, the woman remembered the same little priest. He left a parcel with her and asked her to post it to Westminster.

At last the three men came to the hills of Hampstead. The evening was blue and quiet, and the first stars were coming out. Two priests were sitting on a bench under a tree. One was the little priest from the train, and the other was very tall. Valentin and the policemen crept behind the tree and listened.

"In other worlds, maybe reason means nothing," the tall priest was saying. "No, reason is always reasonable, even on the farthest star," said Father Brown. Suddenly the tall priest stood up, and his voice was not gentle any more. "Give me that cross, because we are alone here," said Flambeau. "But I think you have it already," Father Brown answered quietly. Flambeau laughed. "Yes, I changed the parcels on the way," he said. "I know, so I changed them back. The real cross is going to Westminster by post," said the little priest.

For the first time in his life, Flambeau did not know what to say. "How did you know that I was not a priest?" he asked at last. "You spoke against reason, and a real priest never does that. I also saw a thief's bracelet on your arm. So I left a clue in every place we visited. I swapped the salt and the sugar, threw the soup and broke the window. Those clues brought the police after us. They are behind that tree now," said Father Brown.

Valentin stepped out with the two policemen, and Flambeau did not run. The great detective took off his hat. "Let us both bow to this man, Flambeau. He is the master of us both," he said. The little priest just blinked and looked around for his umbrella.

## Level B2

Early one silver morning, a boat came into Harwich. A quiet man in ordinary clothes stepped onto the shore. Nobody looked at him twice, and that was exactly what he wanted. He was Valentin, the head of the Paris police and the most famous detective in Europe. He had come to England to catch the greatest thief of the age, a man called Flambeau. Valentin was a man of cold, clear reason, and he did not believe in luck.

Flambeau had played tricks on the police of half a dozen countries. He was known for clever crimes that made people laugh as much as they made them angry. He was also a master of disguise, but there was one thing he could never hide: he was enormously tall. Valentin knew that Flambeau was planning something during a large meeting of priests in London. So all the way to the city, he watched the faces on the train, looking for a giant.

He saw no giant, only ordinary travelers. At a little station in Essex, a short Catholic priest climbed into the carriage. He had a face as round and dull as a pudding and a big shabby umbrella. He also carried several brown paper parcels, which he kept dropping. His name, Valentin later learned, was Father Brown. "I have to be very careful. One of these parcels holds a silver cross with blue stones in it," the priest kept telling everyone who would listen. Valentin felt a kind of pity for this simple man, who was announcing his treasure to a whole train.

In London, the detective had nothing to go on. When a case gave him no clue, Valentin had a strange method of his own. He would walk without a plan and stop at the first thing that seemed out of place. In his experience, the unusual often led somewhere. In a quiet street he went into a small restaurant and ordered coffee. When he tasted it, he found that the sugar bowl was full of salt. The salt cellar, of course, was full of sugar. The waiter, who was as surprised as he was, suddenly remembered two priests who had come in that morning. "The short one threw his soup at the wall," the waiter said, "and then the two of them walked out."

Valentin followed the street until he reached a fruit shop. There, the sign for oranges had been placed on a pile of nuts. The label for the nuts was sitting on the oranges. The shopkeeper was still angry. "A pair of priests went past earlier," he complained, "and the little one knocked my apples all over the road."

By now Valentin was certain that he was on a real trail, and he asked two London policemen to come with him. They rode north on the top of a bus, watching the streets carefully. Suddenly Valentin saw a restaurant with a broken window, and they all jumped off. The waiter there explained that a short priest had paid for lunch and left far too much money. When the waiter pointed this out, the priest replied that the extra paid for the window, and then he broke it. A little further on, in a sweet shop, the woman behind the counter remembered the same pair. The little priest had left a parcel there and asked her to post it to an address in Westminster.

The trail ended on the open hills of Hampstead, just as the sun went down. The evening sky was a deep blue, and the first stars were shining. On a bench under a tree sat two priests. One of them was the dull little man from the train, and the other was very tall. Valentin and the policemen crept up behind the tree and listened.

Strangely, the two men were talking about the stars and about reason. The tall priest suggested that in other worlds, far away in space, reason might not work at all. For a few minutes Valentin wondered whether he had made a terrible mistake. The two men sounded exactly like ordinary priests discussing the heavens. Father Brown disagreed quietly but firmly. "Reason and justice reach even the farthest star. If you went there, you would still find that two and two make four," he said.

Then the tall priest stood up, and his voice became hard. "Give me that cross, my friend, because we are quite alone here," said Flambeau. Father Brown did not look frightened at all. "I think you already have it. Didn't you change my parcel for a false one on the way?" he asked. Flambeau laughed and admitted that he had. "Yes, but I saw you do it," said the little priest, "so I changed them back again. The real cross is in the post, on its way to a friend in Westminster."

For a long moment, the great thief could not speak. "How did you know who I was?" he asked at last. "First, you spoke against reason, and that is bad theology. No real priest would do that. Second, I noticed a thief's bracelet under your sleeve. I hear a great many confessions, so I know a great deal about wrong deeds. That is why I left all those strange signs behind us. I swapped the salt and sugar, threw the soup, upset the apples and broke the window. I knew someone would follow them, and now the police are standing behind that tree," Father Brown replied.

Valentin stepped out with his two policemen, and Flambeau made no attempt to run. The famous detective took off his hat. "Let us both bow, Flambeau," he said, "to the master of us both." They stood there for a moment with bare heads, while the little priest from Essex blinked and looked around for his umbrella.

## Level C1

On a bright morning, when the sea behind Harwich glittered like a sheet of silver, a small and unremarkable man stepped off the boat among the crowd of travelers. There was nothing about him to attract a second glance, which was precisely the point, for this was Valentin, head of the Paris police and the most celebrated detective alive. He had crossed the water on the trail of Flambeau, a thief whose daring had amused and infuriated the newspapers of half a dozen countries for years.

Flambeau's crimes had a theatrical flavor that the public adored. Once, it was said, he had repainted the numbers on an entire street overnight simply so that a single traveler would walk into his trap. Yet for all his genius with wigs, voices and false names, there was one thing the great thief could never disguise: he was a giant, far taller than ordinary men. A man may change his hair, his accent and his manners, Valentin liked to say, but he cannot easily lose a foot of height. Valentin had learned that Flambeau meant to use a great gathering of priests in London as cover for some new scheme. All the way to the capital, therefore, he studied every face in the train with patient attention, measuring each passenger against the giant he expected.

Not one of his fellow travelers came anywhere near the right height. At a little station in Essex, however, a short Catholic priest clambered aboard. He carried a battered umbrella that kept falling to the floor and a heap of brown paper parcels that he could barely hold. His face was as round and as dull as a pudding, and he seemed entirely unable to keep track of his ticket. This was Father Brown, though Valentin did not yet know his name. "I really must take care, because one of these parcels contains real silver," the little man confided to anyone in the carriage who would listen. He even added, with an anxious smile, that it was set with blue stones. Valentin, a skeptic who had little patience with priests, nevertheless felt a flicker of pity for a man so innocent that he announced his treasure to an entire train.

London swallowed the detective, and for an hour he had nothing whatever to go on. In such moments Valentin relied upon a method that his colleagues found eccentric. Since reason alone could not tell him where an unpredictable criminal had gone, he would deliberately wander and stop wherever something looked wrong. When there was no obvious thread to pull, he argued, the unexpected was the only one worth pulling. So it was that he found himself in a modest restaurant in a quiet street, stirring what he took to be sugar into his coffee. It was salt, and when he examined the salt cellar, he discovered that it had been filled with sugar. The waiter, scandalized, could offer only one explanation. Two clergymen had breakfasted there that morning, and as they were leaving, the shorter of the pair had thrown his soup straight at the wall.

A few doors further on, outside a greengrocer's, Valentin noticed something equally odd. The card advertising oranges had been propped against a heap of nuts, while the card for the nuts lay among the oranges. The shopkeeper, still smarting, explained that two priests had passed by earlier and that the little one had sent a whole basket of apples rolling into the road. Valentin no longer doubted that he was on a genuine trail, however absurd it appeared, and he persuaded two London policemen to accompany him.

They rode north on the open top of a bus, the detective scanning every shopfront until, with a sudden cry, he spotted a restaurant whose window had been smashed. Neither of the policemen had understood what they were looking for, and Valentin could not have told them, since he hardly knew himself. Inside, the waiter told them that a little priest had paid for his lunch and left a sum far larger than the bill. When the waiter tried to return the difference, the priest had calmly remarked that the extra money would cover the window, and then he had broken the glass himself. A short distance away, a woman in a sweet shop recalled the same two clergymen. The smaller one had left a parcel on her counter and asked her to send it by post to an address in Westminster, which she had duly done.

By the time the three men reached Hampstead, the light was fading over the wide, open hills. The sky had turned the deep blue of evening, and one by one the stars were appearing above the trees. On a bench beneath a large tree sat two figures in black: one was the dumpy little priest from the train, and the other was immensely tall. Valentin and the policemen crept up behind the trunk and listened, hardly daring to breathe.

The conversation they overheard was hardly what one expects of a master criminal. The tall priest was speaking, rather dreamily, about the vastness of the heavens, and suggesting that in some distant universe reason itself might turn out to be unreasonable. For several uneasy minutes Valentin wondered whether he had chased two harmless scholars halfway across London. Father Brown, however, answered with quiet conviction. "Reason and justice hold even on the loneliest star," he said, "and if you travelled there, you would still find that two and two make four. The universe may be vast, but it is not foolish."

At that, the tall man rose to his feet, and the mildness drained out of his voice. "Hand over that cross, since there is nobody here to help you," said Flambeau. Father Brown remained seated, blinking up at him with every appearance of calm. "But surely you have it already," he said, "for didn't you exchange my parcel for a dummy somewhere along the way?" Flambeau, delighted with himself, admitted that he had, and asked how a country priest could possibly know such an old trick. "Because I noticed you doing it," replied the little man, "and so I quietly changed the parcels back again. The real cross left a sweet shop this afternoon and is travelling by post to a friend in Westminster."

For perhaps the first time in his career, Flambeau was lost for words. When at last he found his voice, he asked how this dull little cleric had seen through him at all. "In the first place," said Father Brown, "you attacked reason, which is bad theology, and no genuine priest would ever do so. In the second, I caught sight of a thief's bracelet beneath your sleeve, and a man who hears confessions learns a great deal about the wrongs people do. So, from the moment I suspected you, I made sure our journey would be remembered. I changed the salt and sugar, I sent the soup against the wall, I upset the apples, and I paid for a window before I broke it. Every one of those oddities was a signpost, and I fancy the police who followed them are standing behind that tree at this moment."

He was right. Valentin stepped into the open with his two policemen, and Flambeau, who was not a fool, made no attempt to escape. The detective, however, did not look at his prisoner with triumph. Instead he took off his hat, and turning to the thief, he spoke with real respect. "Let us both bow, Flambeau, to the master of us both," he said. For a moment the great detective and the great thief stood bareheaded together in the twilight. Between them, the little priest from Essex blinked about him in search of his umbrella.
