# The Canterville Ghost

Summary: An American family buys an old English house, but its ghost cannot make them afraid.

## Level A1

Mr Otis came from America. He bought an old house in England. Its name was Canterville. "A ghost lives there," said Lord Canterville. Mr Otis only laughed. "There are no ghosts," he said.

The Otis family moved into the house. Mrs Otis came with her son Washington. Little Virginia and the twins came too. Mrs Umney worked in the house. In the library, they saw a red stain. "It is very old," said Mrs Umney. "It never goes away."

Washington cleaned the stain. But the next morning, it was back! He cleaned it again and again. The red stain always came back.

The ghost was Sir Simon. Long ago, he did a bad thing. Now he walked at night with chains. "Your chains are loud," said Mr Otis. "Use this oil." Sir Simon was very angry.

He tried to scare the family. But nobody was afraid of him. The twins threw pillows at him. They put butter on the floor. Sir Simon fell down. Then he saw a second ghost. Sir Simon ran away! It was only a sheet. The twins made it.

The stain came back every day. But now it was purple or green. Somebody took Virginia's paints too. Soon the ghost felt tired and sad.

One day, Virginia found him upstairs. "You took my paints!" said Virginia. "Yes, I needed red," said Sir Simon. "I never sleep, and I am tired. I want to rest in a quiet garden. A kind girl must pray for me."

Virginia took his cold hand. They went into the wall together. The family looked for her for hours. At midnight, Virginia came back. She had a box of jewels. "Sir Simon is at rest now," she said.

In the garden, an old tree had flowers. The family made a quiet place for him. Virginia put flowers there. The old house was quiet at last.

## Level A2

Mr Hiram B. Otis was an American in England. He wanted an old house, so he bought Canterville. Lord Canterville gave him a warning. "There is a ghost in the house," he said. "My family saw it many times." Mr Otis only laughed. "Then sell me the ghost too," he said. "We can put it in a museum in America!"

The family moved in on a summer evening. Mr and Mrs Otis came with their son Washington. Virginia was fifteen, and she was kind and brave. The twins were young boys, and they loved jokes. An old woman, Mrs Umney, looked after the house.

In the library, Mrs Otis saw a red stain on the floor. "It is very old," said Mrs Umney. "Long ago, Sir Simon did a bad thing here. Nobody can clean the stain away." Washington rubbed it with a small cleaning stick, and it disappeared. Then there was a loud crash of thunder. Poor Mrs Umney almost fell over. The next morning, the stain was back.

That night, Mr Otis heard heavy chains in the hall. He opened his door and saw an old ghost. "Your chains are very loud," said Mr Otis. "Please put some oil on them." He gave the ghost a small bottle and went back to bed.

Sir Simon was very angry. For three hundred years, everybody was afraid of him! Then the twins came out and threw pillows at him. The ghost quickly went away through the wall.

At night, he laughed a terrible laugh. But Mrs Otis only offered him some medicine. Every morning, the stain came back in a new color. Some days it was red, and other days it was green. At the same time, Virginia's paints went missing.

The twins had more tricks. They put butter on the floor, and Sir Simon fell. Then a jug of water fell on him from a door. One night, he met another ghost in the hall! Sir Simon ran back to his room in fear. In the morning, he looked again. It was only a sheet and a big vegetable. The twins made it, of course.

Soon Sir Simon felt tired and sad. One afternoon, Virginia found him by a window upstairs. "You took my paints, and stealing is wrong," said Virginia. "I needed something red," said Sir Simon. "I never sleep, and I am so tired. I want to rest in a quiet garden far away. An old poem on the library window talks about it. A kind girl must cry for me and pray for me."

Virginia was afraid, but she took his cold hand. The wall opened, and they went through it together. The family looked for Virginia everywhere. At midnight, she came down the stairs with a box of jewels. "Sir Simon gave me these," said Virginia. "He can rest now."

She showed them a small secret room behind the wall. In the garden, an old dry tree suddenly had white flowers. Later, the family made a quiet resting place for Sir Simon. Virginia put flowers there, and the house was peaceful at last.

## Level B1

Mr Hiram B. Otis was an American who worked in London for his country. He decided to buy Canterville, a large old house in the countryside. Lord Canterville gave him a serious warning. "A ghost lives in that house," said the old lord. "My family has seen it many times." Mr Otis just smiled. "Then I will take the ghost with the furniture," he said. "In America, we will put him in a museum."

The Otis family arrived on a warm summer evening. Mrs Otis was cheerful, and Washington, their eldest son, was a polite young man. Virginia was fifteen, with large blue eyes, and she loved riding her pony. The twins were two noisy little boys who loved jokes. An old housekeeper, Mrs Umney, welcomed them at the door.

During tea in the library, Mrs Otis noticed a dark red stain by the fire. "It is very old, madam," said Mrs Umney. "Long ago, Sir Simon did a terrible thing in this room. Since then, nobody has been able to clean the stain away." Washington took a small black cleaning stick from his pocket. He rubbed the floor, and soon the stain disappeared. At that moment, lightning lit up the room, and thunder shook the house. But the next morning, the red stain was back. Washington cleaned it again, but it kept coming back.

That night, the sound of heavy chains woke Mr Otis at one o'clock. He opened his door and saw a very old man with wild gray hair. "My dear sir, those chains are far too loud," said Mr Otis. "Please put some of this oil on them." He left a small bottle on a table and went back to bed.

Sir Simon was so angry that he could not move. Then he threw the bottle down and rushed along the hall. Suddenly a door opened, and the twins threw two big pillows at his head! The ghost escaped through the wall.

For three hundred years, everyone was afraid of Sir Simon. He laughed his most terrible laugh in the dark. Mrs Otis only came out and offered him some medicine for his stomach. He put on an old suit of armor, but it fell with a great crash. Meanwhile, the stain came back every morning in a new color. Sometimes it was red, sometimes purple, and once it was bright green. At the same time, the paints in Virginia's paint box kept disappearing.

The twins played more tricks on him. They made a slide of butter on the floor, and the ghost fell. A jug of water fell on him from a door, and he caught a cold. One night, he met another ghost with a round, shining face. Sir Simon was so frightened that he ran back to his room. In the morning, he went back to look at it. It was only a sheet, a brush and a hollow vegetable with a candle inside. The twins made it, of course.

After that, Sir Simon was too tired and sad to frighten anyone. The young Duke of Cheshire, who loved Virginia, came to stay. One afternoon, Virginia found Sir Simon in an empty room upstairs. He was sitting alone by the window. "I am sorry for you," she said kindly. "But you took my paints, and that was wrong." "I needed something red for the stain," said Sir Simon sadly.

"I have not slept for three hundred years, and I am so tired," he went on. "Far away, there is a quiet garden where the grass grows tall. I want to rest there, but I cannot go alone. An old poem on the library window says a kind girl can help me. She must cry for me and pray for me. Then the dry old almond tree will flower, and the house will be peaceful." Virginia was afraid, but she was also brave. "I will help you," she said. The wall opened like a door, and they went through it together.

The whole family searched the house for hours. At midnight, Virginia came down the stairs with a little box of jewels. "Sir Simon gave me these," she said. "He is at peace now." She led them through a secret door into a tiny, dusty room. From its window, one of the twins saw the old almond tree. In the moonlight, it was full of white flowers.

Later, the family gave Sir Simon a quiet resting place under an old tree. Virginia laid flowers there. Mr Otis wanted to return the jewels, but Lord Canterville said no. "They belong to Virginia now," he said. "She was kind to Sir Simon when nobody else was." Years later, Virginia married the Duke of Cheshire. He asked her about that night, but she only smiled. "Sir Simon showed me what life and death mean," she said. "He taught me why love is stronger than both."

## Level B2

When Mr Hiram B. Otis, an American diplomat, bought Canterville, everyone said he was making a mistake. Lord Canterville himself was honest enough to warn him. "The house has a ghost," he said. "My family has seen it many times, and none of us could ever sleep there in peace." Mr Otis was not impressed in the least. "If there were a ghost in Europe, we would have bought it already for one of our museums," he replied. "I will take the furniture and the ghost at a fair price."

The family arrived on a lovely summer evening. Mrs Otis was a handsome, practical woman, and Washington, their eldest son, had excellent manners. Virginia, a girl of fifteen with large blue eyes, rode her pony faster than anyone in the area. Finally, there were the twins, two lively little boys who were always being told off for their jokes. An old housekeeper, Mrs Umney, served them tea in the library.

It was there that Mrs Otis noticed a dark red stain on the floor beside the fireplace. "It cannot be cleaned, madam," said Mrs Umney in a trembling voice. "Long ago, Sir Simon did a wicked deed in this very room, and the stain has been there ever since. Soon afterwards he vanished, and his ghost still walks these halls." Washington only laughed. He took a small black cleaning stick out of his pocket and rubbed until the stain was gone. At that moment, lightning filled the room and a crash of thunder shook the house. Mrs Umney fell into a chair with a cry. The next morning, however, the stain was back in its old place. Washington removed it again and again, but it always returned.

That night, Mr Otis was woken at one o'clock by a strange metallic sound in the corridor. He put on his slippers, opened his door and found himself face to face with a very old man. His gray hair was long and wild, and heavy chains hung from his arms and ankles. "My dear sir," said Mr Otis politely, "I really must ask you to oil those chains. I have brought you a small bottle for the purpose." He placed the bottle on a table and went back to bed.

The old ghost stood still for a moment, almost speechless with anger. Then he threw the bottle to the floor and hurried down the corridor, groaning loudly. At once, a door flew open, two small figures in white appeared, and a large pillow flew past his head. He escaped through the wall and went back to his secret room to think. In three hundred years, he had frightened lords and ladies, servants and bishops. Never once had he been treated like this.

Over the following weeks, he tried every trick he knew. He let out his most terrible laugh at midnight. Mrs Otis simply opened her door and offered him some medicine for his stomach. He put on an old suit of armor, but it was too heavy and fell down the stairs with a crash. Meanwhile, the stain kept returning each morning, though its color had become rather strange. Some days it was dull red, some days purple, and once it was a bright emerald green. At the same time, the paints in Virginia's paint box kept mysteriously running out.

The twins never stopped their jokes. They made a butter slide in the corridor, and the ghost slipped and fell. They balanced a jug of water on a half-open door, and he caught a terrible cold. Worst of all, one night he turned a corner and met a horrible ghost with a wide grin. Sir Simon, who had never seen another ghost before, ran back to bed and hid his face. The next morning, he discovered that it was only an old sheet, a brush and a hollow vegetable. It had been made by the twins, of course.

After that, Sir Simon grew weak and nervous and hardly left his room. Not even the young Duke of Cheshire, who was in love with Virginia, could tempt him out. One afternoon, however, Virginia found the ghost in an empty room upstairs, sitting by the window. He looked so lonely that she forgot to be afraid. "I am very sorry for you," she said, "but you shouldn't have taken my paints. I couldn't paint any sunsets after that." "I needed something red for the stain," said Sir Simon. "When the red was gone, I had to use whatever was left."

Then he spoke more seriously. "I haven't slept for three hundred years, and I am so very tired. Beyond the pine woods there is a little garden where the grass grows long and soft. A nightingale sings there all night, and I long to rest in that quiet place. Have you read the old poem on the library window? It says that if a pure-hearted girl cries and prays for me, the dry almond tree will flower. Then the house will finally be at peace." Virginia turned pale, but she did not hesitate. "I'm not afraid," she said. "I'll help you." The wall slowly opened, and the two of them disappeared through it.

The family soon realized that Virginia was missing. Washington and Mr Otis searched every room, and the young Duke galloped through the fields. Just as the clock struck twelve, Virginia stepped out of a door at the top of the stairs. She was pale but calm, and she was holding a small box of jewels. "I have been with the ghost," she said quietly. "He has gone to his rest, and he gave me these before he left." She led them through a secret door into a tiny, dusty room behind the wall. Looking out of its window, one of the twins cried out in surprise. The old almond tree in the garden was covered in white flowers.

A few days later, the family gave Sir Simon a quiet resting place under an old tree. Virginia laid a cross of white flowers there. Mr Otis wanted to return the jewels to Lord Canterville, but the old lord refused. "Your daughter was kind to Sir Simon when nobody else was," he said, "so they are hers." Years later, Virginia married the Duke of Cheshire and wore the jewels at her wedding. The Duke once asked her what had happened on that night, but she would not tell him. "Sir Simon helped me understand life and death," she said gently, "and why love is stronger than both."

## Level C1

Everyone agreed that Mr Hiram B. Otis, the American diplomat, was making a mistake when he bought Canterville, an old house deep in the English countryside. Even Lord Canterville felt obliged to warn him, since several members of his family had met the ghost and their nerves had never quite recovered. "If there were such a thing as a ghost anywhere in Europe," Mr Otis replied cheerfully, "we Americans would have bought it long ago for one of our museums. I shall take the furniture and the ghost at a fair valuation." Lord Canterville smiled the patient smile of a man who has seen many confident visitors, and the papers were signed within the week.

The family arrived on a golden summer evening, and they made an impressive group. Mrs Otis was a handsome, sensible woman who believed firmly in fresh air and good medicine. Washington, their eldest son, was a fair-haired young man with excellent manners and a passion for anything new, efficient and American. Virginia, at fifteen, was slim and lively, with large blue eyes, and she rode her pony across the park with a courage that astonished the local gentlemen. Last came the twins, two cheerful little boys who regarded every rule in the house as a personal challenge.

Mrs Umney, the elderly housekeeper, served them tea in the library, and it was there that Mrs Otis noticed a dark red stain on the floor beside the fireplace. "Ah, madam," said Mrs Umney, lowering her voice, "that stain has been there for three hundred years. Sir Simon did a wicked deed in this very room long ago, and soon afterwards he disappeared, though his restless spirit has walked these corridors ever since. No one has ever been able to remove it." "Nonsense," said Washington, producing a small black cleaning stick from his pocket, and within a minute he had rubbed the floor perfectly clean. At that instant, a dreadful flash of lightning lit the room, the thunder made the windows rattle, and Mrs Umney sank into an armchair with a little cry. The following morning, to everyone's mild surprise, the stain had returned, and although Washington cleaned it again and again, it was always back by breakfast.

It was around one o'clock that night when Mr Otis was woken by a curious sound in the corridor, rather like metal being dragged slowly across stone. He put on his slippers, opened the door, and found himself facing an old man of truly alarming appearance, with tangled gray hair and clothes from another age. Heavy, rusty chains hung from his wrists and ankles. "My dear sir," said Mr Otis, with the courtesy of a born diplomat, "I really must insist that you oil those chains. I have brought you a small bottle for the purpose." Having placed the bottle on a table, he bowed and returned to bed.

For a moment the old ghost stood perfectly still, trembling with outrage. Then, dashing the bottle to the floor, he fled down the corridor, groaning in his most hollow and ghostly manner. At once a door flew open, and two small figures in white sent an enormous pillow whistling past his ear. There was plainly no time to lose, so he slipped through the wall and retreated to his secret chamber to consider his position. In three centuries of devoted haunting, he had reduced duchesses to tears, sent servants running from the house in their nightcaps and kept a bishop awake for an entire week. Never, in all that time, had anyone offered him oil.

His pride demanded satisfaction, and over the following weeks he tried every trick he knew. He let out his most terrible laugh at midnight, only for Mrs Otis to open her door and kindly recommend her medicine for his digestion. He put on an old suit of armor for a grand appearance, but it proved far too heavy, and it went crashing down the staircase while the twins applauded from the landing. Meanwhile, the stain in the library continued to reappear each morning, though its color had grown increasingly unreliable. One day it was a dull red, the next a rich purple, and once, to the family's great amusement, a brilliant emerald green. It was also noticed that the paints in Virginia's little paint box were disappearing at an astonishing rate.

The twins, for their part, never rested. They made a butter slide in the corridor that sent the ghost sliding helplessly into the darkness. They balanced a jug of water on a half-open door, and he arrived in his room soaked through, with a cold that lasted a fortnight. Their masterpiece, however, was another ghost. Turning a corner one night, Sir Simon came face to face with a pale, round-faced figure grinning at him from the shadows. He, who had frightened half of England, fled to his bed and pulled the covers over his head. Returning at dawn to propose an alliance with this fellow spirit, he found nothing but an old sheet, a kitchen brush and a hollow vegetable with a candle inside.

From then on, Sir Simon grew weak and nervous, and he rarely left his room. Not even the arrival of the young Duke of Cheshire, who was openly and hopelessly in love with Virginia, could tempt him into a single performance. It was Virginia, in the end, who found him. Coming into an empty room upstairs one autumn afternoon, she found him by the window, watching the yellow leaves drift across the lawn. He looked so lonely and so tired that her fear simply vanished. "I am very sorry for you," she said, "but you had no right to take my paints. Because of you, I couldn't paint a single sunset, and the green was the last straw." "What else was I to do?" asked the ghost with dignity. "A stain must be kept up, and after you had used all the red, I was obliged to make do with whatever remained."

Then his voice softened, and the old actor fell silent. "I have not slept for three hundred years," he said, "and I am more tired than you can possibly imagine. Far away, beyond the pine woods, there is a little garden where the grass grows long and deep and a nightingale sings all night to the moon. I long to lie there in the quiet and forget, but I cannot find the way alone. Have you never read the old verse painted on the library window? It promises that when a pure-hearted girl weeps and prays for me, the dry old almond tree will blossom, and peace will come to Canterville." Virginia turned pale, and for a long moment she said nothing. Then she stood up, looked at him steadily and held out her hand. "I am not afraid," she said, "and I will ask for mercy for you." With a faint sigh of gratitude, he led her towards the wall, which opened silently before them and closed again behind them.

Her absence was noticed at teatime, and by evening the whole household was searching for her. Washington and his father went through every room with lanterns, while the young Duke galloped across the countryside until his horse could hardly stand. Just as the clock struck midnight, a door opened at the top of the staircase, and Virginia stepped out, pale but perfectly calm, carrying a small box of glittering jewels. "I have been with the ghost," she said. "He has gone to his rest, and before he left, he gave me these to remember him by." She led them through a secret door into a tiny, dusty room behind the old wall. One of the twins, glancing out of its narrow window, gave a shout of wonder. In the moonlight, the old almond tree, bare and dry for as long as anyone could remember, was covered in white blossom.

A few days later, Sir Simon was given a quiet resting place beneath an ancient tree, and Virginia laid a cross of white flowers there with her own hands. Mr Otis, a scrupulously honest man, offered the jewels to Lord Canterville, but the old lord would hear nothing of it. "Your daughter showed kindness to Sir Simon when no one else in three hundred years had done so," he said, "and she has earned every one of them." When, some years later, Virginia married the Duke of Cheshire, she wore the jewels on her wedding day. The Duke asked her, not for the first time, what had happened behind the wall on that strange night. "I can never tell you that," Virginia replied gently, "but Sir Simon helped me understand what life is and what death means, and why love is stronger than both." The Duke took her hand and, wisely, asked no more questions.
