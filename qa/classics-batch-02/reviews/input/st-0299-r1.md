# Cyrano de Bergerac

Summary: A brave poet with a very long nose writes love letters for another man.

## Level A1

Cyrano lived in Paris long ago. He was a poet and a soldier. He was brave, funny and very clever. But he had a very long nose.

Valvert, a young lord, laughed at Cyrano. "Your nose is too big!" said Valvert. Cyrano smiled and took out his sword. He won the fight very fast. Then Valvert ran away.

Cyrano loved his cousin Roxane. She was beautiful and kind. But Cyrano did not tell her. "She can't love this face," he thought.

One morning, Roxane came to see him. "I love a young man," she said. "His name is Christian. He is in your group of soldiers. Please help him." Cyrano was sad, but he said yes.

Christian was very handsome. But he was not good with words. "I can't write to her," said Christian. "I can help you," said Cyrano. So Cyrano wrote beautiful letters. Christian put his name on them. Roxane loved the letters very much.

One night, Christian stood under Roxane's balcony. He could not find good words. So Cyrano stood in the dark and spoke. Roxane listened and smiled. That night, she married Christian.

Then the soldiers went to war. Every day, Cyrano wrote to Roxane. He put Christian's name on every letter. In the war, Christian fell and died. Roxane cried for a long time. Cyrano kept his secret.

Fifteen years went by. Roxane lived with the nuns. Their house had a quiet garden. Every Saturday, Cyrano came to see her. One evening, he came late. He was very ill.

"Can I read Christian's last letter?" asked Cyrano. He read it in the dark. Roxane listened, and then she knew. "You can't see the words!" said Roxane. "You know them by heart. You wrote all the letters!" "No, no," said Cyrano with a smile. "I love you," said Roxane softly. Cyrano died that evening, at peace.

## Level A2

Long ago, a poet and soldier lived in Paris. His name was Cyrano de Bergerac, and he came from Gascony. He was brave, funny and very clever. But he had a very long nose, and he knew it.

At the theater, a young lord, Valvert, laughed at Cyrano. "Your nose is very big," said Valvert. "Is that all you can say?" asked Cyrano with a smile. Then he made a funny poem and fought at the same time. At the end of the poem, Cyrano won the fight. Valvert left in a hurry, and everyone cheered.

Cyrano had a secret. He loved his cousin Roxane. She was beautiful and smart. But Cyrano never told her about his love. "No woman can love a face like mine," he thought.

One morning, Roxane asked Cyrano to meet her at Ragueneau's bakery. Cyrano was full of hope, but Roxane had different news. "I love a young man," she said. "His name is Christian. He is a new soldier in your group. Please keep him safe." Cyrano felt sad, but he gave his word.

Christian was very handsome, but he was shy with words. On his first day, he made jokes about Cyrano's nose. Everyone waited for a fight, but Cyrano did nothing. Later, he told Christian about Roxane. "She wants a letter from you," said Cyrano. "But I can't write nice things," said Christian. "Then I can write them for you," said Cyrano. So Cyrano wrote the letters, and Christian signed them. Roxane loved every word.

A powerful man, de Guiche, wanted Roxane too. One night, Christian stood under Roxane's balcony. But he could not find any good words. So Cyrano stood in the dark and spoke for him. Roxane listened, and she loved Christian more than ever. That same night, a priest married Roxane and Christian in secret.

De Guiche was very angry. He sent Cyrano and Christian to the war at Arras. There, the soldiers had very little food. But every day, Cyrano wrote a letter to Roxane. He signed it with Christian's name. One day, Roxane arrived with Ragueneau and a carriage full of food. "Your letters changed me," she told Christian. "Now I love your soul, not your face."

Christian was quiet, because now he understood. Roxane really loved the writer of the letters. "Tell her the truth," Christian said to Cyrano. But a few minutes later, the fighting started, and Christian fell. He died that day. Cyrano kept his secret.

Fifteen years went by. Roxane lived with the nuns in a quiet garden in Paris. Every Saturday, Cyrano visited her. One autumn evening, he came late for the first time. He was very ill.

"May I read Christian's last letter?" asked Cyrano. It grew dark, but he read on and on. Suddenly Roxane understood. "You can't see the words," she said. "You know them by heart! The voice under the balcony was yours!" "No, my dear, I never loved you," said Cyrano with a smile. "I love you," she said, and she held his hand. Cyrano died peacefully that evening, with his friends near him.

## Level B1

In old Paris, there was a soldier and poet called Cyrano de Bergerac. He served with a company of proud young soldiers from Gascony. Cyrano was brave, funny and wonderful with words, but he had an enormous nose. He often joked about it himself, but he did not let anyone else laugh at it.

One evening, the theater was full of people who were waiting for a play. A proud young lord, Valvert, walked up to Cyrano. "Your nose is very... large," said Valvert. Cyrano laughed at him. "Is that the best you can do?" he asked. "I can describe my nose in a hundred better ways!" Then he made up a poem on the spot. He fought a duel with Valvert while he was speaking. On the last line of the poem, Cyrano won, and Valvert hurried away.

Later that night, Cyrano told his good friend Le Bret his secret. "I love my cousin Roxane," he said. "She is the most beautiful woman in Paris. But look at me. How can she ever love a man with this nose?"

The next morning, Roxane asked to meet Cyrano at Ragueneau's bakery. Ragueneau was a kind baker who loved poets. But Roxane wanted to talk about someone else. "I am in love with Christian, the new soldier in your company," she said. "Will you be his friend and protect him?" Cyrano hid his pain and promised.

Christian was very handsome, but he was not clever with words. On his first day with the soldiers, he wanted to look brave. So he made jokes about Cyrano's nose again and again. The other soldiers were sure of a terrible fight. But Cyrano sent everyone away and spoke to Christian alone. "Roxane loves you," he said. "She will expect a beautiful letter from you." "I am a fool with words," said Christian. "I will lose her." "Then let us work together," said Cyrano. "You have the face, and I have the words."

So Cyrano wrote long, wonderful letters, and Christian signed them. Roxane read every letter again and again, and she fell more deeply in love. Meanwhile, the powerful Count de Guiche also wanted Roxane, but she did not like him. One evening, Christian decided to speak to Roxane without Cyrano's help. Under her balcony, he could only say, "I love you very much." Roxane was disappointed and went back inside. Quickly, Cyrano pulled Christian into the shadows under the balcony. Then Cyrano began to speak in a low voice, and Roxane came back out to listen. In the dark, she believed it was Christian. So Cyrano told her everything that was in his own heart.

Just then, a monk arrived with a letter from de Guiche. De Guiche was coming to see Roxane that night. Roxane thought fast and asked the monk to marry her and Christian at once. To give them time, Cyrano stopped de Guiche in the street. He pretended to be a strange traveler from the moon and told a long, crazy story. When de Guiche finally heard about the wedding, he was furious. "The soldiers will leave for the war tonight," he said. "And Christian will go with them."

At Arras, the army was hungry and tired. Every morning, Cyrano went past the enemy to send a letter to Roxane. He signed every letter with Christian's name. Then one day, a carriage arrived, and Roxane jumped out with Ragueneau and plenty of food. "Your letters brought me here," she told Christian. "At first I loved your handsome face. Now your face doesn't matter to me, because I love your soul."

Christian understood everything at once. Roxane loved the man who wrote the letters. "Tell her the truth," he said to Cyrano. "Let her choose." But before Cyrano could speak, the fighting started, and Christian fell in battle. He died that day with Roxane beside him and his last letter close to his heart. Cyrano decided to keep his secret forever.

Fifteen years passed. Roxane lived quietly with the nuns at a convent in Paris. She still wore black for Christian. Every Saturday, Cyrano visited her in the convent garden with the news. One autumn evening, he arrived late for the first time. He was very ill, but he did not tell her. As the light was fading, he asked to read Christian's last letter. He read it aloud in a soft voice, even after the garden became dark.

Suddenly Roxane understood. "You can't see the letter," she said. "You know it by heart. It was you, all those years ago, under the balcony!" "No," said Cyrano gently. "I never loved you." But she knew the truth, and she held his hand. "I love you," she whispered. "Please stay with me." Le Bret and Ragueneau arrived, and Cyrano smiled at his old friends. He died peacefully that evening, proud and free to the end.

## Level B2

In seventeenth-century Paris, there lived a soldier and poet named Cyrano de Bergerac. He served with the cadets of Gascony, a company of proud young men from the south of France. Cyrano was famous for three things: his courage, his clever tongue and his enormous nose. He was happy to make jokes about his nose himself, but anyone else who mocked it soon regretted it.

One evening, a crowded theater was waiting for a play to begin when Cyrano stood up and stopped the show. He did not like the main actor, and he ordered him off the stage. A proud young lord, Valvert, decided to teach this noisy soldier a lesson. "Sir, your nose is... very large," he said with a cold smile. Cyrano looked at him with pity. "Is that all?" he asked. "A man with a little wit could say so much more!" Then he listed twenty funnier ways to insult his own nose, and the audience laughed with him, not at him. After that, he challenged Valvert to a duel and made up a poem while they fought. He promised to win on the last line, and he kept his promise. Valvert left the theater in a hurry, and the crowd cheered for Cyrano.

Afterwards, his loyal friend Le Bret asked him why he always made enemies. Cyrano finally told him the truth. "I love Roxane, my cousin," he said. "She is clever and beautiful, and she adores poetry. But how could anyone love a face like mine?" Le Bret said that Roxane had looked at him with admiration during the duel. For one night, Cyrano allowed himself to hope.

The next morning, Roxane asked to meet him at the bakery of Ragueneau, a baker who loved poets. Cyrano waited there with a love letter in his pocket, but he never gave it to her. Roxane told him, blushing, that she had fallen in love with a young man she had only seen from a distance. His name was Christian, and he had just joined Cyrano's company. "The other cadets may be unkind to him," she said. "Will you be his friend and protect him for me?" Cyrano hid his broken heart and gave his word.

That afternoon, Christian wanted to prove his courage, so he made one joke after another about Cyrano's nose. The cadets were certain that a duel was coming. But Cyrano, remembering his promise, did not draw his sword. Instead, he sent everyone away and told Christian that Roxane loved him and was waiting for a letter. Christian's face fell. "I am handsome, but I am a fool with words," he admitted. "If I write to her, she will stop loving me." Cyrano thought for a moment and then made an unusual offer. "Then let us become one perfect man," he said. "You will lend me your face, and I will lend you my words."

From then on, Cyrano wrote the letters and Christian signed them. Every line was full of Cyrano's real feelings, and Roxane fell more deeply in love with each one. Meanwhile, the powerful Count de Guiche also wanted Roxane, and he was not a man who accepted a refusal.

One evening, Christian insisted on speaking to Roxane without help. Standing under her balcony, he could only repeat, "I love you," again and again. Roxane was so disappointed that she went back inside. Cyrano quickly hid in the shadows under the balcony and called her back out. Speaking softly so that she would think it was Christian, he said everything he had never dared to say. Roxane was deeply moved, and Cyrano felt for one night that she was truly listening to him.

Just then, a monk arrived with a message from de Guiche, who was planning to visit Roxane that very night. Roxane quickly asked the monk to marry her and Christian before de Guiche could arrive. While the wedding took place, Cyrano stopped de Guiche in the dark street. He pretended to be a confused traveler who had just fallen from the moon. His wild story kept the count listening for a quarter of an hour. When de Guiche learned the truth, it was too late. In revenge, he ordered the cadets to leave for the war at Arras that same night.

The siege of Arras was long and hard, and the cadets were starving. Yet every morning, Cyrano slipped past the enemy lines to send a letter to Roxane, always signed with Christian's name. One day, to everyone's surprise, a carriage rolled into the camp. Roxane had crossed the enemy lines with Ragueneau, and the carriage was full of food for the soldiers. She told Christian that his letters had changed her. "At first I loved you for your face," she said. "Now I love you for your soul, and I would love you even if you were ugly."

These words hurt Christian more than any sword. He realized that Roxane did not really love him but the man who wrote the letters. He begged Cyrano to tell her everything and let her choose between them. But before Cyrano could speak, the battle began, and Christian fell. He died that day, and Roxane found his last letter close to his heart. Cyrano knew that the truth would only add to her grief, so he decided to keep his secret forever.

Fifteen years passed. Roxane now lived among the nuns of a convent in Paris and still dressed in black for Christian. Every Saturday, Cyrano came to sit with her in the convent garden and tell her the news. He was poor and had many enemies, but he had never missed a single visit. One autumn evening, he arrived late for the first time. He was gravely ill, and he knew that he did not have long, but he hid it from her.

As the sun went down, he asked if he might read Christian's last letter. Roxane gave it to him, and he began to read aloud. He continued long after the garden had grown too dark to see the page. Suddenly Roxane understood. "You know it by heart," she said. "It was your voice under the balcony, and your words in every letter." "No," Cyrano replied gently. "I never loved you." But it was useless to deny it now. "I love you," Roxane whispered, holding his hand. "Please stay with me." Le Bret and Ragueneau arrived to find their old friend smiling. Cyrano died peacefully that evening, as proud and free as he had always lived.

## Level C1

In the Paris of the sixteen-forties, few men were as admired, or as feared, as Cyrano de Bergerac, poet, swordsman and officer in the cadets of Gascony. He could compose a sonnet faster than most men could tie their boots, and he spoke his mind to dukes and beggars alike. Nature, however, had played one cruel trick on him: his nose, which entered every room a moment before he did. Cyrano mocked it freely himself, but he made sure that nobody else ever did so twice.

The story begins in a crowded theater, where the audience sat waiting for a play that Cyrano had decided would not take place. Having forbidden the leading actor to appear on stage, he drove the man away with a few sharp words and a great deal of noise. A fashionable young lord named Valvert, irritated by such boldness, strolled over to put the upstart in his place. "Your nose is... rather large," he remarked, as though nobody had noticed it before. Cyrano regarded him almost with sympathy. "Is that really all?" he asked. "With a little wit and a few letters, you might have said so much more!" Then, to the delight of the audience, he performed the insult Valvert had failed to deliver, offering it in a dozen styles, from the tender to the absurd. Not content with this, he challenged the young lord to a duel and announced that he would compose a poem while fighting and win on the final line. Valvert soon discovered that Cyrano was a man of his word, and he left the theater with his pride rather more damaged than his person.

Late that night, his faithful friend Le Bret asked him why he insisted on making enemies everywhere. Cyrano, who rarely spoke of himself, confessed what lay beneath all the noise. "I love Roxane," he said quietly. "My cousin, the brightest, loveliest woman in Paris. And what can such a woman feel for a face like this?" Le Bret pointed out that Roxane had followed the duel with admiration in her eyes, and for a few hours Cyrano dared to hope.

The next morning, she sent word that she wished to see him at the bakery of Ragueneau. This pastry cook was so devoted to poets that he fed them all on credit and recited their verses over his ovens. Cyrano arrived early with a letter of confession in his pocket, which he never found the courage to give her. For Roxane had come to speak of someone else. Blushing, she explained that she had fallen in love with a young man whom she had only admired from a distance, a new cadet named Christian. "I am told the cadets are cruel to newcomers," she said. "Will you look after him for my sake?" Cyrano swallowed his disappointment with remarkable grace and promised that he would.

Christian, meanwhile, had decided that the best way to earn respect among the cadets was to provoke their most dangerous member. Again and again he interrupted Cyrano's stories with remarks about noses, while the other soldiers waited in breathless silence for the explosion. It never came. Bound by his promise, Cyrano simply cleared the room and told the astonished young man that Roxane loved him and was expecting a letter. Christian's courage drained away at once. "I have a handsome face and an empty head," he admitted miserably. "The moment I write to her, she will see through me." It was then that Cyrano made his strange proposal. "Together, we could make one perfect lover," he said. "You lend me your face, and I will lend you my words." Christian accepted, and from that day the letters that reached Roxane were written by one man and signed by another.

Every sentence carried Cyrano's own longing, and Roxane, who had always valued wit above beauty, fell ever more deeply in love. Her happiness, however, did not go unobserved. The Count de Guiche, a powerful and proud nobleman, had his own designs on Roxane and was not accustomed to being refused.

One evening, tired of reciting another man's words, Christian insisted on courting Roxane alone. Beneath her balcony, he managed nothing better than "I love you," repeated with growing desperation, until she went back inside in disgust. Cyrano, hidden among the shadows below, pulled him out of sight and called her back. Speaking low so that his voice would pass for Christian's, he poured out everything he had kept silent for years. Roxane listened, enchanted, and for one night Cyrano knew what it was to be heard by the woman he loved, even if she did not know it was he who spoke.

Their happiness was interrupted by a monk bearing a letter from de Guiche, who intended to call on Roxane that very evening. With admirable speed, Roxane persuaded the monk that the letter instructed him to marry her to Christian without delay. Somebody had to hold back the count, and Cyrano gladly took the task. Leaping into the dark street, he played a bewildered traveler fresh from the moon, describing his journey in such detail that de Guiche listened for a quarter of an hour. By the time the count realized he had been tricked, the wedding was over. His revenge was swift: the cadets of Gascony, Christian among them, were to leave for the siege of Arras that same night.

At Arras, the army slowly grew thin and silent with hunger. Yet every single morning, Cyrano crept past the enemy lines to send a letter to Roxane, each one signed with her husband's name. Then, one astonishing day, a carriage rattled into the camp, and out stepped Roxane herself. Ragueneau was acting as her coachman, and enough food lay hidden under the seats to feed the whole company. She had crossed the enemy lines with nothing but a smile and a determined heart. Taking Christian aside, she told him that his letters had transformed her love. "At first, I admit, I loved your face," she said. "Now it is your soul I love, and I would love you just as much if you were ugly."

To Christian, this tender declaration was a sentence of exile. He saw at last that Roxane loved not him but the voice behind the letters, and he could not bear to be loved in another man's place. He begged Cyrano to tell her the truth and let her choose freely between them. But before Cyrano could say a word, the enemy attacked, and Christian was among the first to fall. He died that day, and Roxane found on him the last letter he had carried close to his heart. Seeing her grief, Cyrano understood that the truth could now bring her nothing but further pain, and he resolved to keep his secret to the end.

Fifteen years later, Roxane was living among the nuns of a quiet convent in Paris, still dressed in mourning for Christian. Every Saturday, without fail, Cyrano came to sit with her beneath the trees of the convent garden and entertain her with the gossip of the week, which he called his gazette. He had grown poor, and his sharp pen had made him many powerful enemies, yet in fifteen years he had never once been late. One autumn evening, however, the clock struck, and he did not appear. When at last he came, slowly and leaning on his cane, he was gravely ill, though he made light of it and joked with her as usual.

As the leaves fell and the light began to fade, he asked whether he might finally read Christian's last letter. Roxane handed it to him, and he read it aloud, its words of farewell sounding strangely alive in his voice. He went on reading long after the darkness had made the page impossible to see. Slowly, Roxane understood. "You are not reading it," she said. "You know it by heart, because you wrote it, and the voice beneath my balcony that night was yours." "No, no, my dear," Cyrano replied, with a gentle smile. "I never loved you." But his denial only confirmed what she now knew. "I love you," she whispered, holding his hand. "Stay with me." Le Bret and Ragueneau arrived to find him sitting peacefully beneath the trees. Cyrano rose, refused all help and declared that there was one thing he would carry away untouched: his panache, the spirit he had never surrendered to anyone. He died that evening in the quiet garden, with Roxane beside him at last.
