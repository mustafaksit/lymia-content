# Five Weeks in a Balloon

Summary: A daring doctor and two friends try to cross the whole of Africa in a hydrogen balloon.

## Level A1

Samuel Fergusson lived in London. He was a doctor and an explorer. He had a big dream. "I want to fly over Africa," said Fergusson. "Nobody can do that!" people said.

Fergusson made a huge balloon. He gave it a name, the Victoria. It was full of hydrogen. He also made a special heater. The heater made the gas warm. Then the balloon went up. Without heat, it went down.

Kennedy was his old friend. "This is a crazy plan," said Kennedy. But he came too. Joe came with them. Joe was brave and always happy.

The three men sailed to Zanzibar. There, the Victoria rose into the sky. The wind took them west. They saw rivers, forests and big animals. Kennedy smiled at the view.

Then a storm came. The wind was strong and loud. The balloon shook. Fergusson turned on the heater. The Victoria went up above the clouds. Up there, the sky was calm.

Later, they flew over a hot desert. There was no wind. The balloon did not move. Their water was almost gone. The men were very thirsty. At last, a soft wind came. It carried them to trees and water.

Near Lake Chad, big eagles came. They attacked the balloon. One part of the balloon tore. The Victoria began to fall. "Throw things out!" cried Fergusson. The men threw out boxes and bags. The balloon went up again.

But now the balloon was weak. Every day, it flew lower. The men threw out more and more. At last, they saw the Senegal River. "We must get over it!" said Joe. They threw out almost everything. Slowly, the Victoria crossed the river. Then it came down on the other side.

The three friends were safe. They went home to London. Everybody cheered for them. "We did it!" said Fergusson. Kennedy and Joe smiled too.

## Level A2

Doctor Samuel Fergusson was a famous explorer from London. He traveled to many faraway places. But he had one big dream. He wanted to cross Africa from east to west by balloon. Many people laughed at the idea. "It is too dangerous," they said.

Fergusson did not listen to them. He built a large balloon and named it the Victoria. He filled it with hydrogen, a very light gas. He also invented a special heater. When the heater warmed the gas, the balloon rose. When the gas cooled, the balloon went down. So he could find good winds at different heights.

His old friend Kennedy did not like the plan. "You can't control a balloon, Samuel," said Kennedy. "Come and see," said Fergusson with a smile. In the end, Kennedy agreed to come. Joe, Fergusson's loyal helper, came too. Joe was strong, cheerful and never afraid.

The three men sailed to the island of Zanzibar. There, they filled the Victoria with gas. On a bright morning, the balloon rose into the air. Below them, people waved and watched. The east wind carried them over the sea and into Africa.

For days, they flew over forests, hills and wide rivers. They saw herds of animals and quiet villages. At night, they stopped and held the balloon with an anchor. Kennedy soon forgot his fears. "This is wonderful!" he cried.

One evening, dark clouds covered the sky. A terrible storm began. Lightning flashed all around the balloon. "The hydrogen can catch fire!" shouted Joe. Quickly, Fergusson turned up the heater. The Victoria rose higher and higher. Soon they were above the clouds, in the calm moonlight.

Later, the wind took them over a great desert. Then the wind stopped. For days, the balloon hung over the hot sand. Their water was nearly gone. Nobody spoke much in the terrible heat. At last, a wind came and carried them to some palm trees. There they found a well with fresh water.

Near Lake Chad, a group of big eagles flew at the balloon. Their sharp claws tore the cover. Gas rushed out, and the Victoria fell toward the lake. "Throw out the heavy things!" cried Fergusson. Joe looked at the water below. Then he jumped into the lake. Without him, the balloon was lighter and rose again.

Fergusson and Kennedy searched for their friend. Two days later, they found Joe on the shore. He was tired and dirty, but he had a big smile. "I can swim, sir!" laughed Joe. They let down a rope ladder, and he climbed into the basket.

Now the Victoria was weak, and it lost gas every day. The men threw out boxes, tools and even some food. Finally, they saw the Senegal River ahead. The balloon was very low. They threw out almost everything. Slowly, it crossed the water and came down on the far side. Soldiers from a nearby fort ran to help them. Months later, the three friends returned to London as heroes.

## Level B1

Doctor Samuel Fergusson was a famous explorer who lived in London. He traveled across deserts and mountains in many parts of the world. Now he had a new and daring plan. He wanted to cross Africa from east to west in a balloon. "From the sky, I will see places that no map shows," he told his friends. Many people thought he was mad.

Fergusson worked for months on his balloon, which he named the Victoria. It was really two balloons, one inside the other, and both held hydrogen. Under them hung a strong basket for the travelers. His cleverest idea was a special heater for the gas. When the heater warmed the hydrogen, the balloon rose. When he turned it down, the gas cooled and the balloon went lower. In this way, he could look for good winds without losing any gas.

His oldest friend, Kennedy, was a practical man who loved the outdoors. He did not like the plan at all. "You will fall into a jungle, and nobody will ever find you," Kennedy warned. "Then come with me and make sure that it doesn't happen," Fergusson answered. Kennedy argued for weeks, but in the end he packed his bag. The third traveler was Joe, Fergusson's loyal helper. Joe was cheerful, strong and ready for anything.

The three men sailed to Zanzibar, an island near the east coast of Africa. There, they filled the Victoria with hydrogen. On a bright morning, the balloon rose slowly while crowds watched from below. A steady wind was blowing from the east, and it carried them toward the land. Kennedy held the side of the basket tightly. Joe, on the other hand, was laughing and waving his hat.

For many days, they flew over forests, hills and shining lakes. They saw herds of elephants and giraffes that were moving across the grass. At night, they usually dropped an anchor and stayed above the trees. Kennedy began to enjoy the journey. "I was wrong, Samuel," Kennedy admitted, "and this is the best way to travel."

One evening, heavy black clouds were gathering over the mountains. Soon a storm broke around them. Lightning was flashing on every side, and the basket swung wildly. "One spark can set the hydrogen on fire!" shouted Kennedy. Fergusson stayed calm and turned the heater up. The Victoria climbed higher and higher through the rain. At last, it rose above the clouds into a quiet, starry sky. Below them, the storm was still roaring.

Weeks later, the wind carried them over a great desert. Then, suddenly, the wind stopped. For days, the balloon hung over the burning sand and did not move. Their water was running out, and the sun was hot on their faces. Nobody talked much, because even talking was hard work. Joe tried to cheer up the others, but he was weak too. At last, a strong wind came back. It carried them to an oasis, where they found palm trees and a cool well.

Near Lake Chad, they met a new danger. Several huge eagles were circling around the balloon. Suddenly, the birds flew at it and tore the outer cover with their claws. Gas rushed out, and the Victoria began to fall toward the lake. "Throw out the heavy things!" shouted Fergusson. They threw out boxes and bags, but the balloon was still falling. Then Joe looked at his friends. "I can swim," said Joe, and he jumped into the water. Without his weight, the Victoria rose into the sky again.

Fergusson and Kennedy were very worried about their brave friend. They searched the shores of the lake for two days. Finally, they saw a small figure who was waving from the bank. It was Joe, tired and hungry but smiling as always. Fergusson let down a rope ladder, and Joe climbed quickly into the basket. "Did you miss me?" Joe asked with a grin.

The Victoria was weak now, and it lost a little gas every day. To keep it in the air, the men threw out more and more things. They dropped tools, boxes and even some of their food. At last, they saw the wide Senegal River in front of them. The balloon was flying so low that it almost touched the trees. "We must get to the other side!" cried Kennedy. They threw out almost everything they still had. Slowly, the Victoria lifted over the water and came down on the far bank.

Soldiers from a fort near the river ran to meet them. The three friends were safe, and their great journey was over. In five weeks, they crossed the whole of Africa in a balloon. Later, they returned to London, where crowds cheered for them.

## Level B2

One winter evening, Doctor Samuel Fergusson stood before a crowded hall in London and announced an astonishing plan. He was already a famous explorer, yet this time he meant to do something completely new. He intended to cross the whole of Africa, from east to west, in a balloon. "From the air," he declared, "we can see in a few weeks what travelers on foot could not see in many years." Some of his listeners cheered, while others shook their heads and whispered that he had lost his mind.

The balloon, which Fergusson named the Victoria, was really two balloons, one inside the other, both filled with hydrogen. If the outer one was damaged, the inner one would still hold the gas. However, his cleverest invention was a heater that could warm the hydrogen whenever he wished. Warm gas expanded and lifted the balloon, and cool gas let it sink again. In this way, he could climb or descend to find the right wind without throwing out sand or letting gas escape. Ordinary balloonists lost gas or weight every time they changed height, so their flights never lasted long.

Fergusson's oldest friend, Kennedy, was horrified by the idea. He was a practical, steady man who preferred solid ground under his boots. "You will be carried off by the first strong wind," he protested, "and nobody will ever hear of you again." "Then you had better come along and keep an eye on me," Fergusson replied calmly. Kennedy argued for weeks, yet when the ship sailed, he was standing on its deck. With them came Joe, Fergusson's faithful helper, a cheerful young man who trusted the doctor completely.

The travelers sailed to Zanzibar, an island off the eastern coast of Africa, where the balloon was filled with gas. On a fine April morning, the ropes were released, and the Victoria rose gently into the air. Crowds on the shore watched in amazement as it drifted west over the sea. Kennedy gripped the edge of the basket, but Joe leaned out happily and waved to everyone below. Soon the coast disappeared behind them, and the green land of Africa spread out in every direction.

For days, the Victoria sailed over forests, hills and shining lakes. They passed a mountain whose peak was white with snow, even though the sun was burning down on the plains around it. Herds of elephants and giraffes moved across the grass, and the travelers looked down at quiet villages and busy markets. At night, they usually dropped an anchor into a tree and rested above the ground. Fergusson filled his notebook with maps and notes. He was overjoyed to find the lake where a great river began its long journey north. Even Kennedy had to admit that he had never seen anything so beautiful.

One evening, heavy clouds gathered over the mountains, and the air grew strangely still. Then the storm struck. Lightning flashed on every side, and the basket swung so wildly that Joe had to hold on with both hands. "A single spark could set the whole balloon on fire!" Kennedy shouted above the thunder. Fergusson said nothing, but he turned the heater up as high as it would go. Slowly the Victoria climbed through the rain, higher and higher, until it broke out above the clouds. There, in a calm and silent sky full of stars, they watched the storm rage harmlessly beneath them.

A few weeks later, the wind carried them over a great desert. Then, without warning, it stopped completely. For days the balloon hung motionless above the burning sand, and no amount of rising or falling could find a breeze. Their water was running out, and the heater itself needed water to work, so Fergusson could no longer use it freely. The three men sat in silence, their mouths dry and their eyes fixed on the empty horizon. At last a strong wind rose and carried them toward an oasis. There they found shade, palm trees and a deep, cool well.

Their troubles were not over. Near Lake Chad, a group of huge eagles began to circle the balloon. Suddenly the birds attacked, tearing the outer cover with their claws and beaks. Gas rushed out with a loud hiss, and the Victoria began to drop toward the lake. "Throw out everything heavy!" Fergusson ordered. Boxes, bags and tools went over the side, but the balloon kept falling. Joe looked at his two friends for a moment. "I'm a good swimmer, sir," said Joe quietly, and before anyone could stop him, he jumped. Freed of his weight, the Victoria leaped up into the sky.

Fergusson and Kennedy were heartbroken, but they refused to give up hope. They flew back and forth along the shores of the lake for two days, searching for any sign of their friend. At last, they spotted a small figure on the shore, waving both arms above his head. It was Joe, muddy and exhausted but grinning as widely as ever. Fergusson let down the rope ladder, and Joe scrambled up it into the basket. "I knew you would come back for me," said Joe, and Kennedy laughed with relief.

With only the inner balloon left, the Victoria was losing gas every day. To stay in the air, they threw out whatever they did not need: extra ropes, tools, and even part of their food. At last, they saw the wide Senegal River shining ahead of them, with a roaring waterfall nearby. By then, the balloon was barely holding itself above the trees. Fergusson had one last idea. He let out the last of the weak gas. Then they lit a fire of dry grass and filled the balloon with hot air instead. Slowly, the Victoria rose once more and floated across the river. As soon as the three men jumped out on the far bank, the empty balloon drifted away down the river.

A group of soldiers from a nearby fort had watched the strange arrival, and they hurried over to welcome the travelers. In just five weeks, Fergusson, Kennedy and Joe had crossed the whole of Africa through the air. When they finally returned to London, crowds filled the streets to cheer them. Kennedy, who had once called the plan madness, now told everyone that it had been the greatest adventure of his life.

## Level C1

On a cold winter evening in London, a crowded hall fell silent as Doctor Samuel Fergusson, an explorer already famous for his travels, rose to describe his newest plan. He intended, he told his audience, to cross the whole of Africa from east to west, not on foot or by river, but through the air in a balloon. Whatever travelers on the ground might see in years of hard marching, he argued, a balloon could reveal in weeks. The applause was loud, though more than one listener left muttering that the doctor had finally lost his senses.

Fergusson, however, was no careless dreamer, and he had spent years solving the problem that had defeated every balloonist before him. An ordinary balloon could rise only by dropping weight and descend only by releasing gas, so that each change of height shortened its life in the air. His answer was a heater of his own design, which drew its power from water split into its separate gases and used the flame to warm the hydrogen inside the balloon. Warmed, the gas swelled and carried the balloon upward; cooled, it shrank and let the balloon sink, allowing him to hunt for favorable winds without losing a breath of gas. As a further safeguard, the Victoria, as he named it, consisted of two balloons, one inside the other, so that a tear in the outer skin need not bring them down.

Nobody objected more fiercely than his oldest friend, Kennedy, a sensible and steady man who trusted his own two feet far more than any bag of gas. "You'll drift off over some jungle and never be heard of again," Kennedy warned, pacing the doctor's study. Fergusson listened patiently, then pointed out that if Kennedy was so worried, the simplest solution was to come along and watch over him in person. After weeks of grumbling, Kennedy found himself on board the ship, still unable to explain how he had been persuaded. The party was completed by Joe, the doctor's devoted helper, whose good humor never seemed to fail and whose faith in his employer had no limits.

They reached Zanzibar, an island off the eastern coast of Africa, and there the Victoria was slowly filled with hydrogen. On a bright morning in April the last ropes were cast off, and the balloon lifted from the ground so smoothly that Kennedy hardly noticed they had left it. Crowds gathered along the shore to watch the great silk globe drift westward over the water, growing smaller until it vanished into the haze. While Kennedy kept a firm hold on the edge of the basket, Joe leaned out as far as he dared, waving his hat at the shrinking island below.

Over the following days a whole continent unrolled beneath them like an enormous painted map. They floated above forests so thick that the treetops formed a single green carpet, and above rivers winding for miles across the plains. In the villages below, people stopped their work to gaze up at the strange shape passing silently overhead. Herds of elephants and giraffes moved across the grass, quite unaware of the travelers watching them from above. At one point they drifted past a mountain whose peak was crowned with snow, although the plains around it shimmered in the heat. Most nights they let an anchor down into the branches of a tree and slept suspended between earth and sky.

For Fergusson, the high point of these weeks came at a vast lake in the heart of the continent. Studying its shores from the air, he became convinced that he had found the place where a great northern river began its long journey to the sea. He filled page after page of his notebook, while Kennedy, who had sworn he would hate every minute of the voyage, admitted that he had never seen anything to equal it.

The calm did not last. One evening, a wall of black cloud rose above the mountains, and the air took on that heavy stillness which every traveler learns to dread. When the storm broke, lightning split the sky on every side, and the basket swung so violently that Joe clung to the ropes with both hands. "One spark, and this whole balloon goes up in flames!" Kennedy shouted, though the wind snatched the words from his mouth. Fergusson, without a word, turned the heater to its highest point. The Victoria climbed heavily through the rain until it burst out above the clouds into a sky so still and starry that the storm seemed to belong to another world.

The desert presented a danger of a quieter kind. Somewhere above its endless sand the wind simply stopped, and for days the Victoria hung over the same dunes, as motionless as if it had been nailed to the sky. Rising and sinking in search of a breeze proved useless, and since the heater itself depended on water, every hour of waiting made their situation worse. The three men shared out their last drops with great care and spoke as little as possible, while the sun pressed down on them like a heavy hand. Even Joe, whose jokes had carried them through every difficulty, grew silent. When at last a wind arrived, hot and fierce, it bore them to an oasis, where palm trees shaded a well of cool water. No one who has never known real thirst could imagine how sweet that first drink tasted.

Near Lake Chad, the danger came from the air itself. A flock of enormous eagles began circling the balloon in tightening rings, and before Fergusson could take the Victoria higher, they struck. Their claws and beaks ripped the outer skin, and with a long, rising hiss the gas began to pour out. The balloon lurched and dropped toward the lake, and although boxes, bags and instruments were flung overboard in frantic haste, it went on sinking. Joe glanced from the water rushing up to meet them to the faces of his two companions. "I swim like a fish, sir," said Joe, and before either of them could stop him, he had swung himself over the side and dropped into the lake. Lightened of his weight, the Victoria leaped upward, leaving nothing behind but the rings spreading across the water.

Fergusson and Kennedy spent the next two days in misery, steering the damaged balloon along the shore in search of their friend. Neither of them spoke of what each secretly feared. Then, late on the second day, Kennedy caught sight of a figure on the bank, leaping and waving both arms above his head with tremendous energy. It was Joe, covered in mud, worn out and hungry, but grinning exactly as he always did. Fergusson let the rope ladder fall, and Joe climbed it with such speed that he was in the basket before the balloon had properly stopped. "I was beginning to wonder whether you'd forgotten me," Joe remarked, and Kennedy, to his own surprise, found that he could not stop laughing.

With its outer skin gone, the Victoria could no longer hold its gas for long, and each day it flew a little lower than the day before. To keep it aloft, they gave up their possessions one by one: spare ropes, tools, the heavier instruments, and in the end a good part of their food. By the time the broad Senegal River appeared ahead of them, shining in the sun beside a thundering waterfall, the balloon was barely clearing the treetops. It could not possibly carry them across.

It was then that Fergusson had his final and boldest idea. He released the last of the tired hydrogen, and while Joe and Kennedy gathered armfuls of dry grass, he prepared to fill the empty balloon with hot air instead. The fire roared, the silk swelled and stretched, and to the travelers' immense relief the Victoria rose from the ground for the last time. It drifted out over the river, sinking steadily as the air inside it cooled, and touched down on the far bank with very little room to spare. Moments after the three men had scrambled out, the empty balloon lifted away on the wind and fell into the current, which carried it off toward the falls. Fergusson watched it go in silence, for it had served them faithfully to the very end.

Soldiers from a fort further down the river, who had followed the astonishing sight from their walls, hurried to meet the travelers and welcomed them with every kindness. In five weeks, the three friends had crossed the whole breadth of Africa through the air, something no one had ever done before. Their return to London was a triumph, with crowds filling the streets and scholars eager to study the doctor's notes and maps. As for Kennedy, who had once called the plan madness, he now told the story to anyone willing to listen, always insisting that he had been there from the very first day.
