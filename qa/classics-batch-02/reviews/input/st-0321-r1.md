# The Princess and the Goblin

Summary: A little princess and a brave miner boy discover a secret plan under the mountain.

## Level A1

A little princess lived on a mountain. Her name was Irene. Her nurse, Lootie, lived with her. Her father was the king. Irene called him King-Papa.

Goblins lived under the mountain. They were ugly, and they hated the sun. They had hard heads but soft feet. They hated songs, too.

On a rainy day, Irene found old stairs. She went up and up. At the top, she found a room. A beautiful old lady sat there. Her hair was long and white. "I am your great-great-grandmother," she said.

One evening, Irene and Lootie stayed out late. Suddenly, they saw goblins on the path! Then a boy came and sang a song. The goblins ran away. The boy was Curdie, a young miner.

Curdie and his father, Peter, were miners. One night, he heard goblins in the rocks. Their prince's name was Harelip. "We want the princess for him!" they said. The goblins wanted to flood the mine, too. But the goblins caught Curdie. They shut him in a cave.

The old lady gave Irene a ring. A thin thread went from the ring. Only Irene could see it. "Follow the thread," said the lady. That night, Irene followed it into the mountain. It stopped at some stones. Curdie was behind them! Irene pulled the stones away, one by one. Then the thread led them out.

Later, the goblins came again. They came up into the house. But Curdie was there. He jumped on their soft feet. "Ow! Ow!" cried the goblins. They hopped and ran back down. King-Papa came with his soldiers, too.

Then the goblins let water into the mine. But the miners had a strong wall. So the water went into the goblins' home. It carried them deep into the mountain. The goblins never came back.

King-Papa thanked Curdie. "Come with me, Curdie," said the king. But Curdie stayed with his mother and father. Irene went away with King-Papa.

## Level A2

Long ago, a little princess lived in a big mountain house. Her name was Irene, and she was eight years old. Her nurse, Lootie, looked after her. Her father was the king, and he lived far away. Sometimes he rode up to see her. Irene called him King-Papa.

Deep under the mountain lived the goblins. Long ago, they went down into the caves. Now they hated the sun and the people above. They had very hard heads. But their feet were soft, and they had no toes. They also hated songs.

On a rainy morning, Irene found some old stairs. She climbed to the very top. In a small room, an old lady sat at a spinning wheel. Her hair was white, but her face was lovely. "Come in, Irene," she said. "I am your great-great-grandmother." Irene told Lootie about the lady, but Lootie did not believe her.

One evening, Irene and Lootie walked too far. Soon the sun went down. Strange shapes moved on the dark path. Goblins! Then a boy came along and sang a loud song. The goblins hurried away. The boy was Curdie, the son of a miner. He walked them safely back to the house.

Curdie worked in the mine with his father, Peter. One night, he heard goblin voices behind the rock. He followed them to a great hall under the mountain. The goblin king spoke to his people. "Prince Harelip needs a wife from the sun people," he said. "So we must take the princess from her house!" The goblins had another plan, too. They wanted to fill the mine with water. But then the goblins found Curdie. They shut him in a dark cave.

The old lady gave Irene a ring. A thin thread went from the ring to a ball. Only Irene could see it. "When you are afraid, follow the thread," said the lady. That night, Irene followed the thread into the mountain. At last, it stopped at a pile of stones. She heard Curdie's voice behind them! Irene pulled away the stones, and Curdie came out. Then the thread led them both home. Curdie could not see the thread, so he did not believe her.

Curdie told the men at the house about the goblins. Nobody listened to a miner's boy. A few nights later, the goblins came up through the floor. Curdie was ready. He jumped hard on their soft feet. The goblins cried out and hopped about. Then King-Papa arrived with his soldiers. They chased the goblins away. Irene was not in the house. Her thread took her up the mountain to Curdie's home. She was safe there with his mother.

Then the goblins let the underground water out. But the miners had a strong wall in the mine. So the water rushed back into the goblins' caves. It carried them deep into the mountain, and they never came back.

King-Papa thanked Curdie and asked him to come to the palace. But Curdie wanted to stay with his mother and father. So the king rode away with Irene.

## Level B1

Long ago, a little princess called Irene lived in a large old house on a mountain. She was eight years old, and her nurse, Lootie, looked after her. Her father was the king, and he travelled all over his country. Sometimes he rode up to the house, and Irene ran out to meet him. She always called him King-Papa.

Under the mountain, in caves and tunnels, lived a people called the goblins. Many years ago they lived in the sunshine. Then they went underground and stayed there. Now they were ugly and strange, and they hated the people who lived above. Their heads were as hard as stone. But their feet were soft and had no toes. They also could not stand songs, especially new ones.

One rainy day, Irene was wandering through the house and found a strange staircase. She climbed it until she came to a small door at the very top. Inside, an old lady was sitting at a spinning wheel. Pigeons were cooing nearby. Her long hair was white as silver, but her eyes were bright and young. "Come in, my dear Irene," she said kindly. "I am your great-great-grandmother, and my name is Irene, just like yours." Irene was delighted. But when she told Lootie, the nurse only laughed at her.

One evening, Irene and Lootie walked too far up the mountain. When they turned back, the sun was already down. The path was dark. Strange creatures began to appear among the rocks, and Lootie was terribly frightened. Then a cheerful voice started to sing, and the creatures ran away at once. The singer was a boy called Curdie, who worked in the mines. He walked beside them all the way back to the gate.

Curdie worked with his father, Peter, deep inside the mountain. One night he stayed behind in the mine and heard goblins talking beyond the rock. He followed the sound to a huge hall where the goblin king was speaking. "Our prince, Harelip, will marry the princess from the house," said the goblin king. "We will dig a way under the house and carry her off." The goblins had a second plan, too. They were going to let a great lake of underground water into the miners' tunnels. Then the goblins found Curdie. He stamped on the goblin queen's foot, and she cried out loudly. But there were too many goblins. They shut him in a cave behind some stones.

A few days earlier, the old lady gave Irene a ring. A thread, finer than a spider's web, ran from the ring to a ball. "When you are in trouble, follow the thread," she told Irene. That night Irene woke up, felt the thread with her finger, and followed it. It led her out of the house and into a hole in the mountain. At last, it stopped at a heap of stones. She heard Curdie's voice behind them, so she pulled the stones away one by one. Then she took his hand, and the thread led them both safely out. Curdie could not see the thread. And he did not believe in the old lady at all.

Curdie told the soldiers at the house about the goblins' plan. Nobody believed him. A few nights later, the goblins broke through the floor of the house. Curdie was waiting for them. He ran among them and stamped on their soft feet as hard as he could. The goblins howled and hopped away in all directions. At the same moment King-Papa arrived. His soldiers chased the goblins out. Irene was not in the house. When the noise started, her thread led her up the mountain to Curdie's home. Curdie's mother looked after her there until morning.

The goblins still had their second plan. They let the underground water loose, but the miners already had a strong wall. So the water turned and poured back into the goblins' own halls. It carried them into the deepest places of the mountain. The goblins never troubled anyone again.

King-Papa thanked Curdie warmly and asked him to come and live at the palace. Curdie said he wanted to stay with his mother and father for now. So the king rode away with his daughter. Irene still wore her grandmother's ring.

## Level B2

There was once a little princess called Irene, who was about eight years old. Her father, the king, was usually travelling around his kingdom. So she was brought up in a large old house halfway up a mountain. Her nurse, Lootie, was kind but rather nervous, and she worried a great deal about one thing in particular. The princess was never to be outside after dark. Whenever her father rode up to visit, Irene flew into his arms and called him King-Papa.

The reason for Lootie's fear lay under the mountain. Long ago, according to the miners, a group of people had left the sunlit world and gone to live in its caves. Over the centuries they had become the goblins: short, odd-looking creatures with heads as hard as rock. Their feet, however, were soft and had no toes, and that was their great weakness. They could not bear music either, and a cheerful rhyme was enough to send them running. Above all, they hated the people who lived in the sunshine.

One wet afternoon, Irene grew tired of her toys and slipped away to explore. She climbed a narrow staircase she had never noticed before. Up and up it went, until she reached a door at the top of a tower. In the room behind it, an old lady sat spinning, while pigeons murmured on the window ledge. The lady's hair fell around her like silver, yet her face was smooth, with eyes full of light. "Come in, Irene," she said, as if she had been expecting her. "I am your great-great-grandmother, and you were named after me." Irene listened to her for a long time and went downstairs feeling very happy. When she told Lootie, though, the nurse decided that the child had been dreaming. For many days afterwards Irene could not find the stairs again.

Not long after that, the nurse and the princess wandered too far on a summer evening. By the time they turned for home, the sun had set, leaving long shadows across the road. Odd shapes began to creep out from behind the rocks, and Lootie seized Irene's hand in terror. Then a boy's clear voice rang out with a silly, made-up song, and the shapes fled. The singer was Curdie, a twelve-year-old miner who knew exactly how to deal with goblins. He walked them home, still singing, but Lootie was too proud to thank him properly.

Curdie worked in the mine beside his father, Peter, and he often stayed on after the others had left. One evening he heard voices beyond the rock. He followed a crack until he could see into a vast hall. The goblin king was making a speech. "Our son, Prince Harelip, shall marry a princess of the sun people," he announced. "We will tunnel up beneath her house and carry her down to us." And if that failed, the goblins had another scheme: they would break into a great underground lake and flood the miners' tunnels. Curdie crept closer to hear more, but he was spotted. In the struggle he stamped on the goblin queen's foot, and she let out a terrible shriek. She even lost one of her heavy stone shoes. There were too many goblins, however, and in the end they walled him up in a small cave.

Meanwhile, the old lady had given Irene a ring. It was tied to a thread so fine that nobody else could see it. The other end was fastened to a ball in the tower room. "If you are ever frightened, take hold of the thread and follow it," the lady told her. One night Irene woke from a bad dream and did exactly that. The thread led her out of the house and up the hillside, then into a dark opening in the mountain. Deep inside, it disappeared into a heap of stones, and from behind the stones came Curdie's voice. Irene pulled at the stones until her hands were sore, and at last he could squeeze through. Holding the thread, she led him out again into the starlight. To her disappointment, Curdie could not see the thread. When she took him to the tower, he saw nothing but an empty attic, some straw and an old tub. "Never mind," her grandmother told her afterwards. "He will understand one day."

Curdie now knew that the goblins were digging towards the house, so he warned the soldiers there. Nobody took a miner's boy seriously. A few nights later the floor of the kitchen burst open, and the goblins poured into the house. Curdie had been expecting this, and he was ready. He dashed among them, stamping on their soft feet with all his weight, and the goblins howled and hopped about in confusion. Just then King-Papa arrived with his soldiers, and the goblins were driven back down into their holes. When the servants searched for the princess, however, she was nowhere to be found. At the first noise, Irene had felt for her thread, and it had led her up the mountain to Curdie's cottage. His mother had looked after her there, and in the morning Curdie brought her safely back.

The goblins still had one plan left. Deep under the mountain they broke open the walls of the underground lake. But Curdie and his father had warned the miners, who had already built a strong wall across their own tunnels. The water found no way into the mine, so it rushed back through the goblins' halls instead. It swept them away into the deepest parts of the mountain, and nobody was troubled by goblins again.

The king was deeply grateful. He asked Curdie to join his guard, but Curdie said that he could not leave his parents yet. So King-Papa rode away with Irene beside him, and she kept her grandmother's ring safe on her finger.

## Level C1

Halfway up a great mountain, in a rambling house that had once been a country castle, there lived a little princess of about eight called Irene. Her father, the king, spent most of his life riding from one corner of his realm to another, and he judged the mountain air better for his daughter than the city. She was looked after by a nurse named Lootie, a well-meaning woman whose chief anxiety was that the princess must never be out of doors after sunset. On the rare days when her father came clattering up the road with his horsemen, Irene would race to the gate and into the arms of her King-Papa.

Nobody in the house would explain the nurse's rule, but every miner in the valley could have done so. Long ago, the old stories said, a race of people had quarrelled with the king of that time and withdrawn into the hollows of the mountain. There, generations of darkness had slowly changed them into goblins. They had grown squat and peculiar, with skulls as hard as granite, yet their feet had stayed soft and toeless, a weakness they guarded with clumsy shoes. Stranger still, they could not abide singing, so that a single verse of nonsense was enough to scatter a whole crowd of them. What they cherished most was a grudge, slowly polished over the centuries, against everyone who lived in the light.

It was on a dismal, rainy day, with nothing to do and nobody to play with, that Irene wandered off and discovered a staircase she had never seen. Curious rather than afraid, she climbed until she came to a small door at the top of a tower, and from behind it there came a sweet, steady humming. Inside sat an old lady at a spinning wheel, while pigeons cooed on the sill, and Irene had never seen anyone so old and so beautiful at once. Her hair hung about her shoulders like a waterfall of silver, but her face was smooth, with eyes as clear as a child's. "Come in, Irene," she said, with no surprise whatever. "I am your great-great-grandmother, and it was after me that you were named." The princess came down the stairs glowing with her secret, only to find that Lootie regarded the whole thing as a rainy-day fancy. Worse, when Irene went looking for the staircase the next morning, and for many mornings afterwards, she simply could not find it.

One fine summer evening, the nurse and the princess strayed so far along the mountain road that the sun slipped behind the peaks before they noticed. Dusk thickened around them, and out of it crept shapes that were not quite animals and not quite people, gathering on both sides of the path. Lootie snatched up Irene's hand and began to run, gasping with fright, when somewhere ahead a boy's voice burst into a ridiculous rhyme about the goblins and their feet. The shapes melted back among the rocks as though the song had burned them. The singer turned out to be Curdie, a miner's son of about twelve, who escorted them to the very gate and kept singing all the way. Lootie, to Irene's embarrassment, could hardly bring herself to thank a common miner's boy.

Curdie worked beside his father, Peter, in the galleries deep inside the mountain, and he had a habit of staying on after the others had gone home. One evening, hearing voices beyond the rock, he followed a narrow crack until it opened onto an enormous cavern lit by smoky torches and packed with goblins. The goblin king, perched on a rough stone throne, was addressing his people, and what he said made Curdie hold his breath. "Our son, Prince Harelip, shall take a wife from among the sun people, and it is the little princess herself whom we shall have," the king declared. "We shall dig a passage up beneath her house, and on the chosen night we shall carry her down into the dark." Should that scheme fail, they had another in reserve: to break open a vast underground lake and send its waters roaring through the miners' tunnels.

Curdie edged forward to catch the details, and in doing so gave himself away. In the scuffle that followed he brought his heel down hard on the goblin queen's foot, and she let out a screech that echoed round the cavern. In her fury she even lost one of her stone shoes. It was a small victory, and a funny one, but there were far too many goblins for one boy. Before long they had him walled up inside a narrow cave, with only a heap of loose stones between him and the passage beyond.

Some time before this, Irene had found the tower again, and her grandmother had placed on her finger a ring of plain gold. Fastened to it was a thread so fine that it was almost nothing at all, whose other end was rolled into a ball that the old lady kept beside her wheel. "If you are ever frightened, put your finger on the thread and follow wherever it leads, even if it seems to take you the wrong way," she said. On the night Curdie was taken, Irene woke from a nightmare, remembered the advice, and slipped out of bed with her finger on the thread. It drew her out of the house, up the dark hillside and, to her dismay, straight into a black opening in the side of the mountain. On and on it went through twisting passages, until it vanished into a heap of stones, from behind which a familiar voice was singing rather defiantly. Irene tore at the stones until her hands were aching, and at last Curdie squeezed through, more astonished than he would admit.

Holding the thread, she led him back through the tunnels and out under the stars, chattering about her grandmother all the way. Curdie, however, could feel no thread at all. When she brought him to the tower, all he could see was a bare attic with an old tub, a heap of musty straw and a single beam of moonlight. He went home puzzled and a little cross, convinced that the princess was teasing him. Irene, close to tears, climbed back up and found her grandmother as real as ever. "You must not be hard on him," said the old lady gently. "Seeing is not given to everyone at the same moment, and one day he will see."

Curdie, whatever he thought of grandmothers, believed entirely in goblins, and he did his best to warn the soldiers who guarded the house. They laughed at him. So it was that, a few nights later, the kitchen floor heaved and split, and a swarm of goblins came pouring up into the house in search of the princess. Curdie had been keeping watch, and he flung himself into the thick of them, stamping on every soft foot he could reach. The effect was remarkable: the invaders yelped, clutched their toes and went hopping and tumbling over one another in their hurry to get away. At that very hour King-Papa arrived with his soldiers, and the goblins were driven back into their holes in complete disorder.

Yet when the servants searched the house, the princess was nowhere to be found, and for a dreadful hour everyone feared the worst. In fact, at the first crash Irene had felt for her thread, and it had guided her through the night up the mountain to Curdie's cottage. There his mother had wrapped her in a blanket and kept her warm by the fire, and at dawn Curdie carried her safely home to her father.

The goblins, beaten above ground, now turned to their second plan. Far below, they broke through the walls that held back the underground lake, expecting to flood the miners' tunnels in a single night. But Curdie and Peter had warned the other miners long before, and together they had sealed off their own galleries with a strong wall. Finding no way into the mine, the water turned back upon the goblins' own halls and swept through them with tremendous force. It carried the whole goblin nation away into the deepest, darkest places of the mountain, and from that day nobody heard of them again.

The king, who had so nearly lost his daughter, could not thank Curdie enough. He begged the boy to come with him and serve in his guard, but Curdie answered that his mother and father needed him, and he would not leave them yet. So King-Papa rode away down the mountain with the princess beside him, and on her finger, catching the morning light, was her grandmother's ring.
