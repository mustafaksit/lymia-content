# Jason and the Golden Fleece

Summary: A young prince must bring home a magic golden fleece to win back his father's throne.

## Level A1

Jason was a king's son in Greece. But his uncle Pelias took the throne. So Jason grew up far away. His teacher was Chiron, half man, half horse. Chiron taught him many things.

Jason grew tall and strong. Then he went home to Iolcus. On the way, he carried an old woman. He lost one shoe in a river. The old woman was a goddess. King Pelias saw the shoe and felt afraid. "Give me my father's throne," said Jason. "First bring me the Golden Fleece," said Pelias. "I promise," said Jason.

The fleece came from a golden sheep. It hung on a tree in Colchis. A big dragon kept it safe. This dragon never slept.

Jason needed a good ship. Men built a great ship, the Argo. Many heroes came with Jason. Strong Heracles came, and Orpheus with his music. They were the Argonauts.

Two big rocks stood in the sea. They crashed together again and again. Jason let a white bird fly first. The bird got through and lost a feather. Then the heroes rowed very fast. The Argo got through too.

At last, they came to Colchis. The king there was Aeetes. He had two bulls with fiery mouths. "Work my field with them," said the king. "Then plant these dragon teeth."

Medea, the king's daughter, wanted to help. She gave Jason a magic oil. "Now the fire can't touch you," said Medea. Jason worked the field with the bulls. He planted the dragon teeth. Soldiers grew out of the ground! Jason threw a stone among them. The soldiers fought each other. Soon they all went back into the earth.

But King Aeetes still said no. That night, Medea took Jason to the tree. She gave the dragon a magic drink. The dragon closed its eyes and slept. Jason took the Golden Fleece. They ran to the Argo and sailed away.

After a long trip, the Argo came home. Jason carried the shining fleece into Iolcus.

## Level A2

Long ago, a good king ruled Iolcus in Greece. But his brother Pelias took the throne from him. The old king sent his little son, Jason, far away. The boy grew up with Chiron, half man and half horse. Chiron was very wise, and he taught Jason to be brave.

Later, Jason went down to Iolcus. On the way, he came to a wide river. An old woman stood on the bank. "Please carry me across," she said. Jason carried her across, and he lost one shoe. On the other side, the old woman became a shining goddess. "I am your friend, and I can help you," she said.

In Iolcus, King Pelias saw the young man with one shoe. His face went white with fear. "I am Jason, and I want my father's throne," said Jason. "First bring me the Golden Fleece from Colchis," said Pelias. "I promise," said Jason.

The fleece was the golden skin of a magic sheep. It hung on a tree in Colchis, far across the sea. A dragon guarded it day and night, and the dragon never slept.

A clever builder made a fast ship, the Argo. The best heroes in Greece came with Jason. Strong Heracles came, and so did Orpheus with his beautiful music. They called themselves the Argonauts. But the new ship stuck in the sand. Then Orpheus played and sang, and the Argo slid into the sea.

After many weeks, they came to two great rocks in the sea. The rocks crashed together again and again. Jason let a white dove fly between them. The rocks crashed shut, but the dove got through. "Row now!" cried Jason. The heroes rowed hard, and the Argo got through.

At last, they reached Colchis. "First you must do a task for me," said King Aeetes. The king had two bulls with bronze feet, and they breathed fire. Jason had to work a big field with them. Then he had to plant dragon teeth in the ground.

The king's daughter Medea felt sorry for Jason. Medea knew magic, and she gave him a magic oil. "Put this on your body, and fire can't harm you. Men grow from the ground. Throw a stone among them," said Medea.

In the morning, Jason walked into the field. The bulls ran at him, and fire came from their mouths. But the fire did not burn him. He held the bulls by the horns and made them work. Then he planted the dragon teeth. Soon soldiers grew out of the ground. Jason threw a big stone among them. The soldiers fought each other and fell back into the earth.

But King Aeetes still did not give the fleece. That night, Medea led Jason to the tree in a dark wood. The dragon was there. Medea sang softly and gave it a magic drink. Slowly the great dragon closed its eyes and slept. Jason took the Golden Fleece. They ran to the Argo, and the heroes rowed away before morning.

After a long trip, the Argo came home to Greece. Jason carried the shining fleece into Iolcus.

## Level B1

Long ago, a cruel man named Pelias took the throne of Iolcus from his own brother. The old king was afraid for his baby son, Jason, so he sent him away. The child grew up in a mountain cave with Chiron, half man and half horse. Chiron was a wise teacher, and he taught Jason to hunt and to heal.

When Jason was a young man, he went down to win back his father's throne. On the way, he came to a river that was wild after the winter rains. An old woman was sitting on the bank, and she asked him to carry her across. Jason carried her on his back, and the fast water pulled off one of his sandals. On the far bank, the old woman changed into a tall, shining goddess. "You have a kind heart, Jason," she said. "I will help you on every road you take."

Jason walked into Iolcus with only one sandal. When King Pelias saw the stranger's bare foot, he turned pale. Years before, a voice from the gods warned him about a man with one sandal. "I am Jason, the son of the true king," the young man said. "I am here for my father's throne." Pelias hid his fear and smiled. "The throne will be yours," he answered, "but first, bring me the Golden Fleece from Colchis." "I promise to bring it," said Jason.

The fleece was the golden coat of a magic ram from long ago. It hung on a tree in Colchis, at the far end of the world. A huge dragon, which never closed its eyes, guarded it day and night.

With the goddess's help, a clever builder made a strong ship, the Argo. Soon the bravest heroes in the land arrived to sail with Jason. Heracles, the strongest man alive, came, and so did Orpheus, whose music could move stones. They called themselves the Argonauts. At first, the heavy ship stuck in the sand. Then Orpheus began to play and sing, and the Argo slid gently into the sea.

On the way east, an old blind king gave them some advice. "Soon you will come to two blue rocks that crash together," he said. "Send a dove between them first. Where a bird can pass, a ship can pass too." At the rocks, the Argonauts watched the great stone walls crashing together. The rocks closed like thunder, but the dove flew out and lost only a few feathers. As the rocks opened again, the heroes rowed with all their strength. The Argo raced through, and the rocks touched only the very end of the ship.

At last, they reached Colchis. King Aeetes met them in his golden hall with cold eyes. "You may take the fleece after you finish one small task," he said. "I have two bulls with feet of bronze, and they breathe fire. Plow my field with them, and then plant these dragon's teeth in the earth." He was sure that no man could do such a thing.

But the king's daughter, Medea, was listening. She knew all kinds of magic, and she felt sorry for the brave stranger. That night she met Jason in secret and gave him a jar of magic oil. "Put this on your body, and the fire will not touch you," she said. "Men will grow from the ground, so throw a stone into the middle of them."

In the morning, all Colchis was watching. The bulls rushed out, roaring, with flames pouring from their mouths. Jason stood still, and the fire went around him like a warm wind. He caught the bulls by the horns and plowed the whole field with them. Then he planted the dragon's teeth, and soon soldiers were rising out of the earth. Jason threw a heavy stone among them. Each soldier blamed his neighbor for the stone, and they began to fight. Soon they all sank back into the ground, and the field was quiet again.

King Aeetes was furious, and he was secretly planning to burn the Argo that night. Medea knew about his plan, so she led Jason through the dark woods to the tree. The fleece was shining on a branch, and the dragon was lying below it. Medea sang a soft song and poured a sleeping potion over the dragon's head. Slowly its great eyes closed, and for the first time in its life, it slept. Jason climbed up and lifted down the heavy, glowing fleece. They ran to the shore, and the Argonauts rowed out to sea before sunrise. When the king's men reached the beach, the Argo was already far away.

The journey home was long and hard, but at last the Argo sailed back into Greece. Jason walked into Iolcus, and the Golden Fleece shone on his shoulders like the sun.

## Level B2

Long ago, the city of Iolcus in Greece had a gentle king. His half-brother Pelias, a proud and greedy man, took the throne from him by force. The old king feared that Pelias would harm his baby son, Jason, so he secretly carried the child into the mountains. There he left him with Chiron, the wise old centaur, who was half man and half horse. In Chiron's cave, Jason learned to hunt, to heal the sick and, above all, to speak the truth.

When Jason was grown, Chiron told him the story of his family and sent him down to win back his father's kingdom. On the way, he reached a river that the winter rains had turned into a roaring brown flood. An old woman was sitting on the bank, and she begged him to carry her across. Jason took her on his back, although the current nearly knocked him over. In the middle of the river, one of his sandals was torn from his foot. On the far bank, the old woman rose up tall and bright, and Jason saw the queen of the gods. "You helped a poor old woman when nobody was watching," she said. "Now I will help you, wherever you go."

So Jason came into the marketplace of Iolcus wearing only one sandal. When King Pelias noticed the stranger's bare foot, his face turned gray. Long before, an oracle had warned him that a man with one sandal would one day take his kingdom. "I am Jason, son of the king you drove away," the young man said. "I have come to claim my father's throne." Pelias hid his fear behind a friendly smile. He said that the Golden Fleece belonged to their family by right. The gods, he explained, would never bless the kingdom until someone brought it home from Colchis. "I am old, but you are young and strong," he said. "Bring back the fleece, and the throne is yours." Pelias was sure the young man would never return, yet Jason gave his promise gladly.

The fleece had once belonged to a magic ram with a coat of gold. Long ago, the ram had carried a boy from Greece over the sea to safety. Ever since, its shining skin had hung from an oak tree in a sacred wood in Colchis. A dragon that never slept guarded the tree day and night.

A skilled builder made a ship with the help of the goddess of wisdom. She placed in its bow a branch from a magic oak that could speak with a human voice. The ship was called the Argo, and it carried fifty oars. From every part of Greece, heroes came to join the voyage, among them mighty Heracles and Orpheus with his harp. They called themselves the Argonauts. On the day of their departure, however, the heavy Argo stuck fast in the sand. Then Orpheus played his harp and sang so sweetly that the ship slid down into the water by itself.

In one wild land on the way east, Heracles went into the forest to look for a young friend who had disappeared. He never came back, and the ship had to sail on without him. Later, the heroes helped an old blind king, and in return he warned them about the greatest danger on their road. "Beyond my land are two blue rocks that crash together like angry giants," he said. "Let a dove fly between them first, and if the bird passes, row with all your strength." At the narrow channel, the Argonauts saw the cliffs swing apart and slam together in clouds of white spray. Jason released the dove. The rocks met with a crash like thunder, but the bird escaped, leaving only a few tail feathers behind. As the cliffs opened again, the Argonauts bent to their oars, and the Argo flew through the gap. Behind them, the rocks closed once more, touching only the carved end of the ship.

At last, the Argonauts sailed up the wide river to Colchis. King Aeetes listened to Jason's request with growing anger, but he hid it behind a smile. "You may have the fleece," he said, "if you can do what I do. I have two bulls with bronze feet, and their breath is fire. Yoke them, plow my field and sow it with these dragon's teeth."

The king's daughter, Medea, had been watching Jason from behind her father's throne. She was a powerful enchantress, and the queen of the gods had filled her heart with pity for the young man. That night, she met him in a temple and gave him a jar of magic ointment. "Spread this over your body and your shield, and neither fire nor bronze will harm you," she said. "When armed men spring from the earth, do not face them yourself. Throw a stone among them, and let them deal with each other."

The next morning, the whole city gathered to watch. The bulls charged out of their dark stable with flames pouring from their mouths. Yet the fire only flowed around Jason like warm air. He seized the bulls by the horns, forced them under the yoke and plowed the whole field. Then he scattered the dragon's teeth, and soon the soil began to move. Helmets appeared first, then shoulders, until a whole army of soldiers stood in the field. Jason threw a great stone into the middle of them. Each soldier believed that the stone had come from his neighbor, and they turned on one another. When the struggle was over, every one of them had sunk back into the earth.

Aeetes did not keep his word. Instead, he planned to burn the Argo and drive the heroes from his land that very night. Medea learned of the plan, and she knew that she could no longer stay with her father. She led Jason through the dark sacred wood to the oak, where the fleece glowed like a cloud of fire. The dragon raised its huge head and hissed. Medea sang to it softly and sprinkled a sleeping potion over its eyes. Little by little, the great body relaxed, and the creature that had never slept fell into a deep sleep. Jason climbed the tree, lifted down the fleece and wrapped it around his shoulders. They hurried to the shore, where the Argonauts were waiting at their oars. By the time the king's men reached the river mouth, the Argo was far out at sea.

Many storms lay between Colchis and home, but the queen of the gods kept her promise. The Argo came safely back to Greece, and Jason walked into Iolcus with the Golden Fleece shining on his back.

## Level C1

In the days when heroes still walked the hills of Greece, the city of Iolcus lost its rightful king to his half-brother Pelias, a man as cunning as he was greedy. Fearing for his infant son, the old king stole away by night and carried the child up the slopes of a wooded mountain. There, in a cave hung with vines, lived Chiron the centaur, half man and half horse, the wisest teacher the world had known. Under his care Jason grew up among the sons of other heroes, learning to hunt and to heal, and above all never to speak a lie.

When the boy had become a man, Chiron told him at last who he was and bade him go down to claim what was his. The winter rains had swollen a river on his road into a brown, roaring torrent, and on its bank sat a bent old woman, begging to be carried over. Jason hesitated only a moment before taking her on his back and wading in, though the current dragged at his legs. Midway across, the water tore one sandal from his foot and swept it away, yet he struggled on until he could set his burden down. Then the old woman straightened, her rags became a robe of light, and Jason found himself looking up at the queen of the gods herself. "Many proud men passed me by today," she said, "but you stopped. Remember me in your hour of need, for I shall not forget you."

So it was that Jason strode into the marketplace of Iolcus with one foot bare. Pelias stood there at the altar, and the moment his eyes fell upon that single sandal, the color drained from his face. An oracle had long ago warned him to beware of a man wearing one shoe, and here, it seemed, the warning stood before him. "I am Jason, son of the king you drove out," the young man declared before the whole crowd, "and I have come home for my father's throne." Pelias, whose tongue had always been quicker than his conscience, answered with a show of warmth. The Golden Fleece, he explained, belonged by right to their family, and the gods would never smile on Iolcus until someone brought it home from distant Colchis. "I am grown old, while you are young and strong," he said. "Fetch the fleece, and the kingdom is yours with my blessing." He spoke as one who sends a man on a journey from which nobody returns, but Jason swore at once to go.

The fleece had come from a wondrous golden ram, which many years before had carried a frightened boy across the sea to Colchis. There its shining skin hung upon an oak in a sacred grove, and around the trunk lay coiled a dragon whose eyes had never once closed in sleep.

No ship then afloat could have made such a voyage, so a master builder set to work with the goddess of wisdom guiding his hands. Into the bow she fitted a beam cut from a speaking oak, so that the ship itself could give counsel to those who sailed in her. They named the vessel the Argo, and she carried fifty oars. Word of the voyage spread through Greece, and the noblest heroes of the age came to join it, among them mighty Heracles and Orpheus, whose harp could charm the very stones. Calling themselves the Argonauts, they gathered on the beach, ready to depart. But when the moment came, the great ship would not stir from the sand, though fifty men strained against her sides. It was Orpheus who saved the day, for he played so sweetly that the Argo, as though she too had been listening, slid of her own accord into the sea.

Not every hero who set out was to see the voyage end. On one wooded shore, Heracles went into the forest to search for a young companion who had vanished, and the Argonauts, after waiting in vain, sailed on without him. Further along the coast they freed an old blind king from his long troubles, and in gratitude he warned them of what lay ahead. "Beyond my country," he told them, "two blue rocks guard the way into the eastern sea, rushing together and springing apart without rest. Send a dove through before you, and if she escapes, row as you have never rowed in your lives." They heard the rocks long before they saw them, a booming like endless thunder, and then the cliffs swung into view amid towering clouds of spray. Jason loosed the dove from his hands. Straight between the walls she flew, and they closed upon her with a roar, yet when they parted she was beyond them, missing only the tips of her tail. At once the heroes threw their weight upon the oars, and the Argo leapt forward into the gap. Behind them the rocks slammed together one last time, catching only the carved ornament at the stern, and from that day on, the tale goes, they stood still.

At length the Argonauts rowed up the broad river to Colchis, where King Aeetes, who claimed the sun as his father, received them in a palace bright with bronze. He heard Jason's request with a face like stone, and though rage burned behind his eyes, he answered smoothly. "The fleece is yours," he said, "provided you can do in one day what I have done many times. In my stables stand two bulls with hooves of bronze, whose breath is living fire. Yoke them, plow the field of the war god, and sow it with these dragon's teeth." Nobody in the hall doubted that he was sending the stranger to certain failure.

Yet one person in that hall saw the matter differently. Medea, the king's daughter, was a priestess skilled in every kind of enchantment, and the queen of the gods now stirred pity for the calm young Greek in her heart. Long she argued with herself through the night, torn between loyalty to her father and her wish to save the stranger. At last, before dawn, she sent for Jason to meet her at a quiet temple, and there she pressed a little jar of ointment into his hands. "Anoint your body and your shield with this, and for one whole day neither fire nor bronze can harm you," she whispered. "When the earth gives birth to armed men, do not stand against them. Cast a stone into their midst, and leave the rest to them."

The next morning, the people of Colchis crowded the hillsides to watch. Out of their dark stable thundered the bulls, bellowing, with flames streaming from their nostrils, but the fire merely parted around Jason like mist around a rock. Seizing the beasts by their horns, he wrestled them beneath the yoke and drove them up and down until the whole field lay plowed. Then he scattered the dragon's teeth over the dark soil, and before long the earth itself began to heave. First came the glint of helmets, then shields and shoulders, until a whole host of soldiers stood in the field, glaring about them. Remembering Medea's words, Jason hurled a heavy stone into the thick of them. Each soldier, believing his neighbor had thrown it, turned on the man beside him, until one by one they sank back into the earth that had made them.

Aeetes had no intention of keeping his bargain. That very evening he resolved to set fire to the Argo while the Greeks slept, and Medea, learning of it, understood that her father's house was no longer safe for her. Through the black and silent grove she led Jason to the oak, where the fleece hung glowing among the branches like a cloud lit by the setting sun. Around the trunk the dragon uncoiled itself and lifted its great head, hissing, its unsleeping eyes fixed upon the intruders. Medea did not flinch; she began to sing, low and soft, and as she sang she sprinkled a sleeping potion over its head. Slowly, as if the weariness of ages had come upon it at once, the creature lowered its head to the ground, and for the first time, the guardian slept. Jason climbed swiftly into the tree, lifted down the heavy, shining skin and threw it about his shoulders, and together they ran for the river. The Argonauts were already at their oars, and before the first gray light touched the sky, the Argo was gliding silently towards the open sea. When Aeetes and his men came racing down to the shore, they found only an empty mooring and the ripples of a departed ship.

Many a storm still lay between Colchis and home, but the queen of the gods remembered the young man who had carried her across the river. She brought the Argo safely back to Greece. Jason walked once more into the marketplace of Iolcus, no longer a stranger with one sandal, and the Golden Fleece blazed upon his shoulders like the sun.
