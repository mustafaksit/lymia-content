# The Necklace

Summary: A poor clerk's wife borrows a diamond necklace for a grand party, and one night changes her life.

## Level A1

Mathilde was a pretty young woman. She lived in Paris with her husband. He worked in a small office. They did not have much money. Mathilde wanted beautiful things. She dreamed of fine dresses and jewels. Every day, she felt sad.

One evening, her husband came home happy. He had a card in his hand. "Look, Mathilde!" said her husband. "We can go to a big party!" But Mathilde started to cry. "I have no dress," she said. So her husband gave her his own money. She bought a lovely new dress.

But Mathilde was still sad. "I have no jewels," she said. Her husband said, "Visit your friend Jeanne. She is rich."

Jeanne opened a big box. Inside, Mathilde saw a diamond necklace. It was very beautiful. "Can I take this one?" asked Mathilde. "Yes, of course," said Jeanne.

At the party, Mathilde looked wonderful. Everyone looked at her. She danced all night. She was very happy. Late at night, they went home.

Mathilde looked in the mirror at home. The necklace was not on her neck! Her husband went back to the streets. He looked everywhere for many days. But they did not find it.

In a shop, they found a similar necklace. It cost a lot of money. Her husband borrowed money from many people. Mathilde took the new necklace to Jeanne. Jeanne did not open the box.

Then life was very hard. Mathilde and her husband worked day and night. She washed, cleaned and carried water. After ten years, they paid everything back. Now Mathilde looked old and tired.

One Sunday, Mathilde saw Jeanne in Paris. Jeanne was still young and pretty. "Jeanne, it is me, Mathilde!" she said. "Oh, Mathilde, you look so different!" said Jeanne. Mathilde told her the whole story. Jeanne took her hands. Then she said, "Oh, my poor Mathilde! My necklace was not real. It cost very little!"

## Level A2

Mathilde was a pretty and charming young woman in Paris. Her family was not rich. Her husband, Loisel, worked in a government office. But Mathilde was not happy with their simple life. She dreamed of big houses, fine dresses and expensive jewels. She had a rich friend from school, Jeanne Forestier.

One evening, Loisel came home with a big white envelope. Inside was an invitation to a grand party at the ministry. Mathilde threw it on the table and cried, "What can I wear? I have no dress for a party!" "How much does a nice dress cost?" asked Loisel. "About four hundred francs," answered Mathilde. Loisel went a little pale. He wanted that money for summer Sundays in the country with friends. But he gave it to her.

A few days before the party, Mathilde looked sad again. "I have no jewels, not one," she told her husband. Then Loisel said, "Go to your friend Jeanne Forestier. Ask her for some jewels."

The next day, Jeanne opened a big box for Mathilde. Then Mathilde found a black box with a diamond necklace. "Can I take this one, only this one?" she asked. "Yes, of course," said Jeanne.

At the party, Mathilde was the prettiest woman of all. All the men wanted to dance with her. They left at four in the morning. Mathilde felt ashamed of her old coat. The other women had warm, expensive coats. She hurried down the stairs, and they found an old cab.

At home, she stood in front of the mirror. Suddenly she gave a cry. The necklace was not around her neck! Loisel went back to the streets and looked everywhere. He also went to the police. Mathilde wrote to Jeanne and asked for a little more time. After a week, they lost all hope.

In a shop, they found a necklace just like it. It cost thirty-six thousand francs! Loisel had eighteen thousand francs from his father. He borrowed the rest from many different people. Mathilde took the new necklace to Jeanne. "You are very late with it," said Jeanne coldly. She did not open the box.

Then a hard life began. Mathilde and Loisel moved to a tiny room under the roof. She washed clothes and carried water up the stairs every morning. At the market, she argued over every coin. Loisel worked late at night too. After ten years, they paid back all the money.

Now Mathilde was not young or pretty. Her hands were red, and her voice was loud. One Sunday, she took a walk in Paris and saw Jeanne. Jeanne was still young and beautiful. Mathilde went up to her and said, "Good morning, Jeanne." Jeanne did not know her. "I am Mathilde Loisel," said Mathilde. "Oh, my poor Mathilde, you look so different!" cried Jeanne. Then Mathilde told her the whole story of the necklace. "We worked for ten years to pay for it," she said proudly. Jeanne took both her hands and said, "Oh, my poor Mathilde! My necklace was not real. It was worth five hundred francs at most!"

## Level B1

Mathilde was a pretty and charming young woman, but her family was poor and ordinary. She had no money, so she could not marry a rich man. Instead, she married Loisel, a quiet clerk at a government ministry. He was a kind man who loved her and asked for very little from life. The plain walls and old chairs of their flat made her feel poor and miserable. She dreamed of large, quiet rooms, silk curtains and elegant dinners with important guests. She had a rich friend from her school days, Jeanne Forestier, but she rarely visited her. After each visit, she came home and cried for days.

One evening, Loisel came home looking very pleased and handed her a large envelope. It was an invitation to an evening party at the ministry where he worked. Loisel expected his wife to be delighted, but she threw the card onto the table. "And what will I wear?" cried Mathilde, and tears ran down her face. At last, he asked how much a suitable dress would cost. Mathilde thought for a while and then answered, "About four hundred francs." Loisel turned a little pale. He was saving exactly that amount for summer Sundays in the country with his friends. Still, he said, "All right. I will give you the money, so buy a really beautiful dress."

Soon the dress was ready. Mathilde, however, was looking anxious and unhappy again. "I don't have a single jewel to wear," she told her husband. "Wear some fresh roses," said Loisel. But Mathilde said there was nothing more shameful than looking poor among rich women. "Then ask your friend Jeanne Forestier to lend you some jewels," cried Loisel.

The next day, Jeanne took out a big box and opened it for Mathilde. "Choose whatever you like," she said. Mathilde looked at bracelets, pearls and gold crosses, but she could not decide. Then she opened a small black box and found a wonderful diamond necklace. "Can you lend me this one, only this one?" she asked. "Yes, of course," said Jeanne, and Mathilde hugged her happily.

She was the prettiest woman there, elegant, smiling and full of joy. All the men wanted to know her name, and even the minister noticed her. She danced for hours and thought of nothing except her own beauty and happiness. Meanwhile, her husband was sleeping in a little empty room with three other bored husbands.

They left at about four o'clock in the morning. Loisel put a plain old coat around her shoulders. Next to the rich furs of the other women, the coat looked terribly poor. Outside, they walked for a long time before they found an old cab. At home, she stood in front of the mirror for one last look at herself. Suddenly she cried out, because the necklace was no longer around her neck.

They searched her dress, her coat and every pocket, but they found nothing. Loisel went back out and walked the whole way they came. He went to the police and offered a reward in the newspapers. Mathilde wrote to Jeanne that the clasp of the necklace was broken. After a week, they had no hope left, and Loisel looked five years older.

In a shop, they finally found a necklace that looked exactly the same. It cost forty thousand francs, but the shopkeeper agreed to sell it for thirty-six thousand. Loisel had eighteen thousand francs from his father. He borrowed the rest from everyone he knew and signed many papers that worried him. When Mathilde took the new necklace to Jeanne, her friend spoke coldly. "You are very late with it," said Jeanne. She did not open the box, and Mathilde felt very relieved.

From that day, Mathilde lived the hard life of the poor. They sent away their maid and moved to a small room under the roof. She washed dishes and clothes herself and carried water up the stairs every morning. At the market, she argued over every coin with the shopkeepers. Loisel worked in the evenings and often copied documents late into the night. After ten years, they paid back every franc of the debt.

Mathilde now looked old, with red hands and a loud voice. One Sunday she was taking a walk in Paris when she saw Jeanne Forestier. Jeanne was still young, beautiful and charming. Mathilde went up to her and said, "Good morning, Jeanne." Jeanne did not recognize her and looked surprised. "It is me, Mathilde Loisel," said her old friend. "Oh, my poor Mathilde, how you have changed!" cried Jeanne. Then Mathilde told her about the lost necklace and the ten long years of work. "You never noticed the difference, did you?" she asked with a proud smile. Jeanne took both her hands and said, "Oh, my poor Mathilde! Mine was not real. It was worth five hundred francs at most!"

## Level B2

Mathilde was one of those pretty, charming girls who seem to have landed in the wrong family by some mistake of fate. Her parents were humble clerks, and she had no money and no way of meeting a rich, important man. So she allowed herself to be married to a modest clerk at the ministry, a quiet man called Loisel. She suffered constantly, convinced that she had been made for every luxury. The worn walls of their flat and the shabby chairs hurt her every day. She dreamed of silent rooms hung with eastern silk and of elegant dinners served on shining silver. She had a rich friend from school, Jeanne Forestier. After each visit she had come home in despair, so she no longer went.

One evening Loisel came home looking proud and handed her a large envelope. Inside was a printed card inviting them to an evening party at the ministry. Instead of being delighted, as her husband had hoped, she threw it on the table in annoyance. "But, my dear, it was very hard to get, because everybody wants one," said Loisel. She asked impatiently what he expected her to wear. He had not thought about that at all, and he stopped, astonished, when two large tears rolled down her cheeks. Loisel asked how much a suitable dress would cost, something simple that she could wear again. She thought for a few seconds and answered that she could probably manage with four hundred francs. He turned slightly pale, because he had been saving exactly that sum for summer Sundays in the country with friends. Nevertheless, he said, "Very well, I'll give you the money, but try to get a really pretty dress."

The day of the party drew near, and although her dress was ready, Mathilde seemed sad and anxious. "I don't have a single jewel to wear," she told her husband, "so I will look like a beggar among the rich." He suggested fresh roses, which were fashionable that season, but she was not convinced. Then her husband had a sudden idea and cried, "Go to your friend Jeanne Forestier! You know her well enough to borrow some jewels from her."

The next day Jeanne Forestier went to her wardrobe and came back with a large jewel box. She opened it and said, "Choose whatever you like, my dear." Mathilde saw bracelets, a string of pearls and a gold cross set with precious stones. Suddenly, in a black satin box, she discovered a superb diamond necklace, and her heart began to beat with longing. "Can you lend me this, only this?" she asked anxiously. "Yes, of course," said Jeanne, and Mathilde threw her arms around her friend and hurried away with her treasure.

On the night of the party, Mathilde was a triumph. She was prettier than all the other women, elegant, graceful, smiling and wild with joy. All the men asked her name and tried to be introduced, and even the minister noticed her. She danced with delight, thinking of nothing, lost in the pride of her beauty and success. Her husband, meanwhile, had been dozing since midnight in a small empty room with three other husbands.

They left at about four in the morning. Loisel put her plain everyday coat around her shoulders, and it looked even poorer beside her beautiful gown. She wanted to escape before the other women, wrapped in rich furs, could notice her. She ran down the stairs, and they walked toward the river, shivering, until they found an old night cab. At home, she took off the coat in front of the mirror to see herself once more in all her glory. Suddenly she gave a cry, because the necklace was no longer around her neck. They searched the folds of her dress and coat and every pocket, but they found nothing.

Loisel went out again and walked the whole way they had come on foot. Then he went to the police and put a notice in the newspapers, offering a reward. On his advice, Mathilde wrote to her friend that the clasp was broken and was being repaired. By the end of the week, they had lost all hope. Loisel, who seemed to have aged five years, declared that they had to replace the necklace.

They went from jeweler to jeweler, searching for a necklace like the lost one. At last, in a shop in an elegant arcade, they found a string of diamonds that seemed exactly the same. It was priced at forty thousand francs, but the jeweler agreed to let them have it for thirty-six thousand. Loisel had eighteen thousand francs that his father had left him, and he borrowed the rest. He borrowed from friends and from moneylenders of every kind, and he signed promises he was not sure he could keep. When Mathilde returned the necklace, Jeanne Forestier said coldly, "You should have brought it back sooner." Mathilde had been afraid that she would open the box, but she did not.

Mathilde now discovered the hard life of the poor, but she faced it with sudden courage. They sent the maid away and rented a little room under the roof. She washed the dishes and the dirty sheets herself, ruining her pink nails on greasy pots. At the market, with a basket on her arm, she argued over every coin. Her husband kept a shopkeeper's accounts in the evenings, and at night he often copied documents for a few coins.

This life lasted ten years. At the end of that time, they had paid back everything, including the high interest that the moneylenders demanded. Mathilde looked old now, a strong, rough woman from a poor household, with untidy hair and red hands. But sometimes, when her husband was at the office, she sat by the window and thought of that evening long ago. What would have happened if she had not lost the necklace?

One Sunday, walking along a wide avenue in Paris, she suddenly noticed Jeanne Forestier. Jeanne was still young, still beautiful and still charming. She went up to her and said, "Good morning, Jeanne." Jeanne did not recognize her and was surprised that such an ordinary woman spoke to her so warmly. "But, madame, you must be mistaken," said Jeanne. "No, I am Mathilde Loisel." Her friend cried out, "Oh, my poor Mathilde, how you have changed!" "Yes, I have had some very hard days since I last saw you, and all because of you," said Mathilde. Then she told her the whole story: the loss, the new necklace and the ten years of hard work. "You never noticed, did you, because they were so alike," she finished with a proud and simple joy. Jeanne, deeply moved, took both her hands. "Oh, my poor Mathilde! But mine was an imitation. It was worth five hundred francs at the very most!"

## Level C1

Mathilde was one of those pretty, charming young women who seem, through some careless error of fate, to have been born into a family of clerks. She had no money, no hope of an inheritance, and no means of being noticed and married by a man of wealth and distinction. And so she let herself be married to a modest clerk at the ministry, a quiet, good-natured man called Loisel.

She suffered endlessly, feeling that she had been born for every delicacy and every luxury the world could offer. The bareness of their flat, the shabby walls and the faded curtains hurt her pride a little more each day. She dreamed of hushed rooms hung with eastern silk and lit by tall bronze lamps, where famous men came to talk with her. She had a wealthy friend from her school days, Jeanne Forestier, whom she no longer cared to visit, because each visit left her weeping for days.

One evening Loisel came home with an air of triumph and handed her a large envelope. Inside was a printed card on which the minister and his wife requested the pleasure of their company at an evening party at the ministry. Far from being delighted, as her husband had hoped, she flung the invitation on the table with a look of contempt. "But, my dear, I had enormous trouble getting it," said Loisel, "because every clerk in the building wanted one." She gave him an irritated look and demanded to know what, exactly, she was supposed to wear to such an occasion. The question had never crossed his mind, and he fell silent, bewildered, as two large tears slid slowly down her cheeks.

Mastering her distress, she replied in a calm voice that she had no dress and therefore could not go. Loisel asked what a suitable dress would cost, something simple that might serve again on other occasions. She reflected for a few seconds, working out a figure she could ask for without drawing a cry of alarm from the thrifty clerk. At length, hesitantly, she said that she thought she could manage with four hundred francs. He went a little pale, for he had been putting aside exactly that sum to spend summer Sundays in the country with a few friends. Nevertheless, he said, "Very well, I'll give you four hundred francs, but make sure you find a really pretty dress."

As the day of the party approached, Mathilde grew sad and restless, although her gown was ready. When her husband asked what was troubling her, she confessed that it vexed her to have not a single jewel to put on. He suggested fresh roses, which were very fashionable that season, but she was not persuaded, for nothing seemed more humiliating than to look poor among the rich. "How foolish we are!" Loisel suddenly exclaimed. "Go and see your friend Jeanne Forestier and ask her to lend you some jewels."

The next day Mathilde called on her friend, and Jeanne Forestier went at once to a mirrored wardrobe and returned with a large jewel box. Mathilde saw bracelets first, then a string of pearls, then a gold cross of beautiful workmanship, and she tried them on, unable to choose. Then, in a box of black satin, she came upon a superb diamond necklace, and her heart began to pound with an uncontrollable longing. Her hands trembled as she fastened it around her throat, and she stood lost in rapture before her own reflection. "Could you lend me this, only this?" she asked at last, her voice thick with anxiety. "Why, of course," said Jeanne, and Mathilde embraced her warmly before hurrying away with her treasure.

She was prettier than any other woman present, elegant, graceful and smiling, and almost dizzy with joy. Every man turned to look at her and sought an introduction, and even the minister himself took notice of her. She danced with abandon, carried away by pleasure and thinking of nothing at all, floating on the admiration around her as if on a warm cloud. Her husband, meanwhile, had been dozing since midnight in a small deserted side room, along with three other gentlemen whose wives were enjoying themselves enormously.

It was nearly four in the morning when they left, and Loisel threw over her shoulders the modest coat of their everyday life. Its shabbiness looked all the poorer beside the splendor of her gown, and she longed to slip away unseen by the women in their costly furs. Outside, shivering, they at last found one of the battered old cabs that only come out at night, and it brought them to their door. For her, everything was over, while he was reflecting gloomily that he had to be at the office by ten.

She took off her coat before the mirror, wishing to see herself once more in all her glory, and at once she let out a cry: the necklace was gone. They searched the folds of her gown and coat and every pocket, and found nothing, and neither of them had thought to note the number of the cab. Loisel retraced on foot the whole of the route they had taken, returning at seven in the morning with empty hands. He then went to the police and to the newspapers, where he offered a reward. At his suggestion, Mathilde wrote to her friend that she had broken the clasp and was having it mended, which would give them time. By the end of the week they had abandoned all hope, and Loisel, who seemed to have grown five years older, announced that the jewel would have to be replaced.

They went from shop to shop, searching for a necklace like the other one, both of them sick with grief and worry. In a shop in an elegant arcade they at last found a string of diamonds that seemed identical to the lost one. It was priced at forty thousand francs, but the jeweler agreed to let them have it for thirty-six. Loisel possessed eighteen thousand francs left to him by his father, and the rest he would have to borrow. He borrowed a thousand francs here and five hundred there, made ruinous agreements and dealt with moneylenders of every description. When Mathilde brought the necklace back, Jeanne Forestier remarked coldly, "You really ought to have returned it sooner, because I might have needed it." To Mathilde's immense relief, she did not open the box, for what would she have thought had she noticed the substitution?

From then on Mathilde came to know the dreary life of the poor, and she took up her part in it with a courage that surprised even herself. That dreadful debt had to be paid, and she would pay it; they dismissed their maid and rented a small room under the roof. She learned the heavy work of the household and the hateful tasks of the kitchen, ruining her pink nails on greasy pots. Dressed like a woman of the people, she went to the grocer and the fruit seller with her basket on her arm, defending her miserable coins one by one. Her husband worked in the evenings, putting a shopkeeper's accounts in order, and late at night he often copied documents at a few coins a page.

This life went on for ten years, and at the end of ten years everything had been repaid, together with the crushing interest of the moneylenders. Mathilde now looked old, for she had become the strong, hard and rough woman of poor households, with untidy hair and red hands. Sometimes, though, while her husband was at the office, she sat by the window and thought of that distant evening when she had been so lovely and so admired. What would have become of her, she wondered, had she never lost that necklace? Life is so strange and changeable, and so slight a thing can undo a life or rescue it.

One Sunday, strolling along a wide avenue in Paris to refresh herself after the week's labor, she noticed a woman walking with a child. It was Jeanne Forestier, still young, still beautiful, still charming, and Mathilde, since the debt was paid, decided to go up and tell her everything. "Good morning, Jeanne," she said, but her friend did not know her and seemed taken aback at being greeted so familiarly by this woman of the people. "I'm afraid you must be mistaken, madame," said Jeanne politely. "No, I am Mathilde Loisel," came the answer, and her friend gave a cry of amazement at how much she had changed. Mathilde replied that she had indeed lived through hard times since they last met, and that all her misery had come about because of Jeanne. Then she told her everything: the borrowed necklace lost after the party, the new one bought on credit, and the ten years it had taken to pay for it. "It's over at last, and I can't tell you how glad I am," she finished with a proud and simple joy. Jeanne, deeply moved, took both of her friend's hands in her own. "Oh, my poor Mathilde! Mine was nothing but an imitation. It was worth five hundred francs at the very most!"
