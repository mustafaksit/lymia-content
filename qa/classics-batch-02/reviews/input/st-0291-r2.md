# The Story of Doctor Dolittle

Summary: A kind country doctor learns to speak with animals and sails to Africa to help sick monkeys.

## Level A1

Doctor John Dolittle lived in Puddleby. He loved animals very much. Jip the dog lived with him. Dab-Dab the duck lived there too. So did Gub-Gub the pig.

But sick people did not like the animals. Soon they stopped coming. The doctor had very little money. His sister Sarah went away.

The doctor also had an old parrot, Polynesia. One rainy day, she spoke to him. "Animals can talk too," said Polynesia. "I can teach you our words." Soon the doctor understood every animal. So he became a doctor for animals. Animals came from far away to see him.

But animals had no money. Chee-Chee the monkey lived with him now. One winter night, news came from Africa. A swallow brought it to Chee-Chee. "The monkeys are very sick!" he cried. A sailor lent the doctor a ship. The doctor and his animals sailed to Africa.

Near Africa, the ship hit a rock. But everyone got to land safely. Then they came to a high cliff. The monkeys held hands and made a bridge! Everyone walked across it.

Many monkeys were very sick. The doctor worked day and night. At first the lion did not help. Then his own baby got sick. So the lions came and helped. After two weeks, all the monkeys were well.

The monkeys wanted to thank the doctor. "He needs money," said Chee-Chee. So they found a very strange animal. It was the Pushmi-pullyu. It had a head at each end! It was shy, but it liked the doctor.

Polynesia and Chee-Chee stayed in Africa. Back home, people paid to see the animal. Soon the doctor was rich! Then he went back to his little house. And Dab-Dab the duck was very happy.

## Level A2

Doctor John Dolittle lived in a little house in Puddleby. He was a clever doctor, and he loved animals. There were even white mice in his piano! His best friends were Jip the dog and Dab-Dab the duck. Gub-Gub the pig and Too-Too the owl lived there too.

His sister Sarah did not like the animals at all. One day an old lady sat on a sleeping hedgehog! She never came back. Soon other people stopped coming too. The doctor got poorer and poorer.

Polynesia, the doctor's parrot, was very old and very clever. One rainy afternoon, she flew down onto his table. "Animals can talk, you know," said Polynesia. "We have our own language, and I can teach you." All afternoon, she gave him bird words, and he wrote them down. Soon he could talk with every animal. So John Dolittle became an animal doctor. An old horse needed glasses, so the doctor gave him green ones. Animals came from far and wide to see him.

The doctor also took in a little monkey, Chee-Chee. Then a crocodile with a bad tooth came to live with them. People were afraid of it, and they stopped bringing their pets. "Send that crocodile away, John!" said Sarah. The doctor said no, so Sarah packed her things and left. Now the doctor was poorer than ever.

One cold winter night, Chee-Chee ran into the kitchen. "Doctor, I have news from Africa!" he cried. "A swallow brought it. The monkeys there are very sick, and they need you." A sailor lent the doctor a ship. The doctor sailed away with Polynesia, Chee-Chee and his other animals. The crocodile came too.

Near Africa, a storm came, and the ship hit a rock. Dab-Dab flew to land with a rope. She tied it to a tree. Everyone got to land safely. After many days, they came to a high cliff over a river. Quickly, the monkeys held hands and feet and made a bridge! The doctor and his animals walked across it.

Thousands of monkeys were sick. The healthy monkeys got medicine, so they did not get sick. He put the sick ones in a big house with many beds. He asked other animals for help, but the proud lion said no. Then the lion's own baby got sick. So the lion came back, and all the animals helped. After two weeks, the last monkey was well again.

The monkeys wanted to give the doctor a present. "He needs money," said Chee-Chee. "Give him a strange animal. People can pay to see it." So the monkeys found the shy Pushmi-pullyu. It had no tail and a head at each end. It liked the kind doctor, so it agreed to go.

Polynesia, Chee-Chee and the crocodile stayed in Africa, their home. Back in his own country, people paid to see the Pushmi-pullyu. Soon the doctor was rich. He went back to Puddleby and bought the sailor two new ships. Dab-Dab was very happy, because now there was always enough money.

## Level B1

Doctor John Dolittle lived in Puddleby, in a small house with a very large garden. He was a clever doctor, but he loved animals even more than people. There were rabbits in his kitchen and white mice in his piano. He loved Jip the dog, Dab-Dab the duck and Gub-Gub the pig best. He also had Too-Too the owl and a very old parrot, Polynesia.

His sister Sarah, who kept house for him, did not like the animals at all. One day an old lady sat on a hedgehog, which was asleep on the sofa. She never came back. One by one, his patients went to other doctors. Soon his money began to run out.

One afternoon, the man who sold meat for cats came to see the doctor. "You know more about animals than any vet," he said. "Why don't you become an animal doctor?" When the man left, Polynesia flew onto the table. "He is right, Doctor," she said. "And here is a secret. Animals can talk, and I can teach you their languages." All that rainy afternoon, the doctor wrote down bird words in a book. After a while, he could talk with almost every animal.

So John Dolittle became a doctor for animals. A farm horse told him that he was going blind in one eye. The doctor gave him green glasses, and soon the horse could see perfectly. Birds carried the news of him to other countries, and he became famous among animals everywhere.

He saved a little monkey, Chee-Chee, from a man who treated him badly. Then a crocodile with a toothache ran away from a circus and came to stay. Now the farmers were afraid to bring their sheep and cows. "Send that crocodile away, or I am leaving," said Sarah. The doctor said no, so Sarah packed her bags and left. Soon the doctor was poorer than ever. The animals did the housework, but there was still very little money.

One cold night in December, Chee-Chee ran into the kitchen. "Doctor, a swallow has brought me a message from Africa!" he cried. "A terrible sickness is spreading among the monkeys there, and they need you." The money box was empty, but a sailor lent the doctor a ship. Then the doctor sailed away with his animal friends and the crocodile.

For six weeks the swallow flew in front of the ship to show the way. Near Africa, a great storm came, and the ship hit a rock and broke. Dab-Dab flew to the shore with a rope and tied it to a palm tree. Everyone reached the land safely along the rope. Chee-Chee led them through the forest toward the Land of the Monkeys. At last, they came to a steep cliff above a river. In a flash, the monkeys held each other's hands and feet and made a bridge. The doctor and his animals walked safely across it.

Thousands of monkeys were ill. The doctor gave medicine to the healthy monkeys to keep them well. He put the sick ones in a big house full of beds, but he needed nurses. "I am the King of Beasts," said the proud lion. "I will not look after monkeys!" But at home, his wife told him that one of their cubs was ill. She was very angry with him and sent him straight back to the doctor. After that, all the animals of the forest came to help. In two weeks the last monkey was well, and the doctor slept for three days.

The monkeys wanted to give him a present. "He needs money for a new ship for the sailor," Chee-Chee told them. "Give him an animal which nobody in his country has ever seen." So they found the shy Pushmi-pullyu, which had a head at each end. It talked with one mouth and ate with the other. It did not want to go at first, but it trusted the kind doctor.

Chee-Chee, Polynesia and the crocodile stayed behind in Africa, their real home. When they got back, the doctor took the Pushmi-pullyu to fairs all over the country. People paid a few coins to see it, and soon he had plenty of money. He bought the sailor two new ships and returned to his little house in Puddleby. "Money is a nuisance," said the doctor, "but it is nice not to worry."

## Level B2

In the little town of Puddleby there lived a doctor called John Dolittle. People said he was a very clever man, and dogs and children followed him down the street. His house was small, but his garden was huge, with a wide lawn and willow trees. The doctor, however, loved animals far more than he loved most people. He kept goldfish in the pond, rabbits in the kitchen and white mice in his piano. His closest friends were Jip the dog, Dab-Dab the duck, Gub-Gub the baby pig and Too-Too the owl. Then there was Polynesia, a parrot who was nearly two hundred years old.

His sister Sarah, who kept house for him, complained about the animals constantly. One day an old lady sat down on a hedgehog that had been sleeping on the sofa. She never came back, and she was not the last patient to stay away. "John, how can you expect sick people to come here?" asked Sarah. "But I like the animals better than the important people," the doctor replied. As the months went by, he got more animals and fewer patients, and he had to sell his piano.

One afternoon the man who sold meat for cats and dogs came in with a stomachache. "Why don't you look after animals instead of people?" he asked. "You understand them far better than the vets do." As soon as the visitor had gone, Polynesia flew down onto the table. "That man has sense," she said. "Now listen, Doctor, because I am going to tell you a secret. Animals can talk to each other, and I can teach you their languages." All that rainy afternoon, the parrot gave him bird words to write down. She explained that animals talk with their ears, tails and noses as well as their mouths. With her help, he slowly learned to understand every word they said.

From then on, John Dolittle was an animal doctor. A farm horse explained that he was going blind in one eye and needed glasses, not pills. The doctor got him a fine pair of green spectacles, and the horse could see as well as ever. Since the animals could now tell him where it hurt, curing them was easy. Birds carried the news abroad, and he became famous among animals all over the world.

Some of his patients liked the house so much that they never left. He rescued a little monkey, Chee-Chee, from a street musician who kept him in a tight collar. Later, a crocodile with a toothache escaped from a circus and moved into the fish pond. However, farmers and old ladies were now too frightened to bring their animals. "If you don't send that creature away, I will go and get married!" said Sarah. "All right, then, go and get married," said the doctor, so Sarah packed up and left.

The doctor grew poorer than ever, but he refused to worry. The animals, however, were worried, so they shared the housework among themselves. Chee-Chee cooked, Dab-Dab dusted and Jip swept the floor with a cloth tied to his tail. Too-Too, who was good at sums, worked out that their money would last only one more week.

One freezing night in December, Chee-Chee burst into the kitchen. "Doctor, I have just had a message from my cousin in Africa!" he cried. "There is a terrible sickness among the monkeys, and they are begging you to come." The news had been carried by a swallow, which was waiting outside. The money box was empty, but a sailor whose baby the doctor had once cured agreed to lend him a ship. The grocer was persuaded to wait for his money, and the friends set sail with the crocodile.

For six weeks they followed the swallow, which carried a tiny lantern at night. Just as they came close to Africa, a storm drove the ship onto a rock. Dab-Dab flew to the beach with a rope and tied it to a palm tree. Those who could not swim climbed along the rope, and no one was hurt. Chee-Chee, who knew every path in the jungle, led them toward the Land of the Monkeys. At its edge, they found a steep cliff with a deep river below. While the doctor was looking around for boards, the monkeys made a bridge with their own bodies. Everyone crossed safely, and Chee-Chee said the doctor was the first man ever to see the Bridge of Apes.

The doctor found thousands of sick monkeys, so he separated the sick from the healthy. For three days and nights, healthy monkeys came to a little grass house to be vaccinated. The sick ones were put to bed in a large house, but there were not enough nurses. When the doctor asked the Leader of the Lions for help, the lion refused proudly. "I am the King of Beasts, and I will not wait on monkeys," he said. But at home his wife met him with bad news: one of their cubs would not eat. When she heard how rude he had been, she sent him straight back to the doctor. Soon lions, leopards, antelopes and zebras were all helping, and after two weeks the last monkey was well. The exhausted doctor went to bed and slept for three days.

When the doctor said he had to go home, the monkeys held a meeting. Chee-Chee explained that the doctor needed money, because he owed the sailor a new ship. "Give him an animal that nobody in his country has ever seen," he suggested. So they searched the jungle for the Pushmi-pullyu, which had no tail and a head at each end. At first, it refused because it was very shy and hated being looked at. After three days, it agreed to meet the doctor, and it trusted him as soon as it saw his face. It talked with one mouth and kept the other for eating, so it was never rude at meals.

Chee-Chee, Polynesia and the crocodile stayed in Africa, where they had been born. Once they had found another ship, thousands of swallows came to guide them home. Back in his own country, the doctor travelled from fair to fair with the Pushmi-pullyu. Visitors paid sixpence each to see the two-headed animal, and circus owners offered huge sums for it. "He will never be shut in a cage," said the doctor, and he always refused. Before long, he returned to Puddleby a rich man and bought the sailor two new ships. "Money is a terrible nuisance," he said, "but it is nice not to have to worry." "Yes," said Dab-Dab, "it certainly is."

## Level C1

Long ago, in the little town of Puddleby, there lived a doctor by the name of John Dolittle. Whenever he strolled down the street in his tall hat, dogs and children trotted after him. Behind his small house stretched an enormous garden. What the doctor loved best, however, was not medicine but animals. Rabbits lived in his kitchen, white mice made their home inside the piano and a hedgehog slept in the cellar. He was fondest of Jip the dog, Dab-Dab the duck, Gub-Gub the baby pig and Too-Too the owl. Then there was Polynesia, a parrot so old that she had lost count of her birthdays.

His sister Sarah, who kept house for him, grumbled endlessly that the animals made the place untidy. Her fears came true on the day an elderly lady sat down on the sofa, directly on top of the sleeping hedgehog. The lady never returned. "John, how can you expect sick people to come here when you keep all these creatures?" Sarah demanded. "But I like the animals better than the important people," replied the doctor, and that was the end of the matter. So the pets grew more numerous and the patients fewer, until he had to sell his piano and his best Sunday suit.

It was the man who sold meat for the town's cats who finally changed his life. Calling in with a stomachache, he remarked that the doctor knew far more about animals than any vet and ought to treat them instead. Polynesia, who had been singing a sailor's song in the window, fell silent and listened. No sooner had the visitor left than she flew down onto the table. "That man has sense, Doctor," she declared. "And now I shall tell you something no other person knows. Animals have languages of their own, and I would be happy to teach you." Thrilled, the doctor seized a pencil, and for the rest of that wet afternoon the parrot dictated bird words to him. Animals, she explained, spoke not only with their mouths but with their ears, tails and feet; a twitch of Jip's nose, for instance, was a question. Lesson by lesson, the doctor mastered their languages, until he could understand everything they said.

Having given up human patients altogether, John Dolittle became the finest animal doctor the world had ever known. One farm horse, delighted to meet a man who spoke his language, explained that what he needed was not more pills but spectacles. He was soon working in the fields in a splendid pair of green glasses, and before long a blind horse was unknown in the district. Migrating birds carried his fame across the seas, and in time he was better known among animals abroad than he had ever been among people at home.

Some patients loved the place so much that they refused to leave. Seeing a street musician's monkey in a collar that was far too tight, the doctor paid the man a coin and took the creature home, where he was named Chee-Chee. Later, a crocodile with a raging toothache slipped away from a visiting circus, and once his tooth had been put right, he begged to live in the fish pond. To the household he was as gentle as a kitten, but the farmers and the old ladies were not convinced, and they stopped bringing their animals. "If you don't send that creature away this minute, I shall go and get married!" cried Sarah. "All right," said the doctor mildly, "go and get married," and so she packed her things and departed.

Never had the household been so poor, yet the doctor remained perfectly calm, insisting that money was nothing but a nuisance. His animals were less calm, and one evening, as he dozed by the fire, Too-Too, who was good at arithmetic, whispered that they had enough money for exactly one more week. It was Polynesia who proposed that they take over the housework themselves. Chee-Chee, the only one with proper hands, cooked and mended, Dab-Dab dusted and made the beds, and Jip swept the floors with a cloth tied to his tail. They even sold radishes and roses at the gate, but when the snow came early that winter, many of them went to bed hungry.

On a bitter December night, the door flew open and Chee-Chee rushed in, completely out of breath. "Doctor, my cousin in Africa has sent me a message!" he gasped. "A dreadful sickness has broken out among the monkeys, and they beg you to come and stop it." The messenger turned out to be a swallow, shivering outside, who had stayed behind long after the rest of her kind had flown south. The money box, when fetched from the shelf, proved to be empty. Undismayed, the doctor went down to the coast, where a sailor whose child he had once cured agreed to lend him a ship. The grocer, too, was persuaded to supply the voyage and wait for payment. With Jip, Dab-Dab, Gub-Gub and Too-Too, and with Chee-Chee, Polynesia and the crocodile overjoyed to be going home, he set sail.

For six weeks the swallow flew ahead of them, carrying a tiny lantern by night. Within sight of the African coast, however, a storm hurled the ship against a rock. Dab-Dab flew ashore with a rope and tied it to a palm tree, and those who could not swim climbed along it to the beach. By morning the ship had been beaten to pieces, yet nobody had been hurt, and Polynesia wept for joy at the sight of her homeland. Guided by Chee-Chee, who knew every path through the jungle, they journeyed on for many days, sleeping in shelters made of palm leaves. At the very border of the Land of the Monkeys, though, the path ended at a steep cliff, with a river rushing far below. While the doctor was still searching for planks, the monkeys linked their hands and feet and hung across the gap as a living bridge. Everyone crossed safely, and Chee-Chee told the doctor proudly that no visitor from his country had ever seen the Bridge of Apes before.

The doctor found monkeys of every kind lying sick in their thousands. First he separated the sick from the healthy, and for three days and nights he vaccinated every healthy monkey that came to his little grass hut. The sick were put to bed in a long house, but there were far too few nurses, so he sent for the lions, the leopards and the antelopes. The Leader of the Lions was outraged. "Do you dare to ask me, the King of Beasts, to wait on monkeys?" he roared, before stalking off into the jungle. Waiting for him at home, however, was his wife, with the news that one of their cubs had refused all food since the night before. When she learned how rudely her husband had treated the one man who could help, she sent him hurrying back. Pretending that he had merely been passing, the lion offered his help and asked casually whether the doctor might see a cub later. With the whole jungle helping, the last patient had recovered by the end of the second week. Then the doctor fell into bed and slept for three days.

Learning that he meant to go home, the monkeys held a council under the trees. Chee-Chee explained that in the doctor's country nobody could live without money, and that the doctor owed a poor sailor a new ship. "Give him a creature that nobody there has ever seen," he urged. They chose the Pushmi-pullyu, the rarest animal in the jungle, which had no tail and a horned head at each end. Since one head was always awake, no hunter had ever crept up on one. For three days the monkeys pleaded before it agreed even to meet the doctor, for it was painfully shy and loathed being stared at. One glance at John Dolittle's kind face was enough, however, and it consented to come, provided he promised to send it home if it was unhappy.

Once a ship had been found for the voyage home, Chee-Chee, Polynesia and the crocodile chose to stay in Africa, the land of their birth. The doctor need not have worried about finding his way, for out of the moonlit sky came thousands of swallows, ready to lead him north. Before going home, he toured the country fairs with the Pushmi-pullyu, charging sixpence a look, while Dab-Dab scolded him for letting children in for nothing. Circus owners offered him fortunes for the creature, but he always refused, declaring that it would never be shut in a cage. So many visitors came that he soon returned to his little house a rich man. He bought the sailor two new ships, settled the grocer's bill and purchased another piano so that the mice could move back in. "Money is a terrible nuisance," the doctor remarked, "but it is pleasant not to worry." "Yes," agreed Dab-Dab, who was toasting muffins for his tea, "it is indeed!"
