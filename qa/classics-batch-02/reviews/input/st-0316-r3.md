# The Brave Little Tailor

Summary: A little tailor sews a proud boast on his belt and sets out to test his luck.

## Level A1

A little tailor sat by his window. The tailor put jam on his bread. Soon many flies came to the jam. He hit them with a piece of cloth. Seven flies lay still.

"Seven at one blow!" cried the tailor. He made a belt with these words. He put an old cheese in his pocket. He took a little bird too.

On a mountain, he met a giant. The giant read the belt. He thought of seven men, not seven flies. The giant pressed water out of a stone. The tailor pressed his soft cheese. More water came out! Then the giant threw a stone very high. The tailor threw his bird. It flew away and never came down.

Then the tailor came to a king. The king had a hard task for him. "Two giants live in my forest. Stop them, and you can marry my daughter."

The giants slept under a tree. The tailor climbed up into the tree. He dropped stones on them. Each giant blamed the other. They began to fight. Then they ran away to far mountains.

But the king had two more tasks. First came a unicorn with a long horn. The tailor jumped behind a tree. The horn went deep into the wood. The tailor caught it. Next came a wild pig. It ran after him into a small church. The tailor jumped out of a window. Then he shut the door.

So the tailor married the princess. Now he was a king. One night, he talked in his sleep. "Boy, fix the coat!" he said. "He is only a tailor!" said the queen. Her father made a plan with his men. They had to take him away at night. But a young servant warned the tailor.

That night, the men came to the door. The tailor called out loudly. "I hit seven at one blow! Why must I fear these men?" The men ran away in fear. And the tailor was king all his life.

## Level A2

A little tailor sat by his window. A farm woman came down the street with jam. The tailor bought a little and spread it on some bread. Soon lots of flies came. "Go away!" cried the tailor. He took a piece of cloth and hit them with one blow. When he counted, seven flies lay still on the table.

"Seven at one blow! The whole world must hear about this!" said the tailor. He made himself a belt and sewed the words on it. Then he put an old cheese in his pocket. He caught a little bird too, and off he went.

On top of a high mountain sat a huge giant. "You are only a tiny little man!" laughed the giant. The tailor opened his coat and showed him the belt. The giant read the words and thought of seven men. He pressed a stone until a little water came out. The tailor pressed his soft cheese, and much more water came out. Next the giant threw a stone high into the sky. The tailor threw his bird, and it never came back.

That night, the giant took the tailor to his cave. Other giants sat there by the fire. The bed was too big, so the tailor slept in a corner. At midnight, the giant hit the bed with a heavy stick. But the bed was empty. In the morning, the giants saw the tailor in the forest. They were very afraid and ran away fast.

Later the tailor fell asleep in a king's garden. People read his belt and told the king about him. The king was afraid of this hero. So he gave the tailor a hard task. "Two wild giants live in my forest. Stop them, and you can marry my daughter," said the king.

The tailor found the giants asleep under a tree. He climbed the tree and dropped stones on them. "Why did you hit me?" cried the first giant. "I didn't hit you!" said the second giant. Soon they were very angry, and they began to fight. At last, they ran away to faraway mountains.

But the king had two more tasks. First a unicorn ran at the tailor with its long horn. He jumped behind a tree, and the horn stuck in the wood. Then a wild pig ran after him into a small church. He jumped out through a window and shut the door.

So the tailor married the princess and became a king. But one night, the young queen heard him in his sleep. "Boy, finish the jacket and fix the trousers!" he said. Now she knew the truth about her husband. Her father wanted his servants to take the tailor away at night. But a young servant heard the plan and told the tailor.

That night, the tailor did not really sleep. "I hit seven at one blow and beat two giants! Why must I fear the men at my door?" he called out. The servants heard him and ran away in fear. And the tailor stayed a king all his life.

## Level B1

One sunny morning, a little tailor was sitting by the window, sewing happily. A farm woman came down the street, calling out that she had good jam to sell. The tailor bought a small spoonful and spread it on a slice of bread. While he was finishing a jacket, the sweet smell brought a crowd of flies. "Who invited you to my table?" cried the tailor. He took a piece of cloth and struck them with one blow. When he counted, there were seven flies lying still in front of him.

"Seven at one blow! The whole world must hear about this," said the tailor. He made himself a belt and stitched the words on it in big letters. He put an old cheese in his pocket and set off. Near the door, he caught a bird in a bush and took it along too.

At the top of a high mountain sat a huge giant. The giant laughed and called the tailor a tiny little man. Then the tailor opened his coat and showed him the belt. The giant read the words and thought they meant seven men, not seven flies. He squeezed a stone until a few drops of water ran out. The tailor took the soft cheese from his pocket and squeezed out much more. Next, the giant threw a stone so high that the eye could hardly follow it. "But your stone came back down, and mine will never return," said the tailor. He threw the bird into the air, and it flew away for good.

Then the giant asked him to help carry a great oak tree. "You take the trunk, and I will carry the branches," said the tailor. The giant lifted the trunk onto his shoulder, but the tailor simply sat among the branches. The giant could not look back, so he carried the tree and the tailor as well. At last, he was too tired to go on. The tailor jumped down quickly and put his arms around the branches. "Such a big fellow, and you cannot even carry a tree!" he said.

That night, the giant took the tailor to his cave. Other giants were sitting there by the fire. The bed was far too big, so the tailor slept in a corner. At midnight the giant brought a heavy iron bar down on the bed. But the next morning, the tailor came walking through the forest, cheerful as ever. The giants were so afraid that they ran away as fast as they could.

The tailor traveled on and fell asleep in the garden of a king's palace. People read his belt and told the king. The king invited him to join his army, but the soldiers were afraid of him. "He can strike seven of us at one blow. We will all leave!" the soldiers told the king. The king was afraid of the tailor too, so he thought of a clever plan. "Two giants are causing great trouble in my forest," he told the tailor. The man who stopped them would get the king's daughter and half his kingdom.

The two giants were sleeping under a tree in the forest. The tailor filled his pockets with stones and climbed up into the tree. He dropped one stone after another on the first giant. "Why are you hitting me?" the first giant asked his companion. "You are dreaming," said the second giant, and they both went back to sleep. Then the tailor dropped a stone on the second giant, and he woke up angry. They argued until they were furious, and at last they began to fight. In the end, both of them ran away to the faraway mountains.

But the king did not keep his promise and sent him after a wild unicorn. When the unicorn ran at him, the tailor jumped behind a tree at the last moment. Its horn stuck fast in the wood, and the tailor put a rope around its neck. Next the king sent him after a wild boar. The tailor ran into a small chapel, and the boar followed him. He jumped out of a high window at the back, ran round and shut the door.

So the tailor married the princess and became a king himself. Some time later, the young queen heard her husband talking in his sleep. "Boy, finish that jacket and mend those trousers!" he said. She told her father, and he planned to send the tailor away on a ship. But a young servant who liked the tailor heard everything and warned him.

That night, the tailor only pretended to sleep. When the servants came to the door, he called out loudly. "I struck seven at one blow, beat two giants, and caught a unicorn and a boar. Why should I be afraid of the men outside my door?" The servants were so frightened that they ran off, and nobody ever tried again. So the tailor remained a king all his life.

## Level B2

One summer morning, a little tailor was sitting cross-legged on his table by the window, sewing in the best of moods. Down the street came a farm woman, calling out that she had good jam for sale. The tailor called her up, but he bought only a small spoonful, and she went away grumbling. "This will give me strength," he said, spreading the jam on a thick slice of bread. But first he wanted to finish his jacket.

Meanwhile, the sweet smell drifted through the room, and the flies soon noticed it. "Who invited you?" cried the tailor, waving them away, but they kept coming back in greater numbers. At last, he lost his temper, seized a piece of cloth and struck them with all his might. When he lifted the cloth, no fewer than seven flies lay still in front of him.

"What a fellow I am! The whole world should hear of it!" cried the tailor. He quickly made himself a belt and stitched on it in large letters: "Seven at one blow." He slipped an old cheese into his pocket, and a bird caught in a bush by the door went in beside it.

The road led up a high mountain, and on the very top sat a huge giant. The giant looked at the tailor with scorn and called him a miserable little creature. In reply, the tailor unbuttoned his coat and showed him the belt. The giant read the words and imagined seven men, not seven flies, but he still wanted to test the stranger. He took a stone and squeezed it so hard that a few drops of water came out. "That is child's play," said the tailor, and he squeezed the soft cheese until the liquid ran out. Then the giant threw a stone almost out of sight. "Not bad, but your stone fell back to earth," said the tailor. He threw the bird into the air, and it flew away and never came back.

Next the giant asked for help in carrying a huge oak tree that had been cut down. "You take the trunk, and I will carry the heavy branches," the tailor suggested. So the giant lifted the trunk onto his shoulder, while the tailor sat comfortably among the branches. Because the giant could not turn his head, he carried the whole tree and the tailor as well. At last, he could go no further. The tailor jumped down at once, put his arms around the branches and pretended he had carried them all along.

Later the giant pulled down the top of a cherry tree so that the tailor could eat the ripest fruit. But the tailor was too light to hold it, and when the giant let go, the tree threw him into the air. He landed unhurt on the other side. "Weren't you strong enough to hold that little tree?" asked the giant. "Of course I was, but I jumped over it on purpose," replied the tailor. The giant tried to do the same, but he got stuck in the branches.

That evening the giant took the tailor to his cave, where other giants were sitting by the fire. The bed he was given was so large that he crept into a corner instead. At midnight the giant brought a heavy iron bar down on the bed, sure he had seen the last of his guest. The next morning, however, the tailor came strolling through the forest, as bold and cheerful as ever. The giants were so frightened that they ran away at once.

The tailor wandered on until he came to the garden of a royal palace, where he fell asleep in the grass. People read the words on his belt, and soon the king heard about the mighty warrior. He was given a place in the army, but the soldiers were alarmed. "If we ever quarrel with him, he will strike seven of us at every blow," they complained. Since the king was afraid of the tailor himself, he decided to get rid of him by a trick.

He sent word that two giants were causing great harm in his forest. If the tailor defeated them, he would receive the king's daughter and half the kingdom as well. "That is just the right job for a man like me," said the tailor, and he set off with a hundred riders. At the edge of the forest, he told the riders to wait.

He found the giants asleep under a tree, snoring so loudly that the branches shook. He filled his pockets with stones, climbed into the tree and began dropping them, one by one, onto the first giant. At last the giant woke up and angrily asked his companion why he was hitting him. "You must be dreaming," said the second giant, and they lay down again. Then the tailor dropped a stone on the second giant, who woke up in a rage and blamed the first. They quarreled until they started such a wild fight that the ground shook. In the end, both ran off to the distant mountains. The tailor climbed down and calmly told the riders that the work was done.

The king, however, regretted his promise and demanded that the tailor first catch a wild unicorn. When the unicorn came charging at him, he stood still in front of a tree and jumped aside at the last moment. The animal ran into the trunk so hard that its horn was stuck fast. The tailor put a rope around its neck, freed the horn and led the unicorn to the king. Next he was sent after a wild boar. When it rushed at him, he ran into a small chapel with the boar close behind. He leapt out through a high window at the back, ran round and shut the door.

Now the king had to keep his word, and the little tailor became a king himself. Some time later, the young queen heard her husband talking in his sleep. "Boy, finish that jacket and mend those trousers, or you will be sorry!" he muttered. Realizing that he was only a tailor, she complained to her father the next morning. The old king planned to have the tailor tied up in the night and carried onto a ship. But the king's young squire, who was fond of the tailor, overheard the plan and warned him.

That night the tailor only pretended to fall asleep. As soon as he heard the servants at the door, he began to speak in a loud voice. "Boy, sew that jacket quickly! I struck seven at one blow, defeated two giants, and caught a unicorn and a wild boar. Why should I fear the men outside my door?" The servants fled in terror, and no one ever dared to trouble the tailor again. So he remained a king for the rest of his life.

## Level C1

On a bright summer morning, a little tailor sat cross-legged on his table beside the window, stitching away in the best of spirits. Down the street came a farm woman, calling out that she had good jam to sell, and the words sounded so sweet that the tailor leaned out and called her up. Having bought a single small spoonful, to the woman's great annoyance, the tailor spread it over a thick slice of bread, which he set aside until he had finished his jacket.

The sweet smell, however, reached the flies on the wall, which came down in a crowd and settled greedily on the bread. "And who invited you, may I ask?" cried the tailor, waving them away, but the flies simply returned in ever greater numbers. At last his patience ran out; he snatched up a piece of cloth from the table and brought it down on them with all his might. When he lifted the cloth and counted, he found no fewer than seven flies lying motionless before him.

"What a remarkable fellow I am! The whole town ought to hear about this, and why stop there, when the whole world deserves to know?" cried the tailor. Without wasting a moment, he made himself a belt and stitched across it, in large letters, the proud words "Seven at one blow." All he found to take along was an old cheese, which he slipped into his pocket, and a bird caught in a bush outside the door soon joined it.

The road climbed a high mountain, and on its summit sat an enormous giant. The giant looked down at him with contempt and called him a miserable little creature. By way of reply, the tailor unbuttoned his coat and displayed the belt, and the giant, reading the words, naturally supposed that seven men had fallen to that single blow. Impressed as he was, he still meant to test the stranger, so he picked up a stone and squeezed it until a few drops of water trickled out. "Child's play," said the tailor, who drew out the soft cheese and squeezed it until the juice ran. Scarcely believing his eyes, the giant flung a stone so high that it could barely be seen, yet in the end it fell to earth, as stones must. Then the tailor tossed his bird upward, and it flew off rejoicing in its freedom and never came back.

Next the giant led him to a mighty oak that lay felled on the ground and asked for help in carrying it out of the forest. "Gladly," came the answer, "you shoulder the trunk, and I shall take the branches, which are surely the heavier part." So the giant heaved the trunk onto his shoulder while the tailor settled among the branches, and since the giant could not turn round, he carried the tailor too. Before long the giant could go no further and called out that he must let the tree drop. Quick as a squirrel, the tailor sprang down and threw his arms around the branches, remarking that a fellow of such size ought to manage a simple tree.

On they went until they came to a cherry tree, whose top the giant bent down so that his guest might pick the ripest fruit. The tailor, however, was far too light to hold it, and when the giant let go, the tree sprang back and tossed him over to the other side, quite unhurt. "What, are you too weak to hold such a little sapling?" asked the giant. "Weakness has nothing to do with it; I jumped over on purpose, and I should like to see you do the same," replied the tailor. The giant made the attempt, but he got caught in its branches, and once again the little man had the better of him.

That evening the giant brought the tailor to his cave, where several other giants were sitting by a roaring fire. Shown to a bed of enormous size, the tailor found it far too vast for comfort and crept into a corner instead. At midnight the giant rose, took up a heavy iron bar and brought it down upon the bed, quite sure that he had rid himself of the troublesome stranger. The next morning, when the giants went out into the forest, there came the tailor strolling towards them, as bold and cheerful as ever. Convinced that nothing could stand against such a creature, they fled in terror and were soon out of sight.

The tailor wandered on until he came to the garden of a royal palace, where he lay down in the grass and fell asleep. Curious people gathered round to read his belt, and before long word of the mighty warrior reached the king himself. The king offered the tailor a place in his army, but the soldiers were far from pleased with their new companion. "If we ever quarrel with him, he will strike seven of us at every blow," they complained, and they threatened to leave the king's service altogether. The king, unwilling to lose his whole army and secretly afraid of the tailor himself, began to look for a way to be rid of him.

At length, he hit upon a plan and sent word that two giants were doing great harm in one of his forests, robbing travelers and frightening everyone nearby. Whoever overcame them, the message said, would receive the king's only daughter as his wife and half the kingdom besides, and a hundred riders would go along. "A princess and half a kingdom are not offered every day," said the tailor to himself, and he accepted at once. At the edge of the forest he told his escort to wait and went on alone.

Before long he discovered the two giants asleep under a tree, snoring so loudly that the branches shook. Climbing into the tree with his pockets full of stones, he dropped them one by one onto the first giant, who at last woke and shoved his companion. "You are dreaming; I have not touched you," grumbled the second giant, and they both lay down to sleep again. Then the tailor chose his largest stone and let it fall on the second giant, who sprang up roaring and accused the first of the very thing he had just denied. Their quarrel grew louder until they flew at each other and fought so wildly that the earth trembled. In the end, sick of each other, both giants fled to the far mountains, never to be seen in that forest again. The tailor climbed down at his leisure and informed the riders, with perfect composure, that the work was done.

The king, however, was in no hurry to keep his promise and announced that the hero must first capture a wild unicorn that roamed the forest. "One unicorn is less trouble than two giants," said the tailor, and taking a rope and an axe, he went off into the woods. When the unicorn came charging, the tailor stood before a tree until the last moment, then slipped behind it, and the beast struck the trunk so hard that its horn stuck fast. Then he put the rope around the unicorn's neck, freed the horn from the tree with his axe and led the animal, quite tame now, to the king.

Still the king would not hand over his daughter, but demanded a third service: the capture of a wild boar, with the royal hunters to help. The tailor, however, sent the hunters away, for he wanted no help. The moment the creature saw him, it rushed at him, but the nimble little man darted into a small chapel and leapt straight out through a high window. The boar thundered in after him, and the tailor ran round and shut the door, so that the furious animal, far too heavy to jump through the window, was caught.

Now the king had to keep his word, and the wedding was celebrated with great splendor but rather little joy, and so a king was made out of a tailor. Some time later, the young queen heard her husband talking in his sleep: "Boy, finish that jacket and mend those trousers, or there will be trouble!" In that instant she understood where the young gentleman came from, and the next morning she begged her father to free her from such a husband. The king told her to leave the bedroom door open, for his servants would bind the tailor while he slept and carry him onto a ship bound for a distant shore. The king's young squire, who had grown fond of the tailor, overheard every word and hurried to warn his master.

"I shall put a stop to that," said the tailor, and that night he went to bed at the usual hour. When the queen believed him to be asleep, she rose quietly and opened the door, but the tailor, who was only pretending, began to speak in a loud, clear voice. "Boy, sew that jacket and patch those trousers, or I shall come after you! I struck seven at one blow, defeated two giants, brought in a unicorn and caught a wild boar, so why should I fear the people outside my door?" Hearing this, the servants were seized with such terror that they fled as though a whole army were at their heels, and not one of them ever dared to try again. And so the little tailor remained a king, as long as he lived.
