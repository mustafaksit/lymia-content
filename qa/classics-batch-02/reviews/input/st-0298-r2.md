# Pride and Prejudice

Summary: A clever young woman meets a proud rich man, and neither of them likes the other.

## Level A1

Mr and Mrs Bennet had five daughters. The family lived at Longbourn. Jane, the oldest, was very kind. Elizabeth was clever and liked to laugh. Mrs Bennet wanted rich husbands for her girls.

A rich young man came to Netherfield. His name was Bingley. His friend Darcy came with him. Darcy was very rich, but very proud.

There was a big dance in Meryton. Bingley danced with Jane two times. He liked her very much. Darcy did not want to dance. "Elizabeth is not pretty enough," said Darcy. Elizabeth heard him and laughed. "What a proud man!" she said to Jane.

Then Elizabeth met Wickham, a young soldier. He was friendly and handsome. "Darcy was very unkind to me," said Wickham. Elizabeth believed him. Soon Bingley went away to London. Jane was very sad.

In spring, Elizabeth visited her friend Charlotte. Darcy was there too, at his aunt's house. One evening, Darcy asked her to marry him. But he spoke badly about her family. "No!" said Elizabeth. "You are proud and unkind."

The next day, Darcy gave her a letter. In it, he told the truth. Wickham was not a good man. He wanted Darcy's young sister for her money. Elizabeth felt bad. "I was wrong about Darcy," she said.

In summer, Elizabeth went on a trip. Her aunt and uncle went with her. They visited Pemberley, Darcy's beautiful home. Darcy was there, and he was very kind. Then bad news came from Jane. Lydia, the youngest sister, ran away with Wickham. Her father did not know about it.

Darcy found them in London. He paid Wickham, and Wickham married Lydia. Darcy told nobody about it. But Lydia talked, and Elizabeth learned the truth.

Bingley came back and married Jane. Then Darcy asked Elizabeth again. This time, she said yes. They lived happily at Pemberley.

## Level A2

Mr and Mrs Bennet lived at Longbourn with their five daughters. Jane, the oldest, was beautiful and kind. Elizabeth, the second, was clever and full of fun. Mary, Kitty and Lydia were younger. Mrs Bennet wanted rich husbands for all of them. One autumn, a rich young man came to live at Netherfield. His name was Bingley, and he was friendly to everyone. His friend Darcy was even richer, but cold and proud.

Soon there was a ball in Meryton. Bingley danced with Jane twice and could not stop smiling. "Go and dance with Elizabeth," Bingley told Darcy. "She is not pretty enough for me," answered Darcy. Elizabeth heard every word. Later she laughed about it with her friends.

A few weeks later, Jane visited Netherfield and became ill. Elizabeth walked across muddy fields to look after her. Bingley's sisters laughed at her dirty dress. But Darcy looked at her bright eyes and said nothing. He began to like her, and this surprised him.

Then a cousin, Mr Collins, came to Longbourn. He was a silly man, and he wanted a wife. He asked Elizabeth to marry him, but she said no. Three days later, he asked her friend Charlotte. Charlotte said yes because she wanted a home.

Then Elizabeth met Wickham, a young officer. "Darcy's father was good to me," Wickham told her. "He wanted to give me a good job. But Darcy said no and kept it from me." Elizabeth believed him and disliked Darcy even more. In winter, Bingley went to London and did not come back. Jane was very sad.

In spring, Elizabeth visited Charlotte and Mr Collins in their new home. Nearby lived Lady Catherine de Bourgh, Darcy's rich and proud aunt. Darcy came to stay with her. One evening, he came to see Elizabeth alone. "I love you, and I want to marry you," said Darcy. "But your family is not good enough for me." "No," said Elizabeth angrily. "You took Bingley away from my sister. And you were cruel to Wickham. I can never marry you."

The next morning, Darcy gave her a long letter. In his eyes, Jane did not really love Bingley. Then he told the truth about Wickham. Wickham took money instead of the job and spent it all. Later he tried to marry Darcy's young sister for her money. "I was blind," thought Elizabeth.

In summer, Elizabeth went to Derbyshire with her aunt and uncle Gardiner. They visited Pemberley, Darcy's beautiful house. Suddenly Darcy came home, and he was very kind. Then bad news came from Jane. Lydia ran away with Wickham without her father's permission. Elizabeth told Darcy, and he left quickly.

A few weeks later, Lydia and Wickham married in London. "Darcy came to my wedding!" she said. Elizabeth's aunt wrote and told her everything. Darcy found the pair and paid Wickham's debts. He did it for Elizabeth, but he told no one.

In autumn, Bingley came back and asked Jane to marry him. Then Lady Catherine arrived at Longbourn, very angry. "Never marry my nephew!" cried Lady Catherine. "I promise you nothing," said Elizabeth. Soon Darcy and Elizabeth went for a walk together. She thanked him for his help with Lydia. "My feelings are the same," said Darcy. This time, Elizabeth smiled and said yes.

## Level B1

Mr and Mrs Bennet lived at Longbourn, a comfortable country house, with their five daughters. Jane, the oldest, was gentle and always saw the best in people. Elizabeth, the second, was clever and honest, and she loved to laugh. Mary, Kitty and Lydia were younger. Mrs Bennet had one goal in life: rich husbands for all her girls. One autumn, a rich young man rented Netherfield, a large house nearby. His name was Bingley, and he was cheerful and friendly to everybody. He brought his sisters and his close friend Darcy, who was even richer.

The two friends came to a ball in the town of Meryton. Bingley danced with Jane twice, and everyone could see his admiration. Darcy, on the other hand, danced only with Bingley's sisters and spoke to nobody else. "Why don't you dance with Elizabeth Bennet?" Bingley asked him. "She is pretty enough, but not pretty enough for me," Darcy replied coldly. Elizabeth heard every word. She soon turned it into a funny story for her friends.

Some time later, Jane rode to Netherfield in the rain and became ill there. Elizabeth walked three miles across wet, muddy fields to look after her sister. Darcy, however, was thinking about her bright, intelligent eyes. Slowly, and against his wishes, he was beginning to admire her.

Soon afterward, a cousin, Mr Collins, arrived at Longbourn. Mr Bennet had no son, so one day Longbourn would belong to Mr Collins. He was a foolish man who talked endlessly about his rich friend, Lady Catherine de Bourgh. He asked Elizabeth to marry him, and she refused him firmly. Only three days later, Elizabeth's friend Charlotte accepted him because she wanted a safe home.

Around this time, Elizabeth met Wickham, a charming young officer who was staying in Meryton. He told her a sad story about Darcy. "Old Mr Darcy promised me a good position in the church," he said. "But when the old man died, his son gave it to someone else." Elizabeth believed every word, and her dislike of Darcy grew stronger. Then, in the winter, Bingley left Netherfield suddenly and went to London. Jane said little, but her heart was broken.

In the spring, Elizabeth visited Charlotte and Mr Collins at their new home. Lady Catherine, who was Darcy's aunt, lived nearby in a grand house. Soon Darcy arrived to visit her, together with his cousin Fitzwilliam. One day Fitzwilliam told Elizabeth that Darcy saved his friend Bingley from an unwise marriage. Elizabeth understood at once that the lady was Jane.

That same evening, Darcy came to see Elizabeth alone. "I can't fight my feelings any longer," he said. "I love you, and I want you to be my wife." Yet he also said that her family was far beneath his own. Elizabeth's face grew hot with anger. "You ruined my sister's happiness, and you treated Wickham cruelly," she said. "You are proud and selfish, and I could never marry you." Darcy turned pale and left without another word.

The next morning, Darcy handed her a letter and walked away. He truly believed that Jane did not love Bingley. Then he told the truth about Wickham. Wickham asked for money instead of the church position and quickly spent it all. Later he tried to marry Darcy's young sister in secret, because he wanted her money. Elizabeth read the letter many times. "I was so proud of my good judgment, but I was blind," she thought.

In the summer, Elizabeth traveled to Derbyshire with her aunt and uncle Gardiner. They visited Pemberley because Darcy was away from home. The housekeeper told them that he was the kindest master in the world. Then Darcy suddenly appeared, and to Elizabeth's surprise he was gentle and polite to everyone. He even introduced his shy young sister to her. But a few days later, a letter came from Jane with terrible news. Lydia ran away with Wickham without her family's permission. Elizabeth told Darcy everything, and he left at once.

Mr Bennet and Mr Gardiner searched London, and soon a letter arrived with better news. Lydia and Wickham were going to marry. After the wedding, Lydia came home and talked carelessly about it. "Darcy was at my wedding, but it is a secret!" she said. Elizabeth wrote to her aunt and learned the whole story. Darcy found the couple, paid Wickham's debts and arranged the marriage, all for Elizabeth.

In the autumn, Bingley returned to Netherfield and asked Jane to marry him. Soon after, Lady Catherine arrived at Longbourn in a fury. "Promise me that you will never accept my nephew!" she demanded. "I will promise nothing," Elizabeth replied calmly. When Darcy heard about this, he began to hope again. He walked with Elizabeth along a quiet lane, and she thanked him for helping Lydia. "My feelings are still the same," said Darcy. This time, Elizabeth gladly said yes.

## Level B2

Mr and Mrs Bennet lived at Longbourn, a comfortable country house not far from the small town of Meryton. They had five daughters and no son, which meant that the house would one day pass to a cousin. Jane, the eldest, was beautiful and gentle, and she could never think badly of anyone. Elizabeth, the second, was quick, honest and witty, and she was her father's favorite. Mary, Kitty and Lydia were younger, and Lydia in particular was loud, careless and spoiled. Mrs Bennet had only one serious aim in life, and that was to see her daughters married to rich men.

So when a wealthy young gentleman named Bingley rented Netherfield, she could talk of nothing else. Bingley arrived with his two sisters and his closest friend, Darcy, who was even richer than he was. Darcy owned a famous estate in Derbyshire called Pemberley, and everyone said he had ten thousand a year. At the first ball in Meryton, however, people quickly decided that he was the proudest man in England.

Bingley danced every dance, and he danced twice with Jane, which everyone noticed. Darcy, on the other hand, walked about the room in silence and refused to be introduced to anyone. Bingley urged him to dance with Elizabeth. Darcy glanced at her and said she was acceptable, but not pretty enough to interest him. Elizabeth, who was sitting close by, heard the whole remark. Rather than feeling hurt, she repeated it to her friends as an excellent joke against him.

A few weeks later, Jane was invited to Netherfield and caught a heavy cold after riding there in the rain. Elizabeth, worried about her sister, walked three miles across the wet fields and arrived with her skirt covered in mud. Bingley's sisters were shocked and mocked her as soon as she left the room. Darcy, though, could only think how her walk had brightened her fine dark eyes. To his own surprise, he found himself watching her more and more closely.

Soon afterward, the cousin who would inherit Longbourn arrived for a visit. Mr Collins was a clergyman, a pompous and foolish man who spoke constantly of his noble patron, Lady Catherine de Bourgh. He had come to choose a wife among the Bennet girls, and he chose Elizabeth. She turned him down, and nothing he said could change her mind. Only three days later he proposed to her friend Charlotte, who accepted him because she wanted a secure home.

Around the same time, a group of soldiers came to stay in Meryton. Among their officers was a handsome young man called Wickham. He was charming and easy to like, and he told Elizabeth that he had known Darcy since childhood. "Old Mr Darcy meant to give me a good position in the church," he said sadly. "But after his death, his son refused to keep the promise, out of pure jealousy." Elizabeth believed him completely, and her dislike of Darcy hardened into contempt. At the beginning of winter, the whole Netherfield party suddenly left for London. Bingley did not return. Jane hid her pain bravely, but Elizabeth could see how deeply she had been hurt.

In the spring, Elizabeth went to visit Charlotte in her new home near Lady Catherine's grand house. Lady Catherine turned out to be Darcy's aunt, and before long Darcy arrived with his cousin, Colonel Fitzwilliam. The colonel was pleasant company, and one day he mentioned that Darcy had recently saved a friend from a most unwise marriage. Elizabeth understood immediately that the friend was Bingley and the lady was her own sister.

That very evening, Darcy called on her while she was alone. After a few nervous moments, he told her that he loved her and could not hide his feelings any longer. Yet even as he proposed, he spoke at length about her family's low position. Elizabeth's anger burst out. She accused him of destroying her sister's happiness and of treating Wickham shamefully. "You were the last man I could ever have married," she told him. Darcy, white with anger, wished her well and left the house.

The next morning he met her on her walk, handed her a long letter and went away. In it, he admitted that he had separated Bingley from Jane. He had honestly believed that Jane did not care for his friend. The truth about Wickham was very different from the story she had heard. Wickham had asked for money instead of the church position, had spent it all, and had later asked for more. When this was refused, he had tried to persuade Darcy's young sister to run away and marry him, mainly for her fortune. Elizabeth read the letter again and again until she saw, with deep shame, how badly she had judged both men.

That summer, Elizabeth traveled north with her aunt and uncle Gardiner. They decided to see Pemberley while its owner was away. The house was beautiful, and the housekeeper praised her master as the kindest and most generous of men. Then, quite unexpectedly, Darcy himself appeared on the lawn. He was polite and gentle, and he spoke warmly to her aunt and uncle. He even asked to introduce his shy sister to Elizabeth. She began to wonder whether his feelings for her might still be alive. But a few days later, two letters arrived from Jane with alarming news. Lydia had run away with Wickham without her parents' permission. Nobody knew whether he intended to marry her.

Elizabeth told Darcy everything, and he soon took his leave with a serious look. She returned home, convinced that she would never see him again. Mr Bennet and Mr Gardiner went to London to search for the couple. Before long, word came that the two would be married. After the wedding, Lydia visited Longbourn and carelessly mentioned that Darcy had been present. Elizabeth wrote to her aunt and finally learned the truth. Darcy had found the pair, paid Wickham's debts and arranged the marriage. He had asked that no one should ever be told, because he wanted no thanks for what he had done.

In the autumn, Bingley came back to Netherfield, and he soon asked Jane to be his wife. Shortly afterward, Lady Catherine arrived at Longbourn without warning. She had heard a story that Elizabeth would marry Darcy, and she demanded a promise that she never would. Elizabeth calmly refused to promise anything. When Darcy heard of this conversation, it gave him hope. A few days later, walking alone with Elizabeth, he told her that his feelings had not changed. This time she answered that hers had changed completely, and she accepted him with joy.

## Level C1

At Longbourn, a comfortable but unremarkable estate a short walk from the market town of Meryton, Mr and Mrs Bennet had raised five daughters and no son. Because of an old legal arrangement, the property could not pass to any of the girls, and on Mr Bennet's death it would go to a distant cousin. This fact gave Mrs Bennet's life its single purpose, which was to marry her daughters, as quickly and as profitably as possible, to men of fortune. Her husband, a dry and clever man who preferred his library to his family, watched her efforts with a kind of tired amusement. Of the five girls, Jane was the loveliest and the most gentle, and Elizabeth the sharpest and the most lively. Mary, Kitty and Lydia, in their different ways, gave their father little reason for pride.

The arrival of a rich young bachelor named Bingley at Netherfield, the largest house in the district, therefore struck Mrs Bennet as a gift from heaven. Bingley was open, warm and easily pleased, and at the first public ball in Meryton he charmed everyone and danced twice with Jane. His friend Darcy, who was said to possess a magnificent estate in Derbyshire called Pemberley, made a very different impression. He danced only with Bingley's sisters, spoke to no one else, and stood about the room with an air of cold superiority. When Bingley pressed him to ask Elizabeth to dance, Darcy remarked, within her hearing, that she was passable enough, but hardly handsome enough to tempt him. Elizabeth, far from being hurt, found the remark so absurd that she repeated it with great delight to anyone who would listen.

The two families met often over the following weeks, and on one occasion Jane, sent to Netherfield on horseback through heavy rain by her scheming mother, fell ill and had to stay. Elizabeth set off on foot to nurse her, crossing three miles of wet fields and arriving with her stockings dirty and her face glowing from the exercise. Bingley's sisters mocked her appearance the moment she left the room, but Darcy, who had begun to notice the unusual intelligence of her dark eyes, would not join them. In the long evenings that followed, he and Elizabeth argued about books, character and pride, and each conversation left him more interested and more uneasy.

Into this situation stepped Mr Collins, the cousin who would one day inherit Longbourn. He was a clergyman of remarkable self-importance, whose conversation consisted chiefly of praise for his noble patron, Lady Catherine de Bourgh. Having decided, as an act of generosity, to choose a wife from among his cousins, he settled on Elizabeth and proposed in a speech of extraordinary length. Her refusal puzzled him deeply, since he could not imagine that any sensible young woman might really mean no. Within three days, however, he had transferred his attentions to Elizabeth's friend Charlotte, who accepted him with open eyes, preferring a secure home to the uncertain hope of love.

Meanwhile, a regiment had arrived in Meryton for the winter, and among its officers was a young man named Wickham, whose easy manners won Elizabeth's liking almost at once. Wickham confided that he had grown up at Pemberley as the son of old Mr Darcy's steward, and that the old gentleman had intended a comfortable position in the church for him. "His son disliked me from boyhood," he said with a look of gentle regret, "and when the time came, he simply gave the place to someone else." Elizabeth, already prejudiced against Darcy, accepted every word without question. Soon afterward the Netherfield party left suddenly for London, and a letter from Bingley's sister made it plain that they did not intend to return. Jane bore the disappointment with quiet dignity, but Elizabeth suspected that the sisters, and perhaps Darcy, had worked to separate her from Bingley.

In the spring, Elizabeth traveled to visit Charlotte in her new home, which lay beside the great park of Lady Catherine de Bourgh. Her ladyship proved to be a domineering woman who offered advice on every subject, and she was also, as it turned out, Darcy's aunt. Darcy soon arrived on a visit, accompanied by his cousin Colonel Fitzwilliam, an agreeable man who talked to Elizabeth with easy friendliness. During one of their walks, the colonel mentioned, without realizing its significance, that Darcy had lately congratulated himself on rescuing Bingley from a most imprudent marriage. Elizabeth went back to the house so distressed that she excused herself from dinner that evening.

It was while she was sitting alone that Darcy unexpectedly called and, after pacing the room in agitation, declared that he loved her passionately and could no longer struggle against it. What might have been a moving confession was spoiled by his frankness, for he dwelt at length on the inferiority of her connections and on everything his pride had had to overcome. Elizabeth's reply was as cold as it was swift. She told him that his conduct toward Jane was unforgivable and that his treatment of Wickham was shameful. His manners, she added, had shown her from the very first how arrogant and selfish he was. "You could not have offered me your hand in any way that would have tempted me to accept it," she said. Darcy, pale with anger, begged her pardon for having taken up her time and left her.

Early the next morning he met her in the park, placed a long letter in her hand and walked away without waiting for a reply. As to Jane, he wrote, he had observed her closely and had honestly believed that her heart was untouched, and he had therefore advised his friend against the match. As to Wickham, the account was very different from the one she had heard. Wickham had declined the church position in exchange for a large sum of money, had wasted it, and had then returned to demand the position after all. When Darcy refused, Wickham had tried to persuade his young sister to run away and marry him, chiefly, it seemed, for the sake of her considerable fortune. Elizabeth read the letter many times, first with disbelief and then with growing shame, until she was forced to admit that vanity, not reason, had guided her judgment of both men.

In July, she set out on a holiday with her aunt and uncle Gardiner, a sensible and well-bred couple from London, and their journey brought them into Derbyshire. Assured that the family was away, they agreed to visit Pemberley, and Elizabeth found a house of great beauty and quiet good taste. The old housekeeper, who had known Darcy since he was a child, spoke of him as the best of masters and the most generous of brothers. Then, as they crossed the lawn, Darcy himself appeared, having returned a day earlier than expected. Instead of the cold reserve she remembered, he showed a warmth and courtesy that astonished her, talking pleasantly with her aunt and uncle and asking permission to introduce his shy young sister. For several days Elizabeth could hardly decide what she felt, except that it was no longer anything like dislike.

That fragile happiness was broken by two letters from Jane. Lydia, who had been staying at a seaside town with a friend, had run off with Wickham without her family's consent. It was by no means certain that he meant to marry her. When Darcy found Elizabeth in tears, she told him everything, and he listened gravely and soon took his leave. She hurried home with her aunt and uncle, believing that whatever regard he had felt for her could not survive such a family disgrace.

Mr Bennet and Mr Gardiner searched London without success, until a letter from Mr Gardiner announced, to everyone's relief, that the couple had been found and the wedding arranged. When the newly married Lydia came to Longbourn, quite unashamed, she let slip that Darcy had attended her wedding, and then remembered that it was meant to be a secret. Elizabeth wrote at once to her aunt, whose reply revealed the whole story. It was Darcy who had traced the couple, paid Wickham's debts, and persuaded him to marry Lydia, asking only that his part should never be made known.

In the autumn Bingley returned to Netherfield, and within a few weeks he and Jane were engaged, to the noisy joy of Mrs Bennet. Shortly afterward, Lady Catherine swept into Longbourn, having heard a report that her nephew intended to marry Elizabeth. She demanded a promise that Elizabeth would never accept him, and Elizabeth, with perfect calm, declined to give one. It was this refusal, reported indignantly to Darcy by his aunt, that taught him to hope. Not long afterward, walking alone with Elizabeth, he told her that his wishes were unchanged. She thanked him at last for what he had done for Lydia, and admitted that her own feelings had changed entirely. Mr Bennet, who had believed his favorite daughter hated the man, needed some persuading, but in the end he gave his blessing, and Elizabeth went to live at Pemberley as Darcy's wife.
