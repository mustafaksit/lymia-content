# Captains Courageous

Summary: A spoiled rich boy falls from a great ship and must work all summer on a fishing schooner.

## Level A1

Harvey was fifteen and very rich. His father had many trains and ships. But Harvey was rude and lazy. Nobody liked him very much.

In May, he sailed on a big ship. The sea was rough, and he felt sick. He went to the side for some air. A big wave came. Harvey fell into the cold sea!

A fisherman found him in the water. His name was Manuel. Manuel pulled Harvey into his small boat. Then he took him to a fishing schooner. Its name was the We're Here. The captain was Disko Troop.

"My father is very rich," said Harvey. "Take me home now!" he shouted. Disko shook his head. "We fish here until September. You can work for us." Disko did not believe Harvey's story. Harvey was angry, and he cried.

Dan was the captain's son. He was Harvey's age, and he was kind. "Come and work with me," said Dan. So Harvey said sorry to Disko. Then he started to work.

He cleaned fish from morning to night. Long Jack showed him the ropes. Dan taught him to row a small boat. His hands hurt, but he did not stop. Slowly, Harvey became strong and kind. Manuel, Penn and Salters liked him now.

In September, the boat was full of cod. They sailed home to Gloucester. Harvey sent a message to his parents. All summer, they were very sad. Now they came on a fast train.

His mother held him and cried. His father looked at his strong son. "You are a man now," said his father. Disko laughed at his own mistake. "So your story was true!" said Disko. Harvey's father gave Dan work on his ships. And Harvey went to college.

## Level A2

Harvey Cheyne was fifteen, and his father was very rich. He owned trains, ships and mines. Harvey always got everything he wanted. He was rude to people, and nobody liked him much.

In May, Harvey crossed the ocean on a big passenger ship. He talked loudly and showed off his money. One afternoon the sea was rough, and he felt very sick. He went to the side of the ship for some fresh air. The ship rolled, and Harvey fell into the cold sea.

Later Harvey opened his eyes. He lay on a pile of fish in a small boat. A fisherman with a big smile sat beside him. His name was Manuel. He found Harvey in the waves and pulled him out. Then he rowed back to his schooner, the We're Here.

The captain of the schooner was Disko Troop. He was a quiet man with sharp eyes. "Take me home at once! My father can pay you anything," said Harvey. Disko shook his head. "We fish here on the Grand Banks until September. Then we go home to Gloucester." Then Harvey put his hand in his pocket. His money was gone! "Someone on this boat took my money!" he shouted.

Disko looked at him calmly. "You came here with no money, boy. The cold sea hurt your head a little, I think." He did not believe a word of Harvey's story. He offered Harvey a job as the ship's boy. Harvey refused in a rude voice, and the captain's face grew hard. So Harvey sat alone on the deck and cried.

Dan was the captain's son, and he was Harvey's age. He came and sat next to him. "Dad is almost never wrong about people," said Dan. Then he smiled. "But I believe you a little. Come and work with me." Harvey thought about it for a long time. Then he went to Disko and said sorry.

Life on the schooner was hard. Harvey worked from early morning until late at night. He cleaned the cod and packed them in salt. Long Jack showed him every rope on the ship. Dan taught him to row a small boat. He also showed him how to catch fish on a line. His hands hurt, and his back ached, but he did not stop.

Slowly, Harvey changed. He became strong, brown and polite. He liked Manuel's songs and the quiet talk of Penn and Salters. Disko taught him about the sea, the weather and the stars. For the first time in his life, Harvey felt useful.

By September the schooner was full of salted cod. The We're Here sailed home to Gloucester. There, Harvey sent a telegram to his parents. All summer they had no news of their son. They were full of sorrow. Now they crossed the whole country on a fast train.

At Gloucester, Harvey's mother held him and cried. His father looked at his son's hard hands and clear eyes. Disko heard the whole story and laughed at himself. "I was wrong about your head, boy," said Disko. Harvey's father gave Dan a job on one of his ships. And Harvey went to college to learn his father's business.

## Level B1

Harvey Cheyne was fifteen, and his father was one of the richest men in the country. Mr. Cheyne owned railways, ships, mines and forests. Harvey had plenty of money and nobody who ever said no to him. As a result, he was proud, rude and very sure of himself.

In May, he was crossing the ocean on a great passenger ship. He spent his days showing off his money to the other passengers. One afternoon the sea was rough, and Harvey began to feel terribly sick. He went out to the rail for some fresh air. At that moment the ship rolled heavily, and he fell over the side into the sea.

When Harvey woke up, he was lying on a heap of fish in a small boat. A fisherman with a wide smile was rowing hard through the waves. His name was Manuel, and he was the one who saved Harvey. Soon they reached a fishing schooner on the Grand Banks. Its name was the We're Here.

Captain Disko Troop listened to the boy's story without a word. "My father is a very rich man," said Harvey. "Take me back to land now, and he will pay you well," Harvey went on. Disko shook his head. "We are fishing here until September, and then we will go home to Gloucester. I cannot lose a whole season for one boy." Then Harvey put his hand in his pocket and found nothing. "My money is gone, and somebody here took it!" he cried.

The captain's eyes grew cold. "You had no money when you came aboard," said Disko quietly. In his opinion, the fall hurt the boy's head and filled it with strange stories. He offered Harvey a job as ship's boy for ten and a half dollars a month. Harvey refused, called him a thief, and ended up alone on the deck in tears.

There the captain's son found him. Dan was the same age as Harvey, with a friendly face and quick hands. "Dad doesn't make many mistakes about people," said Dan. He sat down and smiled. "But I half believe your story. Take the job, and stay with me." Harvey thought about this for a long time. Then he went to Disko and told him he was sorry.

The work was harder than anything Harvey knew. He cleaned cod for hours and packed the fish in salt down in the dark hold. Long Jack, a big man with a loud laugh, taught him the name of every rope. Manuel showed him how to row a dory, the small boat which the men fished from. Dan and Harvey fished side by side, and their lines were often heavy with cod. At night Harvey's hands burned, and he fell asleep the moment he lay down.

The other men were kind to him in their own way. Salters, the captain's brother, loved to argue about farming. Penn was a gentle, quiet man who was always close to Salters. Manuel sang songs in his own language and played a small guitar. Disko himself taught Harvey to read the sea, the weather and the stars.

Once a thick fog came down over the Banks, and the men could see nothing. Suddenly a huge passenger ship came out of the fog and passed very close. Then it was gone again, and the sea was quiet. Harvey thought of his own great ship and felt glad of the small, safe schooner.

Week after week, Harvey grew stronger and browner. He stopped boasting and learned to listen. He did his share of the work without complaint, and the men began to trust him. For the first time in his life, he was earning his own money.

By September the hold was full, and the We're Here sailed proudly home to Gloucester. As soon as they landed, Harvey sent a telegram to his parents. All summer they were sure their son was gone forever. Now they crossed the whole country in their own private railway car. Harvey's mother held him and cried, and his father hardly recognized the tall, polite young man.

Disko could not believe his eyes. "I was wrong about your head, Harvey," the captain said with a slow smile. He paid Harvey every dollar of his wages. Harvey was prouder of that money than of all his father's gold. Mr. Cheyne thanked the crew and offered Dan a place on one of his own ships. Harvey decided to go to college and then work in his father's business.

## Level B2

Harvey Cheyne was fifteen, and he had never been told no in his life. His father was one of the richest men in the country, the owner of railways, ships, mines and forests. Harvey had more pocket money than most grown men earned in a year, and he made sure everyone knew it. He was clever enough, but he was spoiled, lazy and terribly rude.

In May, he was crossing the ocean on a great passenger ship and boasting to anyone who would listen. The other passengers found him tiring, and most of them avoided him. One gray afternoon the sea grew rough, and Harvey, who was not used to the motion, felt dreadfully sick. He staggered out to the rail to breathe some fresh air. Just then the ship rolled deeply, and before he could hold on, he was thrown over the side into the sea.

When Harvey came to, he was lying on a heap of fish in the bottom of a small boat. A dark-eyed fisherman was rowing steadily and singing to himself. His name was Manuel, and he had seen the boy floating in the waves and pulled him in just in time. Before long they reached a fishing schooner called the We're Here, which was spending the summer on the Grand Banks.

The captain, Disko Troop, was a calm, heavy man who said little and noticed everything. Harvey explained, in his most important voice, who his father was. "Take me back to land at once," he demanded, "and my father will pay you ten times what your fish are worth." Disko simply shook his head. "We fish here until September, son, and then we go home to Gloucester. I'm not losing a whole season's catch for one passenger." Harvey reached for the roll of money he always carried, but his pocket was empty. "Someone on this boat has stolen my money!" he shouted.

The captain's face darkened. "You had nothing when Manuel brought you aboard," said Disko. He had heard enough to make up his mind: the fall had clearly upset the boy's head, and he was imagining things. Still, he offered Harvey a job as ship's boy at ten and a half dollars a month, which was a fair wage. Harvey refused, called the captain a thief, and soon found himself alone on the deck, shaking with anger and shame.

Dan, the captain's son, came and sat beside him. He was the same age as Harvey, with a cheerful face and a fisherman's quick hands. "Dad's hardly ever wrong about people," said Dan, "but this time I'm not so sure." He asked about Harvey's home, and the answers about private railway cars seemed too strange to be invented. "Take the job," Dan advised. After a long, silent struggle with his pride, Harvey went back to Disko and apologized.

The work was harder than anything Harvey had imagined. From dawn until late at night he cleaned cod, carried them below and packed them in salt in the hold. Long Jack, a big, loud man, taught him the name and purpose of every rope on the ship. Manuel showed him how to handle a dory, one of the small boats from which the men did their fishing. Dan and Harvey fished side by side, and when Harvey pulled up his first cod by himself, the whole crew cheered. His hands blistered, and his back ached, but each night he slept more deeply than he ever had at home.

The crew of the We're Here were a varied group. Salters, the captain's brother, was a farmer at heart and argued about crops and soil with anyone. Penn, his close friend, was a gentle, quiet man who rarely spoke. Manuel sang songs from his own country and played a little guitar on calm evenings. Disko, who understood the sea better than anyone, began to teach Harvey to read the weather, the currents and the stars.

Life on the Banks was not always peaceful. One day a thick fog rolled in, and the crew took turns blowing a horn so that other ships would hear them. Out of the gray wall a huge passenger liner appeared, passed so close that they saw its lit windows, and vanished. Later in the season a fierce storm struck, and for two days every man worked without rest to keep the schooner safe. When it was over, Harvey realized that he had not once thought about himself.

As the weeks passed, the spoiled boy disappeared. Harvey became brown, strong and quick, and he learned to keep quiet and listen to men who knew more than he did. He did his share without being asked, and the crew began to treat him as one of them. For the first time, he understood what it meant to earn a dollar.

In September the hold was finally full, and the We're Here raced proudly home to Gloucester. The moment they landed, Harvey sent a telegram to his parents. They had believed all summer that the sea had taken their son, and his mother had almost lost her health from grief. Mr. Cheyne had his private railway car rushed across the whole country, and it made the journey in less than four days.

The meeting in Gloucester was full of tears and laughter. Harvey's mother would hardly let go of him, and his father kept staring at the tall, steady, polite young man. Disko, meanwhile, had to admit his mistake. "I was wrong about your head, Harvey," he said, "and I'm not often wrong." He paid Harvey his wages to the last cent, and Harvey valued that small sum more than anything he had ever owned.

During the following days, Mr. Cheyne told his son the story of his own hard youth, when he had started with nothing. Harvey listened carefully and decided to go to college for four years before joining his father's business. Mr. Cheyne liked Dan and offered him a place on one of his ships, with the chance to become an officer. In this way, the summer on the We're Here changed both boys' lives.

## Level C1

Harvey Cheyne was fifteen years old and had never, as far as anyone could remember, been refused anything. His father was one of the wealthiest men in the country, with railways, timber, mines and a fleet of ships to his name. His mother, a nervous and loving woman, gave the boy whatever he asked for. The result was a youth with a great deal of pocket money, very little to do, and an unshakable belief that the world existed to admire him.

That May he was crossing the ocean on a great passenger liner, and he passed the hours lecturing the other passengers on the size of his father's fortune. Most of them listened politely for a minute and then found somewhere else to be. One gray afternoon, when the swell had grown heavy, Harvey began to feel unwell, and pride prevented him from admitting it to anyone. He made his way unsteadily to the rail, hoping the wind would clear his head. At that very moment the liner rolled deeply, his hands slipped, and he went over the side and into the cold gray water.

He came to his senses lying on a slippery heap of fish in the bottom of a small open boat. A fisherman with dark, merry eyes was rowing steadily and humming a tune in a language Harvey did not know. This was Manuel, who had seen something floating among the waves, rowed over out of curiosity, and hauled the soaked, exhausted boy aboard just in time. Within the hour they had reached a trim fishing schooner, the We're Here, which was working the Grand Banks for the summer.

The captain, Disko Troop, was a broad, slow-speaking man whose quiet manner concealed a mind that missed nothing at sea. Harvey, still dripping, announced who his father was and instructed the captain to turn round and take him to land at once. "My father will pay you more than your whole cargo is worth," he added grandly. Disko considered this for a while, then shook his head. "We fish here till September, and then we go home to Gloucester. I'm not throwing away a season's work for one passenger, however rich his folks may be." It was at this point that Harvey felt for the thick roll of banknotes he always carried, and discovered that it was gone. "One of your men has robbed me!" he cried.

The captain's expression hardened. "There was no money on you when Manuel brought you aboard," said Disko evenly. Privately he had already reached a conclusion: the boy's fall had disturbed his mind, and the tales of private railway cars and endless money were simply the wanderings of a shaken head. Nevertheless, being a practical man, he offered Harvey work as ship's boy at ten and a half dollars a month. Harvey's reply was so insulting that Disko's patience ran out, and the conversation ended abruptly, leaving the boy alone on the deck, furious and close to tears.

It was Dan, the captain's son, who came to find him. He was Harvey's age, sunburned and good-natured, and he had the quick, sure hands of someone born on a fishing boat. "Dad doesn't make many mistakes about folks," Dan remarked, "but I'll own I'm not certain about this one." He questioned Harvey about his home, and the replies, too odd and too detailed to have been invented, gradually persuaded him that the stranger might be telling the truth. "Either way, you'd better take the job," he said. Harvey swallowed what remained of his pride, went aft to the captain, and apologized.

What followed was the hardest work of Harvey's life. From before dawn until long after dark he cleaned cod, passed them down into the hold, and helped pack them in layers of salt. Long Jack, a huge man with a voice like a foghorn, drilled him in the name and purpose of every rope until he could find each one in the dark. Manuel taught him to handle a dory, the light flat-bottomed boat from which the men fished with long lines. The first time Harvey brought up a large cod entirely on his own, the crew greeted it with a cheer that pleased him more than any compliment he had ever received. His palms blistered and hardened, his shoulders ached, and every night he fell into a sleep deeper than any he had known on land.

The We're Here carried a crew as varied as its catch. Salters, the captain's brother, regarded himself as a farmer who had strayed onto the water by accident, and he would debate soil and seed with anyone patient enough to listen. Penn, his inseparable companion, was a mild, absent-minded man whose gentle temper seemed to soften the whole ship. Manuel, on calm evenings, sang the songs of his own country and accompanied himself on a little guitar. Disko, meanwhile, discovered in Harvey a pupil worth teaching and began, without much ceremony, to show him how a sailor reads the weather, the tides and the stars.

The Banks were not always kind. On one morning a fog settled so thick that the men could barely see the length of the deck, and they took turns sounding a horn to warn other vessels. Out of that gray blindness a great liner suddenly loomed, so close that they glimpsed lit windows and passengers at dinner, and then it was swallowed again, leaving only its wake. Harvey, staring after it, could not help reflecting that he had been one of those careless people only a few weeks before. Later, a gale kept the schooner fighting the wind for two days, and every man aboard, Harvey included, worked until he could hardly stand.

By midsummer the transformation was plain to everyone. The pale, boastful boy had become lean, brown and quiet, a lad who listened before he spoke and did his share without being told. The crew teased him less and trusted him more, and Harvey found, somewhat to his surprise, that their good opinion mattered to him a great deal. He was, for the first time, earning his keep, and he had begun to understand what lay behind each dollar he had once spent so carelessly.

In September, with her hold packed to the hatches with salted fish, the We're Here raced homeward and came proudly into Gloucester. Harvey went straight to the telegraph office and sent a message to his parents. They had spent the summer in grief, convinced that the ocean had claimed their son, and his mother had very nearly broken under the strain. Mr. Cheyne, on reading the telegram, had his private railway car hurried across the entire continent, and it reached the coast in less than four days.

The reunion was a blur of tears and laughter. Harvey's mother clung to him as if he might vanish, while his father studied, with quiet astonishment, the steady young man before him. Disko, for his part, had to swallow a little pride of his own. "I was mistaken about your head, Harvey," he admitted, "and I don't make that kind of mistake often." He then paid the boy his wages to the last cent, and Harvey treasured those few hard-earned dollars more than all the money in his father's banks.

In the quiet days that followed, Mr. Cheyne told his son, for the first time, how he had begun life with nothing and built his fortune through years of hard work. Harvey, who would once have yawned at such a story, listened with complete attention, and resolved to spend four years at college before joining his father in the business. Mr. Cheyne, having taken a strong liking to Dan, offered him a place on one of his own ships, with the chance of rising to become an officer. So two boys who had met on a fishing deck, one by accident and one by birth, set out on roads that their summer together had made possible.
