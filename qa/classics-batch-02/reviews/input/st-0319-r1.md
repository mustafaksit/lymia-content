# The Little Match-Seller

Summary: On a freezing New Year's Eve, a poor girl lights her matches and sees wonderful things.

## Level A1

It was New Year's Eve. The night was dark and very cold. A little girl walked alone in the snow. She had no hat and no shoes. Her small feet were red with cold.

The girl was poor and hungry. She had a lot of matches. But nobody bought a match all day. She was afraid to go home. Her father wanted money, and she had none.

The girl sat down between two houses. Her hands were very cold. "One match can warm my hands," she thought. She lit a match.

Suddenly she saw a big iron stove. It was hot and lovely. She put out her feet to warm them. Then the flame went out. The stove disappeared.

She lit a second match. Now she could see through the wall. On a big table was a roast goose. The goose jumped down and walked to her! Then the match went out. She saw only the cold, dark wall.

She lit a third match. Now she sat under a beautiful Christmas tree. A thousand candles shone on its branches. The match went out. The candles went up and became stars. One star fell across the sky. The girl thought of her grandmother. Her grandmother loved her very much. But she was in heaven now.

She lit a fourth match. And there was her grandmother! Grandmother was bright, kind and smiling. The girl cried, "Grandmother, take me with you! Please do not go away!" She lit all her matches very fast. Grandmother took the little girl in her arms. Together they flew up high. They went to a place with no cold. There was no hunger and no fear there.

In the morning, people found the girl. She sat between the houses with her matches. Her cheeks were red, and she smiled. "She wanted to get warm," they said. But nobody knew her secret. She was with her grandmother now.

## Level A2

It was the last evening of the year, New Year's Eve. Snow fell, and the night was dark and very cold. A poor little girl walked alone through the streets. She had nothing on her head and nothing on her feet.

When she left home, she had a pair of slippers. They were her mother's old slippers, and they were too big. Two carriages raced toward her, and she ran. The slippers fell off her feet. She never found one of them. A boy picked up the other and ran away with it. Now her small feet were red and blue with cold.

She carried a lot of matches in her old apron. Nobody bought any matches from her all day.

Lights shone from every window, and the street smelled of roast goose. "It is New Year's Eve," she thought. She sat down in a corner between two houses. She did not dare to go home. She sold nothing, and she feared her father's anger. Her home was cold too, and the wind came in.

Her little hands were stiff and cold. "One match can warm my fingers," she thought. She took a match and struck it against the wall. Suddenly she seemed to sit in front of a big iron stove. She put out her feet to warm them too. But the little flame went out, and the stove disappeared. She had only a burnt match in her hand.

She struck a second match against the wall. In its light, the wall became as thin as a curtain. She saw a big table and a hot roast goose. Then the goose jumped down and walked toward her! The match went out, and she saw only the cold wall.

She lit a third match. Now she sat under a wonderful Christmas tree. Thousands of candles shone on its green branches. The girl lifted both hands toward them, and the match went out. The candles rose higher and higher and became stars. One star fell and made a long line of fire. "A soul is on its way to heaven," the girl said softly. Her old grandmother taught her this about falling stars. Grandmother loved her more than anyone, but she was in heaven now.

She struck another match, and the light shone all around her. There in the light stood her old grandmother. The girl cried, "Grandmother, please take me with you! You go away when the match goes out!" Then she quickly lit all the matches in her hand. She wanted to keep her grandmother there. They shone brighter than the day.

Grandmother took the little girl in her arms. They flew up together in light and joy, high above the world. Up there, there was no cold, no hunger and no fear.

In the cold morning, people found the little girl in the corner. Her cheeks were red, and there was a smile on her face. Burnt matches lay all around her. "She wanted to warm herself," the people said. Nobody knew her secret: she was with her grandmother now.

## Level B1

It was terribly cold on the last evening of the year, New Year's Eve. Snow was falling, and darkness was coming down over the city. Through the cold and the dark walked a poor little girl. She had nothing on her head and nothing on her feet.

When she left home that morning, she was wearing a pair of slippers. They were very large, because they belonged to her mother. As she hurried across the street, two carriages came racing past, and she lost them both. She could not find one of them anywhere. A boy grabbed the other one and ran off with it, laughing. So now she walked on with bare feet that were red with cold.

She carried a lot of matches in her old apron. All day long nobody bought a single match from her. Nobody gave her even a penny. Hungry and shaking with cold, she walked on slowly. Snowflakes settled on her long fair hair, but she did not care about that.

Light was shining from every window, and the whole street smelled of roast goose. She remembered that it was New Year's Eve. In a corner between two houses, she sat down and pulled her little feet under her. But she only grew colder and colder. She did not dare to go home. She was bringing home no money at all, and she was afraid of her father's anger. They lived under a roof full of cracks, and the wind blew in through them.

Her small hands were stiff with cold. "A single match will warm my fingers a little," she thought. She pulled one out and struck it against the wall, and it burst into flame. It gave a warm, bright light, like a small candle. It seemed to the little girl that she was sitting by a big iron stove. The fire burned so warmly and so happily inside it! She stretched out her feet to warm them as well. But then the flame went out, and the stove disappeared. She found herself sitting in the snow with a burnt match in her hand.

She struck another match, and it burned brightly. In its light, the wall became as thin as a veil. She could look straight through it into a room. There stood a table with a shining white cloth and fine china on it. In the middle, a roast goose full of apples and plums was steaming. The goose jumped down from its dish and walked across the floor toward her. At that moment the match went out, and she saw only the thick, cold wall again.

She lit a third match, and suddenly she was sitting under the most beautiful Christmas tree. Last Christmas she looked through a rich merchant's glass door at his tree. This tree was even bigger and more beautiful. Thousands of candles were burning on its green branches. Bright pictures, like the ones in the shop windows, looked down at her. The little girl lifted both hands toward them, and then the match went out.

The candles of the Christmas tree rose higher and higher. Soon she saw that they were the bright stars in the sky. One of them fell and left a long line of fire behind it. "Now someone is going up to heaven," said the little girl. Her old grandmother used to tell her this about falling stars. Grandmother was the only person who truly loved her, and she was in heaven now.

The girl struck another match against the wall, and a soft light shone all around her. In the light stood her old grandmother, gentle and full of love. The little girl cried out, "Oh, Grandmother, please take me with you! I know you will disappear when the match goes out. You will go away like the warm stove, the lovely goose and the big Christmas tree!" Quickly she lit the rest of her matches to keep her grandmother with her. They shone so brightly that the night became brighter than day. Grandmother looked more beautiful and taller than ever before. She took the little girl in her arms. Together they flew up in light and joy, higher and higher. Up there, there was no cold, no hunger and no fear.

On the first morning of the new year, people found the little girl in her corner. Her cheeks were rosy, and there was a smile on her lips. Around her lay the burnt matches. "She wanted to warm herself," the people said quietly. Nobody knew about the beautiful things that she saw in the light of her matches. Nobody knew how happily she went with her grandmother into a bright new year.

## Level B2

It was bitterly cold on New Year's Eve, the very last evening of the year. Snow was falling thickly, and darkness was already settling over the town. Through the cold and the gloom walked a poor little girl, bareheaded and barefoot.

It was true that she had been wearing slippers when she left home, but they had not been much use to her. They were enormous old slippers that had belonged to her mother, so large that they kept sliding off her small feet. As she hurried across the street to get out of the way of two carriages rushing by, she lost them both. One of them was never found. A boy snatched up the other and ran off with it, delighted with his prize. So the little girl walked on with her bare feet, which were red and blue with cold.

She carried a heap of matches in an old apron and held one bundle in her hand. Nobody had bought anything from her the whole day long, and nobody had given her a single penny. Hungry and shivering, she crept along the streets, a sad little picture of poverty. The snowflakes settled on her long fair hair, which curled prettily about her neck, but she gave no thought to that.

Lights were shining from every window, and the delicious smell of roast goose drifted out into the street. After all, it was New Year's Eve, and she could think of nothing else. She found a corner between two houses, where one of them stood a little further out than the other. There she sat down and drew her little feet up under her, but she only felt colder and colder. She did not dare to go home, for she had not sold a single match or earned any money. If she came back with empty hands, she knew her father would be angry with her. Besides, it was almost as cold at home as it was out here. They lived right under the roof, and the wind whistled in through the cracks. The largest holes had been stuffed with straw and rags, but that did little good.

Her little hands were almost numb with cold. A single match might help, if only she dared to take one from the bundle. At last, she drew one out and struck it against the wall. How it crackled and blazed! It burned with a warm, clear flame like a tiny candle, and she cupped her hand around it. It was a strange and wonderful light. It seemed to the little girl that she was sitting before a great iron stove with shiny brass knobs. The fire inside burned so cheerfully and gave out such lovely warmth! She stretched out her feet to warm them too, but in that instant the flame went out. The stove vanished, and she was left sitting in the snow with the burnt end of a match in her hand.

She struck a second match, and it flared up brightly. Wherever its light fell, the wall became as thin as a veil. She could see straight through it into a warm room inside the house. A table stood there, laid with a snow-white cloth and fine china. In the middle, a roast goose stuffed with apples and plums was steaming, and it smelled wonderful. Then, to her amazement, the goose jumped down from the dish and came waddling across the floor toward the poor girl. At that very moment the match went out, and nothing was left but the thick, cold wall.

She lit a third match, and at once she found herself sitting under the most beautiful Christmas tree. Last Christmas she had peeped through the glass door of a rich merchant's house at his tree. But this one was even taller, and it was far more splendidly decorated. Thousands of candles were burning on its green branches. Bright pictures, like the ones in the shop windows, looked down at her. The little girl reached up with both hands, and the match went out.

The Christmas candles rose higher and higher, until she saw that they had become the bright stars in the sky. One of them fell, leaving a long trail of fire behind it across the dark sky. "Now someone is going home to heaven," said the little girl softly. Her old grandmother had once told her that a falling star meant a soul was going up to heaven. That grandmother had been the only person in the world who truly loved her, and she was in heaven now.

Once more the girl struck a match against the wall, and a soft glow spread all around her. In the middle of that glow stood her old grandmother, shining and gentle and full of love. The little girl cried out, "Grandmother, please take me with you! I know you will disappear as soon as the match burns out. You will vanish just like the warm stove, the roast goose and the beautiful Christmas tree!" Then she hurried to strike every match left in the bundle, because she longed to keep her grandmother near. The matches blazed with a light that was brighter than the middle of the day. Never before had her grandmother looked so tall or so beautiful. She lifted the little girl into her arms, and together they flew up in light and joy, high above the earth. Where they went, there was no cold, no hunger and no fear.

On the first morning of the new year, people found the little girl in her corner between the houses. Her cheeks were rosy, and there was a gentle smile on her lips. Around her lay the burnt matches, and one whole bundle had burned down to the end. "She was trying to warm herself," people said, shaking their heads sadly. Nobody knew what beautiful things she had seen in the light of her matches. And nobody knew how gloriously she had gone with her old grandmother into the happiness of the new year.

## Level C1

It was bitterly cold on the last evening of the old year, and the snow was falling steadily as darkness closed in over the town. This was New Year's Eve, a night for warm rooms and full tables, yet the streets were not empty of those who had neither. Through the cold and the gathering dark went a poor little girl, with nothing to cover her head and nothing at all on her feet.

She had, it is true, been wearing slippers when she left home, but what use were they? They were great clumsy things that had once belonged to her mother, so large that they slipped from her feet at every other step. Hurrying across the street as two carriages came rattling past at a terrible speed, the girl lost them both. One was nowhere to be seen, and a boy pounced on the other and ran off with it. He shouted over his shoulder that it would make a splendid cradle when he had children of his own. And so the child walked on, her small bare feet turning red and blue with the cold.

In an old apron she carried a great many matches, and a single bundle of them she held in her hand, ready to offer to anyone who passed. But the whole long day nobody had bought so much as one match from her, and nobody had pressed even a penny into her palm. People hurried by with their collars turned up and their arms full of parcels, and not one of them looked down at her. Hungry and shivering, she crept from one street to the next, the very picture of a poor child with nowhere to go. Large snowflakes settled on her long fair hair, which curled softly around her neck, yet her looks were the last thing on her mind.

Lights glowed in every window, and the rich smell of roast goose drifted out into the street. This, after all, was New Year's Eve, and she could think of nothing else. In a corner formed by two houses, one of which jutted a little further into the street than the other, she sank down, drawing her feet up beneath her. Yet the cold only crept deeper into her, and she did not dare to go home. She had sold no matches, she had earned not a single penny, and she knew all too well that her father would be angry with her. Besides, home was hardly any warmer than the street. The family lived right under the roof, and the wind whistled in through every crack, even though the largest ones had been stuffed with straw and old rags.

Her little hands were nearly numb with cold by now. Surely one match would do some good, she thought, if only she dared to draw it from the bundle, strike it against the wall and warm her fingers over it. For a long while she hesitated, her fingers hovering over the bundle, because each match she burned was one she could no longer sell. She drew one out, and with a sharp scrape against the bricks it burst into flame. How warmly it flared, and how brightly it burned! It gave a clear, warm light, like a tiny candle, as she held her hand curved around it, and a very strange light it turned out to be. It seemed to the little girl that she was sitting before a great iron stove with polished brass knobs, in which a fire burned cheerfully. The warmth it gave out was so comforting that she forgot, for a moment, where she was. She had just stretched out her feet to warm them too when the flame went out. The stove vanished, and she was left in the snow with nothing but the burnt stump of a match between her fingers.

She struck a second match, and where its glow fell upon the wall, the stones turned as thin and clear as a veil, so that she could see into the room beyond. There stood a table spread with a dazzling white cloth and fine porcelain, and in the middle sat a roast goose, stuffed with apples and plums and steaming most deliciously. The sight alone made her feel her hunger more sharply than ever. Then came the most astonishing thing of all: the goose hopped down from its dish and came waddling across the floor toward the poor girl. But at that very moment the match went out, and nothing remained before her except the thick, cold, unfriendly wall.

She lit a third match, and at once she was sitting beneath the loveliest Christmas tree she had ever seen. It was even taller and more richly decorated than the one she had glimpsed last Christmas through the glass door of a wealthy merchant's house. Thousands of candles were burning on its green branches, and brightly colored pictures, like those she had admired in the shop windows, gazed down at her. For a moment she simply sat and stared, hardly daring to breathe in case the whole marvelous sight should disappear. The little girl stretched both hands up toward all that splendor, and then the match went out.

The Christmas candles rose higher and higher, and soon she understood that they were nothing but the clear stars of the winter sky. One of them fell, drawing a long streak of fire behind it across the darkness. "Now someone is going up to heaven," the little girl whispered. Her old grandmother had once told her that whenever a star fell, a soul was rising up to heaven. That grandmother had been the only person who ever showed her real kindness, and now she, too, had left this cold world behind. Since then, nobody had spoken a single tender word to the child.

Once again she struck a match against the wall, and this time a soft glow spread all around her. In the midst of it stood her old grandmother, radiant and gentle, looking at her with more love than ever. The little girl cried out, "Grandmother, oh, take me with you! I know you will disappear the moment the match burns out, just as the warm stove vanished, and the delicious goose, and the great, glorious Christmas tree!" In great haste she struck every match that remained in the bundle, desperate to keep her grandmother there for just a little longer. The matches blazed with such brilliance that the corner grew brighter than broad daylight. Never had her grandmother looked so tall or so beautiful. She gathered the little girl into her arms, and together they rose in brightness and joy, higher and higher above the sleeping town. Where they were going, there was no cold, no hunger and no fear; there was only peace.

The first sun of the new year rose pale and cold over the town, and its light crept slowly into the corner between the two houses. There the little girl was found, still sitting where she had sunk down the evening before. Her cheeks were rosy and there was a smile on her lips, as though she had drifted off in the middle of a happy dream. Around her lay the burnt-out matches, one whole bundle of them used up. "She was only trying to warm herself," said the people who stopped to look, and they shook their heads. Not one of them could guess what beautiful things she had seen in the light of those matches. Nor did anyone know how brightly she had passed, with her grandmother beside her, into the happiness of a new year.
