# Toomai of the Elephants

Summary: A young boy who loves an old working elephant hopes to see something no hunter has ever seen.

## Level A1

Kala Nag was a very old elephant. He worked for the government of India. His rider was a man called Big Toomai. Big Toomai had a son. The boy was ten. Everyone called him Little Toomai. Little Toomai loved the old elephant.

The men went to the Garo hills. They wanted to catch wild elephants. Petersen Sahib was the boss of the hunt. Machua Appa was his best hunter. They drove the wild elephants into a pen.

Little Toomai sat on Kala Nag's back. He watched the hunters in the pen. One hunter dropped his rope. Little Toomai jumped down and ran in. He threw the rope back to the hunter. Kala Nag quickly lifted the boy out. "Never do that again!" said Big Toomai.

Next day, Petersen Sahib smiled at the boy. "You are brave. You can be a hunter one day. But first you must see the elephants dance." All the hunters laughed at this. Nobody ever saw the elephants dance.

That night, Little Toomai slept near Kala Nag. Late at night, he woke up. Kala Nag pulled up his post. He lifted the boy onto his back. Then he walked quietly into the forest.

They crossed a river and climbed a hill. At the top was a wide, open place. Many wild elephants stood there. Then they all began to stamp their feet. The sound was like a big drum. The ground shook. The elephants danced all night long. Little Toomai held on and watched.

In the morning, they went back to camp. Little Toomai was tired and wet. He told Petersen Sahib about the dance. Then he fell into a deep sleep. Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa climbed the hill. They found flat, hard ground. "The boy is right," said Machua Appa.

That night, the camp had a big party. Machua Appa stood up and spoke. "This boy is Toomai of the Elephants!" All the elephants lifted their trunks. They called out for Little Toomai.

Kala Nag was an elephant, and he was nearly seventy years old. He worked hard for the government of India. He carried heavy loads and moved big logs of wood. His name meant Black Snake.

His rider was Big Toomai. Before that, Big Toomai's father rode Kala Nag. Big Toomai had a son of ten, Little Toomai. The boy loved Kala Nag, and the old elephant loved him too. Kala Nag often lifted the boy up with his trunk.

That year, the men went to the Garo hills. They came to catch wild elephants. Petersen Sahib led the hunt. Machua Appa was his best hunter. The men drove the wild elephants into a big wooden pen. Then tame elephants and their riders went in with ropes.

One night, Little Toomai sat on Kala Nag's back near the pen. Inside the pen, a hunter dropped the end of his rope. Quickly, the boy jumped down and ran between the elephants' big feet. He picked up the rope and threw it back to the hunter. Then Kala Nag lifted him out with his trunk. "Never do that again!" cried Big Toomai.

The next day, Machua Appa told Petersen Sahib about the boy. Petersen Sahib smiled at Little Toomai. "You are a brave boy. Maybe you can be a hunter one day. But first you must see the elephants dance," said Petersen Sahib. All the hunters laughed at this old joke. Elephants dance in secret places, but nobody ever sees them. Little Toomai did not laugh.

Soon the hunt was over. The camp got ready to go back to the plains. That night, Little Toomai fell asleep near Kala Nag. Late in the night, he woke up. Very quietly, Kala Nag pulled his post out of the ground. He lifted the boy onto his neck and walked into the forest.

They went through tall grass and across a cold river. Then they climbed a high hill. Other elephants walked with them in the dark. At the top was a wide, flat place with no trees. So many elephants stood there under the moon! There were old elephants, young elephants and mothers with babies.

Then the elephants began to stamp their feet. The sound was like a giant drum, and the ground shook. The elephants called out and danced, and the trees shook too. Little Toomai held on to Kala Nag and watched all night.

In the morning, Kala Nag carried the boy back to camp. Little Toomai was tired, wet and dirty. "I saw the elephants dance!" Little Toomai told Petersen Sahib. Then he fell into a deep sleep. Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa followed Kala Nag's tracks up the hill. They found the flat, hard dancing ground. "The boy speaks the truth," said Machua Appa.

That night, there was a big party in the camp. Machua Appa stood up in front of everyone. "This boy is not Little Toomai now. He is Toomai of the Elephants!" cried Machua Appa. Then all the elephants raised their trunks and called out together.

## Level B1

Kala Nag was an elephant in the service of the government of India. His name meant Black Snake, and he was nearly seventy years old. In forty-seven years of work, he carried heavy loads and moved huge logs.

His driver was Big Toomai. Big Toomai's father drove Kala Nag before him. Big Toomai had a ten-year-old son, and everyone called him Little Toomai. The boy loved the old elephant, and Kala Nag did whatever the boy asked. At a word from him, the great elephant lifted him gently onto his back. Little Toomai liked the busy life in the hills much more than life on the plains.

That year, Petersen Sahib brought his men to the Garo hills to catch wild elephants. He was the man who caught all the elephants for the government. Machua Appa was his chief hunter, and he knew the forest better than anyone. For days the hunters drove wild elephants through the forest towards a strong wooden pen. At night, they shouted and waved burning torches until the elephants ran inside. Then the tame elephants and their drivers went in to tie up the wild ones.

One night, Little Toomai was sitting on Kala Nag's back and shouting with the others. Inside the pen, a hunter was holding a young wild elephant. Suddenly the man dropped his rope. Without stopping to think, the boy slid down and ran between the huge, moving feet. He picked up the end of the rope and threw it back to the hunter. Kala Nag saw him at once and lifted him out with his trunk. Big Toomai was frightened and very angry, and he scolded his son for a long time.

The next morning, Petersen Sahib rode past on his own elephant. Machua Appa pointed at Little Toomai and told Petersen Sahib about the rope. Kala Nag lifted the boy up high, and the great man looked at him. "You are a brave boy," said Petersen Sahib, "and one day you will be a hunter. But first you must see the elephants dance!" All the hunters laughed loudly, because it was a very old joke. Elephants dance in secret places in the forest, but no man ever sees them. Little Toomai did not laugh, and he did not forget those words.

On the last evening, the camp was ready to leave for the plains. Little Toomai sat beside Kala Nag, played a small drum and sang to him. Then he lay down on the soft grass and fell asleep. Far away in the hills, wild elephants were calling. Late in the night, the boy woke up. Kala Nag was standing very still, with his ears forward. Then, very slowly, the old elephant pulled his post out of the ground. "Kala Nag, where are you going?" whispered Little Toomai. The elephant lifted him onto his neck and walked into the forest without a sound.

They crossed a river, and the cold water came up to the boy's feet. Then Kala Nag climbed a steep hill. Other elephants appeared around them in the darkness. At last, they came to a wide, flat place at the top of the hill. The ground was hard and smooth, and the moon lit the whole place. Elephants stood everywhere, old ones and young ones, mothers and their babies.

Suddenly one of them called out, and they all began to stamp their feet together. The sound was like a great drum deep in the earth. The ground shook, and the trees around the edge shook too. Little Toomai held on to Kala Nag with both hands and watched with wide eyes. The dance went on all night, and the boy never forgot it.

When the sun came up, the elephants went away quietly into the forest. The open place was now much bigger. During the night, the dancing feet pressed down all the grass and the young trees. Kala Nag carried Little Toomai back to the camp. The boy was pale and tired, and his clothes were wet. He looked at Petersen Sahib and said, "I saw the elephants dance!" Then he slid down from Kala Nag's neck and fell into a deep sleep.

Later that day, Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa followed Kala Nag's tracks into the hills. They found the wide dancing ground, which was hard and flat like a floor. "I have never seen a place like this in forty years," said Machua Appa.

That night, the camp had a feast for the boy. Machua Appa stood up in front of everyone. "From today," said Machua Appa, "this boy is Toomai of the Elephants!" Long ago, Little Toomai's great-grandfather had the same name. Then all the elephants in the camp lifted their trunks and called out together. It was a great salute, and it was only for Little Toomai.

## Level B2

Kala Nag had served the government of India for forty-seven years, and he was nearly seventy years old. His name meant Black Snake, and he stood almost ten feet tall at the shoulder. In his long life he had carried heavy loads across mountains and stacked great logs in timber yards. The younger elephants respected him, and the men who worked with him trusted him completely.

His driver was Big Toomai, whose father, Black Toomai, had driven Kala Nag before him. Big Toomai's grandfather had been there long ago, on the day the elephant was first caught. Big Toomai had a ten-year-old son, known to everyone as Little Toomai. Everyone expected the boy to become a driver too. Kala Nag adored the child and would do things for him that he did for no one else. If Little Toomai asked, the old elephant lowered his trunk and lifted him gently onto his neck. The boy loved the noise and excitement of the hills far more than the quiet life of the plains.

That year, Petersen Sahib brought his men to the Garo hills to catch wild elephants. Petersen Sahib was in charge of catching elephants for the whole government, and Machua Appa was his chief tracker. For days the hunters drove herds of wild elephants through the forest towards a strong wooden pen. At night, they shouted, waved burning torches and beat on drums until the frightened herds rushed inside. Then the tame elephants and their drivers went into the pen to tie up the wild animals one by one.

One night, Little Toomai was sitting high on Kala Nag's back, shouting with the others in the torchlight. Inside the pen, a hunter was struggling with a young wild elephant when the end of his rope slipped away. Without stopping to think, the boy slid down and ran between the huge, moving feet. He seized the rope, threw it back to the hunter, and was lifted out at once by Kala Nag's trunk. Big Toomai was badly frightened, and he gave his son a long and angry scolding. He was also worried, because he did not want the boy to grow up as a wild forest hunter.

The next morning, Petersen Sahib rode past, and Machua Appa told him what the boy had done. Kala Nag lifted Little Toomai up in his trunk so that the great man could see him clearly. Petersen Sahib looked at the shy boy with real interest and some amusement. "You will make a fine hunter one day. But you may only go into the pen when you have seen the elephants dance," said Petersen Sahib. At this the hunters roared with laughter, because it was one of the oldest jokes in the camp. Machua Appa explained that elephants were said to dance in hidden clearings deep in the forest. No man had ever seen it, so the words really meant "never". Little Toomai did not laugh; he kept the words in his heart.

On the last evening before the camp left for the plains, the boy sat beside Kala Nag. He played a small drum and sang an old song until he grew sleepy. Then he lay down in the soft grass by the elephant's feet and fell asleep. Far away, the wild elephants were calling to each other across the dark hills. Late in the night he woke and saw Kala Nag standing perfectly still, his ears spread wide. Then, slowly and silently, the old elephant pulled his post out of the ground. "Kala Nag, where are you going?" whispered Little Toomai. Without a sound, the elephant lifted him onto his neck and walked into the forest.

They pushed through tall, wet grass and under trees so thick that the stars disappeared. They waded across a river, where the cold water swirled around the boy's feet. Then Kala Nag climbed a steep hill, and the boy noticed other great shapes moving beside them in the darkness. At last, they came out into a wide clearing at the very top of the hill. The ground was beaten as hard and smooth as a floor, and the moonlight covered everything. Elephants were arriving from every direction, old ones and young ones, mothers with their small babies. Little Toomai tried to count them, but there were far too many.

For a while the elephants moved quietly among the trees. Then one of them trumpeted, and they all began to stamp their feet together. The sound grew louder and louder, like a giant drum beating deep inside the earth. The ground trembled, and the trees around the clearing shook as if a storm were passing. Little Toomai held on to Kala Nag with both hands, too amazed to be afraid. The great dance continued all night, and the boy saw every moment of it.

As the sun rose, the elephants went quietly away into the forest in different directions. The boy saw that the clearing had grown much larger, because the dancing feet had pressed the grass and young trees flat. Kala Nag carried him back down the hills, and they reached the camp in the morning. Little Toomai was pale and exhausted, and his clothes were soaked with dew. "The elephants danced, and I saw it all!" he told Petersen Sahib. Then he slid down from Kala Nag's neck and fell into a deep sleep that lasted until the afternoon.

Later that day, Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa followed Kala Nag's tracks up into the hills. They found the dancing ground, flat and hard, with thousands of footprints pressed into it. Machua Appa studied it for a long time in silence. "I have followed elephants for forty years," he said quietly, "and I have never seen such a place."

That night the whole camp held a feast for Little Toomai. Machua Appa stood up and declared that the boy had a new name now. "From today he is Toomai of the Elephants, as his great-grandfather was before him!" Machua Appa cried. At that moment all the elephants in the camp lifted their trunks and trumpeted together. It was a great salute, and it was given to Little Toomai alone.

## Level C1

Kala Nag had served the government of India for forty-seven years, which made him, by the reckoning of the men who looked after him, nearly seventy years old. His name meant Black Snake, and he stood almost ten feet tall at the shoulder. He was a dark, patient giant who had carried heavy loads over mountain passes and stacked great logs in timber yards far to the east. Younger elephants gave way to him without being asked, and the drivers spoke of him with the respect usually kept for a wise old man.

His driver was Big Toomai, whose father, Black Toomai, had driven Kala Nag before him. Big Toomai's grandfather had watched the elephant being caught in these same hills long ago. Big Toomai had a son of ten, known to the whole camp as Little Toomai, and everyone assumed the boy would one day sit on Kala Nag's neck. Between the child and the old elephant there was a deep and easy friendship, for Kala Nag would do things for the boy that he would do for no one else. At a word from Little Toomai, he would lower his huge trunk and lift him gently up onto his neck. The boy, for his part, loved the noise and danger of the hills far more than the orderly life of the elephant lines on the plains, where nothing ever seemed to happen.

That year, Petersen Sahib brought his men to the Garo hills to catch wild elephants, as he had done for the government for many seasons. Of all the men in his service, none knew the forest better than Machua Appa, his chief tracker. He could read an elephant's path the way other men read a letter. For days on end the hunters drove the wild herds through the forest towards a strong wooden pen, closing the circle a little tighter each time. At night, they shouted, waved burning torches and beat on drums until the frightened animals rushed through the gate. Then the tame elephants went in with their drivers, and the slow, noisy business of tying up the wild ones began.

On one such night, Little Toomai was perched high on Kala Nag's back, yelling with the rest. Below him, a hunter was struggling with a young wild elephant. The end of the man's rope had slipped from his hands and was lying in the dust among the stamping feet. Without stopping to think, the boy slid down, darted between the moving legs, snatched up the rope and threw it back to the hunter. Kala Nag had seen everything, and before anyone could cry out, his trunk had lifted the boy back out of harm's way. Big Toomai, white with fright, gave his son a long and furious scolding, though what truly troubled him was something else. He feared that if the great Petersen Sahib took notice of the boy, Little Toomai would turn into a wild forest hunter instead of a respectable driver.

His fears were not entirely misplaced, for the next morning Machua Appa told Petersen Sahib the whole story as they rode past the tame elephants. Hearing the boy's name, Kala Nag curled his trunk round Little Toomai and held him up so that the great man could look the boy in the face. Petersen Sahib, who had noticed that the elephant obeyed the child, asked him why he had taught Kala Nag such a trick. The boy, blushing and stammering, admitted that it was so he could reach the green corn that people laid out on their roofs to dry. "You will make a fine hunter one day," Petersen Sahib said, laughing, "but you may only go into the pen when you have seen the elephants dance." The hunters roared with laughter, because it was one of the oldest jokes in the camp. Afterwards, Machua Appa explained it to the puzzled boy. Somewhere deep in the forest, he said, there were hidden clearings that the elephants used for their dances, yet no man had ever seen one. To be told that you could do something after seeing the elephants dance was therefore a polite way of being told that you never would. Little Toomai said nothing, but he did not forget a single word.

On the last evening before the camp set off for the plains, the boy sat beside Kala Nag, tapping a small drum and singing an old song to him. Eventually he grew drowsy and curled up in the soft grass near the elephant's feet. Far away, the wild herds were calling to one another across the hills. Late in the night he woke to see Kala Nag standing perfectly still, his ears spread wide, listening to something the boy could not hear. Then, slowly and without the smallest sound, the old elephant pulled his post out of the ground, much as a gardener lifts a carrot from soft earth. "Kala Nag, where are you going?" whispered Little Toomai. In answer, the elephant lifted him onto his neck and slipped away into the forest.

What followed seemed to the boy like a dream from which he could not wake. They pushed through grass taller than a man, wet with the night, and under trees so thick that the stars vanished one by one. They waded across a river whose cold water swirled round the boy's feet, and then Kala Nag began to climb, steadily and without a pause. Other great shapes, moving in silence, were gathering on every side of them, and Little Toomai understood at last that they were not alone. At the very top of the hill they came out into a wide clearing, its ground beaten as hard and smooth as the floor of a house, and silver under the moon. Elephants were arriving from every direction, great old tuskers, young ones full of mischief, mothers with tiny babies sheltering between their legs. Little Toomai tried to count them, but he could only count to ten, and after he had counted ten many times over he gave up.

For a long while the herd simply shifted and murmured among the trees, as if waiting for a signal that only they could understand. Then one elephant trumpeted, and all of them, together, began to stamp. The sound rose from the ground like the beating of an enormous drum, so deep that the boy felt it in his chest more than he heard it. Trees at the edge of the clearing trembled, the dew shook down from their leaves like rain, and still the thundering went on. Clinging to Kala Nag with both hands, Little Toomai was far too amazed to be frightened, and he watched every moment until the stars grew pale.

With the first gray light, the dance ended as suddenly as it had begun, and the elephants melted back into the forest in twos and threes. Only then did the boy notice that the clearing had grown far wider during the night, for the dancing feet had trodden the grass and young trees flat. Kala Nag carried him back down through the hills, and it was well into the morning when they walked slowly into the camp. Little Toomai, pale, soaked with dew and half asleep, looked up at Petersen Sahib and managed to say, "The elephants danced, and I saw it all." Then he slid from Kala Nag's neck and fell into a sleep so deep that it lasted until the afternoon.

When he woke and had told his story properly, Petersen Sahib and Machua Appa set off together along the trail of Kala Nag's enormous footprints. High in the hills they found the dancing ground exactly as the boy had described it, flat and hard and marked by countless feet. Machua Appa walked slowly round it, saying nothing for a long time, and when at last he spoke, his voice was low. "Forty years I have followed the elephants, and never once have I seen such a place," he said, shaking his head in wonder.

That night the whole camp held a feast for Little Toomai. The boy sat in the middle of it, still hardly able to believe what had happened. When the meal was over, Machua Appa got to his feet and announced, in a voice that carried to every fire, that the child had earned a new name. "From this night he is Toomai of the Elephants, as his great-grandfather was before him!" cried Machua Appa. At those words every elephant in the lines lifted its trunk and trumpeted, a great roar of greeting that rolled out over the hills. It was the salute that the elephants give only to the greatest of men, and this time it was given to a boy of ten.
