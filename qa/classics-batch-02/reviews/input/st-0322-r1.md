# Five Children and It

Summary: Five children find a grumpy sand fairy who grants one wish a day until sunset.

## Level A1

Five children came to a house in Kent. They were Cyril, Anthea, Robert, Jane and Lamb. Lamb was the baby. Mother and Father were away. Martha, the nurse, looked after them.

Near the house was an old sand pit. One day, the children dug a hole there. Suddenly, a strange animal came out! Its eyes were on long horns. It had ears like a bat. Its body was soft and furry. It was a Psammead, a cross sand fairy. "I give you one wish a day. The magic ends at sunset," said the Psammead.

First, the children wished to be beautiful. But Martha did not know them! They were hungry and sad all day. At sunset, they looked like themselves again.

Next, they wished for gold. The sand pit filled with gold coins. They took the coins to Rochester. But the shops did not want them. A policeman thought the children were thieves. Then the sun set, and the gold vanished. So the policeman let them go.

Then they wished for wings. They flew high over fields and trees. They slept on a church tower. They woke up at sunset with no wings. A kind man helped them down.

Another wish turned the house into a castle. Knights stood all around the walls. There was no food inside. The children were very hungry. At sunset, the castle and the knights disappeared.

One boy made a careless wish. Suddenly, Lamb was a tall young man! He did not want to play with them. He rode away on a bicycle. At sunset, he was a baby again.

Mother came home. The children wished for jewels for her. Beautiful jewels appeared in her room. But they belonged to a rich lady! The children ran to the Psammead. "Please put everything right," said Anthea. The Psammead helped them one last time. The children promised to make no more wishes. The jewels went back to the lady. The Psammead dug into the sand and slept.

## Level A2

Five children spent a summer in a white house in Kent. The four older children were Cyril, Anthea, Robert and Jane. They called their baby brother Lamb. Father was away, and Mother went to look after Granny. So Martha, their nurse, looked after the children.

Near the house there was an old sand pit. One morning, the children dug a deep hole there. Suddenly, Anthea felt something soft and warm under her hand. It was a very strange creature. Its eyes stood on long horns, like a snail's eyes. It had ears like a bat and a fat, furry body. It was a Psammead, a sand fairy, thousands of years old.

The Psammead was often cross, but it agreed to help the children. "I can give you one wish every day. But each wish ends at sunset," said the Psammead.

First, the children wished to be very beautiful. In a moment, they all had lovely faces. But when they went home, Martha did not know them. "Go away!" she said, and she shut the door. The children sat outside, hungry and unhappy, all afternoon. At sunset the magic ended, and Martha let them in.

The next day, they wished for gold. At once, the sand pit was full of gold coins. They filled their pockets and walked to Rochester. But the coins were old and strange, so no shop wanted them. A policeman thought the children were thieves. He took them to the police station. At sunset, the gold disappeared, so he let them go.

Another day, they wished for wings. Soon they flew high over fields, woods and rivers. In the evening, they fell asleep on a church tower. They woke up after sunset, and their wings were gone. They could not get down, so they shouted for help. At last a man heard them and opened the tower door.

Later, a wish turned the house into a big castle. Outside the walls, an army of knights waited. There was no food in the castle, and the children were hungry. At sunset the castle became a house again, and the knights vanished.

One afternoon, Lamb was tired and difficult. "I wish you could grow up!" said one of the boys. At once, Lamb was a tall young man. He did not want to play with the children. He got on a bicycle and rode away. At sunset, he became a baby again, and Anthea carried him home.

They ran to the sand pit. "Please make everything right again," said Anthea. "I can help you one last time. But you must never wish again," said the Psammead. The children promised. The jewels went back to the lady, and everybody forgot the trouble. Then the Psammead dug deep into the sand and went to sleep.

## Level B1

One summer, five children came to stay in a white house in the country in Kent. Cyril was the oldest, then came Anthea, Robert and little Jane. The youngest was a baby boy, and everyone called him Lamb. Their father was away on business, and their mother went to look after their sick grandmother. So Martha, the nurse, was in charge of the house.

Near the house there was an old sand pit, and the children loved to play there. One morning they decided to dig a hole to the other side of the world. While they were digging, Anthea's hand touched something soft and warm. They brushed the sand away very carefully. It was the strangest creature in the world. Its eyes were on long horns, like a snail's, and it had ears like a bat. Thick soft fur covered its round body, and its hands and feet looked like a monkey's.

"Don't touch me," said the creature crossly. It was a Psammead, a sand fairy, and it was thousands of years old. Long ago, children wished for huge animals for dinner, and it gave them their wishes. The Psammead was grumpy, but it agreed to give them one wish a day. "Your wish will last until sunset, and then everything will go back to normal," it warned.

Anthea quickly wished for all of them to be very beautiful. In a moment, the children had perfect faces and bright, shining hair. At first, they laughed, but soon they found a problem. They did not know each other, and at home Martha did not know them either. "I don't let strange children into this house," she said, and she shut the door. They waited outside all afternoon, and they were terribly hungry. When the sun went down, their own faces came back, and Martha let them in.

The next morning, they asked the Psammead for more gold than they could count. At once the sand pit was full of shining coins. They filled their pockets and walked to Rochester to buy wonderful things. But the coins were very old, and the shopkeepers looked at them in a strange way. In the end, a policeman decided they were thieves and took them to the police station. Just then the sun set, and the gold disappeared from his hands. With no gold to show anyone, he had to let them go.

Some days later, the children wished for wings. Soft feathered wings grew from their shoulders, and they rose into the air. They flew over fields, woods and villages, and they felt as light as birds. At last, tired and happy, they landed on a church tower and fell asleep. When they woke up, it was dark, and their wings were gone. They could not climb down, and the door to the stairs was locked. Finally, a man heard their shouts and came up to let them out.

Another wish turned their home into a great stone castle. Outside the walls, an army of knights was waiting to attack. The children ran to the top of the walls and tried to look brave. But there was no food in the castle, and they grew very hungry. The knights shouted and blew their trumpets all day long. At sunset the castle faded away, and the children were sitting in their own house again.

One afternoon, Lamb was tired and very difficult. "I wish you could grow up!" said one of the boys without thinking. The Psammead was near, so the wish came true at once. Lamb became a tall young man in smart clothes. He spoke politely, but he did not want to play with the children at all. He got on a bicycle and rode away down the road. The children ran after him for hours. At sunset, he turned back into a baby, and Anthea carried him home.

Then Mother came home, and the children wanted to give her something beautiful. They wished for lovely jewels for her, and the jewels appeared in her room. But they were not new jewels. They belonged to a rich lady who lived nearby, and now she was looking for them. The police were looking for a thief, and the children were afraid.

They ran to the sand pit and woke the Psammead. "Please put everything right," begged Anthea. The Psammead was tired of wishes and of children who always got into trouble. "I will help you one last time, but you must never ask again," it said. The children promised with all their hearts. The jewels went back to the lady, and everyone forgot the whole trouble. Then the Psammead dug down into the cool sand and was gone. The children walked home slowly, and they felt glad to be ordinary again.

## Level B2

One summer, five children were sent to stay in a white house in the Kent countryside. Cyril was the eldest; after him came Anthea, Robert and Jane, and the youngest was a baby brother they all called Lamb. Their father had gone away on business, and their mother had been called away to look after their grandmother, who was ill. That left Martha, the nurse, in charge of the house, and she had plenty to do.

Behind the house lay an old sand pit, and the children soon decided it was the best place in the world. One morning they set out to dig a hole deep enough to come out on the other side of the earth. They had not got very far when Anthea felt something warm and furry under her fingers. Very carefully, they cleared the sand away. Out crawled a creature unlike anything in their picture books. Its eyes stood out on long horns, like a snail's, and it had ears like a bat's. Its fat little body was covered in thick, soft fur, and its hands and feet were like a monkey's.

"Can't a fairy sleep in peace?" it grumbled. It was a Psammead, it explained, a sand fairy thousands of years old. In the old days, it said, children had wished for huge beasts for dinner, and the leftovers turned to stone at sunset. The Psammead hated getting wet and was usually in a bad temper, but it agreed to grant one wish a day. "Every wish ends when the sun goes down, so choose carefully," it warned them.

Anthea, hardly stopping to think, wished that they might all be beautiful beyond words. In an instant the four of them had lovely faces and shining hair, and they hardly knew each other. The trouble began when they went home for lunch. Martha took one look at the four fine strangers on the doorstep and refused to let them in. "Go on with you, I've never seen you before," she said, shutting the door firmly. They spent a long, hungry afternoon outside, and even their beauty began to feel like a punishment. At sunset their ordinary faces returned, and Martha let them in, scolding them for being so late.

The next wish seemed much more sensible: they asked for gold, more than they could ever spend. In a moment the sand pit was shining with heaps of gold coins. They filled their pockets and walked to Rochester, full of plans. But the coins were old and unusual, and the shopkeepers turned them over suspiciously. Nobody wanted to sell anything to a group of dusty children with pockets full of ancient gold. Before long a policeman arrived, and he was sure they had stolen the money. He marched them to the police station, and things looked very bad indeed. Then the sun set, and the coins vanished from the table in front of everyone. With nothing to prove that a crime had happened, the police had to let the children go.

Later that week they wished for wings, and soon they had them: great soft wings of many colors. They rose into the summer sky and flew over fields, woods and villages, laughing with joy. For hours, they felt as free as swallows. Tired at last, they landed on the flat top of a church tower and fell fast asleep in the warm evening light. When they woke, the sun had set, their wings had gone, and the only way down was through a locked door. They shouted until a man heard them and climbed up to let them out.

Another wish turned the house into a great castle, with an army of knights camped all around it. The knights blew trumpets and shouted, ready for a long siege. The children ran up and down the walls, trying their best to look like a whole army. The castle had no food in it, however, and by evening they were dreadfully hungry. At sunset the walls faded away, the knights disappeared, and the children found themselves back in their own house.

On another day, Lamb was being especially difficult. "Oh, I wish you would grow up!" one of the boys cried without thinking. They were near the sand pit, and the Psammead heard. In a moment the baby was gone, and a tall young man stood in his place, neatly dressed and very polite. He had no interest in his brothers and sisters, however. He got on a bicycle and rode off, and the children chased after him for the rest of the day. At sunset, he turned back into a baby, and Anthea carried him home, glad to have him small again.

Then Mother came home, and the children longed to give her something wonderful. Without thinking it through, they wished that she had beautiful jewels. The jewels appeared in her room, but they were not new ones. They belonged to a rich lady from a big house nearby, and the lady had already told the police they were missing. Now a thief was being searched for, and the children knew that someone innocent might be blamed.

They ran to the sand pit and begged the Psammead to put everything right. The Psammead was weary of wishes and of children who never thought before they spoke. "I will put it all right," it said, "but only if you promise never to ask me for another wish." The children promised gladly. So the lady found her jewels, everyone forgot the whole business, and Mother never knew a thing. Then the Psammead dug itself deep into the cool sand, and the children walked home together, glad to be ordinary again.

## Level C1

Five children once came to spend a summer at a white house in Kent, a place of fields and quarries where, at first glance, nothing exciting seemed likely to happen. Cyril was the eldest, Anthea came next, then Robert and Jane, and last of all there was the baby, whom everyone called Lamb because his first word had been "Baa." Their father had been called away on business, and before long their mother hurried off as well, because their grandmother had fallen ill. The household was left in the hands of Martha, the nurse, who was capable enough but had little patience to spare.

The children's favorite place quickly became the old sand pit behind the house, where birds had made little round doorways all along the high sandy wall. One morning, armed with spades, they set out to dig straight through the earth to the other side of the world, a plan that all of them considered perfectly reasonable. They had not dug very far when Anthea's fingers met something warm and furry, and she gave a startled cry. Carefully, almost holding their breath, they scraped the sand away, and out of the hole came a creature quite unlike anything they had ever seen in books. Its eyes stood out on long horns, rather like a snail's, so that it could push them out and draw them in whenever it pleased. It had ears like a bat's, a round, fat body covered in thick soft fur, and hands and feet like a monkey's.

The creature regarded them with deep displeasure and demanded to know why a person could not be left to sleep. It was, it explained with a certain pride, a Psammead, a sand fairy, and it had been alive for thousands of years. In ancient times, it said, children had wished for enormous beasts for their dinner, and whatever was left at sunset had turned to stone. That, it added with a sniff, was why people still dug up such strange stones. It hated water more than anything in the world and had no patience at all with foolish questions. Nevertheless, after a good deal of grumbling, it agreed to grant the children one wish every day. It added a warning they were far too excited to take seriously: every wish would fade the moment the sun went down.

Anthea, eager to choose before anyone could argue, wished that all of them might be more beautiful than anyone had ever been. The change was instant and complete, and four faces of dazzling loveliness looked back at one another without a flicker of recognition. It was strange enough to be unable to recognize your own brother, but it was far worse when they went home, hungry, for their lunch. Martha opened the door, looked at the four splendid strangers on the step, and declared that she had no intention of letting such fancy children into her clean house. "Be off with you, whoever you are," she said, and closed the door in their faces. They spent the long afternoon outside, faint with hunger, discovering that beauty was of very little use to anyone who simply wanted a slice of bread. At sunset, they became themselves again, plain and dusty and ordinary, and Martha let them in, scolding them soundly for staying out so late.

Having learned their lesson, as they believed, they wished the next morning for something truly useful: gold, more of it than they could ever spend. The sand pit instantly blazed with coins heaped up like yellow sand, and the children stuffed their pockets before setting off for Rochester. There, however, they discovered that ancient gold coins are not at all the same thing as money. Shopkeepers turned the strange coins over and over, frowning at the unfamiliar kings stamped on them, and the more the children tried to spend, the more suspicious everyone became. In the end a policeman, quite convinced that the gold must have been stolen, marched them off to the police station, where matters began to look very serious indeed. It was the sunset that saved them: as the light faded, the heaps of gold on the table simply vanished. With nothing left to prove a crime, the puzzled police had no choice but to send them home.

Wings were the next idea, and for a few glorious hours it seemed the best wish of all. Great soft wings of every color sprang from their shoulders, and the children rose into the summer sky, sailing over fields, woods and winding lanes like birds. When at last they grew tired, they settled on the flat top of a church tower and, warm in the evening sun, fell fast asleep. They woke in the dark to find their wings gone, the stone roof cold beneath them, and the little door to the stairs firmly locked. Only after a great deal of shouting did a man hear them and climb up with a lantern to bring them safely down.

On another day the house became a castle, enormous and gray, with towers and a moat, and around it lay an army of knights who had plainly come to besiege it. The children rushed to the walls and did their best to look like a large and fearsome company of defenders, waving and shouting at the enemy. What nobody had thought to wish for was dinner, for the castle held no food at all, and the long day of defending it passed on empty stomachs. The knights made a tremendous noise, and towards evening they seemed ready to attack in earnest. But at the very moment the sun went down, the castle, the moat and the whole army melted into the quiet summer air.

The most alarming wish of all was one that nobody meant to make. Lamb had been especially tiresome one afternoon, and one of the boys, at the end of his patience, exclaimed that he wished the baby would hurry up and grow up. The Psammead was close enough to hear, and in an instant the baby had vanished. In his place stood an elegant young man, perfectly polite and perfectly uninterested in his brothers and sisters. He had better things to do, he told them coolly, than spend the day with children, and off he went on a bicycle. They followed him along the dusty roads all afternoon, desperate not to lose sight of him. At last the sun set, and the elegant stranger shrank back into a sleepy baby, whom Anthea gathered up and carried home with enormous relief.

Then Mother came home, and the children, longing to give her something splendid, wished without much thought that she might have beautiful jewels. The jewels duly appeared in her room, but they were not new: they belonged to a rich lady at a great house nearby, who discovered at once that they were gone. Soon the police were searching for a thief, and the children realized with dismay that some innocent person might be blamed for what their wish had done. There was nothing for it but to go back to the sand pit and confess everything to the Psammead.

The Psammead listened with weary patience, its eyes drooping on their long horns. It was, it told them, quite worn out by their wishes, but it would put everything right on one condition: they must never again ask it for a single wish. The promise was given at once, and with real feeling. So the lady found her jewels safely back in their place, the police forgot that there had ever been a theft, and Mother never knew how close she had come to trouble. Then, with what might have been a small sigh of relief, the Psammead burrowed down into the warm sand until not even the tips of its eyes could be seen. The children walked home through the evening fields, tired and hungry and perfectly ordinary, and found Martha at the door, complaining that their supper was getting cold.
