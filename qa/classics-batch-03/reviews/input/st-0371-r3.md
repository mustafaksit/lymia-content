# Nasreddin Hodja and the Borrowed Pot

Summary: A clever teacher in an old Turkish town borrows his neighbor's big pot and returns it with a surprise.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): pot, neighbor, donkey

## Level A1

Nasreddin Hodja lived in the town of Aksehir. He was a teacher, and people loved him. Hodja had a neighbor next to his house. This neighbor had a very big pot.

One day, Hodja needed a big pot. He did not have one. So he went to his neighbor's door. "Can I use your big pot?" asked Hodja. "Yes, of course," said the neighbor. Hodja thanked him and took the pot home.

After some days, Hodja went to the market. There he bought a very small pot. He put it in the big pot. Then he carried both pots to his neighbor.

The neighbor looked into the big pot. "What is this little pot?" asked the neighbor. "Good news!" said Hodja. "Your pot had a baby at my house. This little pot is its baby. Now you have two pots!" The neighbor smiled. "Thank you, Hodja!" said the neighbor. He took both pots into his house.

After two weeks, Hodja came again. "Can I use your pot again?" asked Hodja. The neighbor was very happy. He wanted another little pot! So the neighbor gave Hodja the big pot.

One week went by. Then another week went by. Hodja did not bring the pot back. So the neighbor went to Hodja's house. "Where is my big pot?" asked the neighbor. Hodja's face was very sad. "I am very sorry," said Hodja. "Your pot died."

"A pot can't die!" said the neighbor. Hodja smiled. "Your pot had a baby," said Hodja. "You believed it then. Now your pot is dead. Why can't you believe it now?" The neighbor had no answer. He went home without his big pot.

## Level A2

Long ago, Nasreddin Hodja lived in the town of Aksehir. He was a teacher, and everyone in the town knew him. People loved to listen to him, because he often made them laugh.

One morning, Hodja went through the town on his donkey. His students walked behind him. But Hodja sat on the donkey the wrong way. He faced the back, not the front. "Hodja, why do you sit like that?" asked the people. "It is easy," said Hodja. "My students walk behind me. I do not want to show them my back. So I sit this way, and we can see each other's faces." The people laughed and went on their way.

Hodja had a neighbor in the next house. One day, Hodja needed a big pot. But he did not have one. So he went to his neighbor's house. "Could I use your big pot for a few days?" asked Hodja. "Of course," said the neighbor, and he gave Hodja the pot.

A few days later, Hodja went to the market. There he bought a very small pot. At home, he put the small pot inside the big one. Then he carried them back to his neighbor's house. "Thank you for your pot," said Hodja. "And I have good news for you!"

The neighbor looked inside the big pot and saw the small one. "What is this?" asked the neighbor. "Your pot had a baby at my house," said Hodja. "The little one is its child, so it is yours, too." The neighbor was surprised. But he was also happy, because now he had two pots. He thanked Hodja and took both pots into his house.

Two weeks later, Hodja came to the neighbor's door again. "Could I use your big pot one more time?" asked Hodja. This time, the neighbor gave it to him with a big smile. "Maybe my pot can have another baby," thought the neighbor.

Days went by, and then weeks. Hodja did not bring the pot back. At last, the neighbor went to Hodja's house. "Hodja, where is my big pot?" asked the neighbor. Hodja looked down, and his face was sad. "I am very sorry, my friend," said Hodja. "Your poor pot died."

"Died?" said the neighbor. "What are you saying? A pot cannot die!" Hodja gave him a kind smile. "Your pot had a baby, and you believed it," said Hodja. "So why can't you believe it died?" The neighbor opened his mouth, but he could not find an answer. He went home without his big pot.

People in Aksehir still tell this story, and they laugh every time.

## Level B1

Long ago, a teacher lived in the small town of Aksehir. His name was Nasreddin Hodja, and everyone in town knew him. He was a kind man with a quick mind. His strange answers often made people laugh. Some people thought he was a little foolish. But in the end, he was often the cleverest person in the whole town.

One morning, Hodja was going across town on his donkey. His students were walking behind him. But Hodja was sitting on the donkey the wrong way around. He was facing the donkey's tail instead of the road. People in the street stopped and stared at him. "Hodja!" called one man. "Why are you sitting backward on your donkey?" Hodja answered calmly. "My students are walking behind me," he said. "I do not want to turn my back on them, because that is not polite. And they should not walk in front of me, because then I will see their backs. This way, we can all see each other's faces." The people laughed and went on their way.

Hodja lived next door to a neighbor who owned a large cooking pot. One day Hodja needed a big pot, but he did not have one of his own. So he went next door and knocked. "Good morning, neighbor," he said. "Could I borrow your big pot for a few days?" "Of course, Hodja," said the neighbor, and he handed it over.

A few days later, Hodja went to the market. It was busy and loud, as it was every week. Hodja looked at many pots there, and at last he bought a very small one. When he got home, he placed the little pot inside the big one. Then he carried them both next door. "Thank you for the pot," said Hodja. "And I have wonderful news for you. While your pot was staying at my house, it had a baby!"

The neighbor lifted the little pot out and turned it in his hands. "A baby?" he asked. He looked at Hodja's face, but Hodja was not smiling at all. "Yes," said Hodja seriously. "This little one is its child, so of course it belongs to you." The neighbor thought Hodja was talking nonsense, because a pot cannot have a baby. But he liked the idea of a free pot, so he did not argue. He thanked Hodja happily and carried both pots into his house.

Two weeks later, Hodja came to the neighbor's door again. "Neighbor, could I borrow your big pot once more?" asked Hodja. This time the neighbor did not stop to think for a moment. "Take it, Hodja, take it!" he said with a big smile. Secretly, he was hoping for another little pot.

Days passed, then a week, then another week. Hodja did not bring the pot back. Every morning the neighbor looked toward Hodja's door, but the pot never came. He began to worry about it. At last, he grew tired of waiting and went to Hodja's house. "Hodja, where is my big pot?" asked the neighbor. Hodja looked down at the ground and gave a long, sad sigh. "Ah, my dear neighbor," said Hodja. "I am so sorry to tell you this. Your poor pot died."

"Died?" cried the neighbor. "What kind of nonsense is this? Everyone knows that a pot cannot die!" Hodja looked at him calmly. "How strange," said Hodja. "When I told you about the baby, you believed me at once. So why can't you believe me now, when I tell you about its death?" The neighbor opened his mouth, then closed it again. He had no answer at all. Slowly, he walked home without his big pot.

Even today, people in Aksehir love to tell this story, and it still makes them laugh.

## Level B2

Long ago, in the small town of Aksehir, there lived a teacher called Nasreddin Hodja. Everyone in town knew him, from the children in the streets to the old men in the market. He was a gentle man with a sharp mind, and his answers were rarely the ones people expected. Some thought he was a little foolish, while others were sure he was the wisest man they had ever met. People leaned in when he spoke, because they never knew whether something silly or something wise was coming. Most of the time, the truth was somewhere in between, and that was exactly why people loved him.

One morning, Hodja set off across town on his donkey, with a group of his students walking behind him. The people of Aksehir were used to seeing him on his donkey, but that morning something was different. Hodja was sitting the wrong way around, facing the donkey's tail instead of the road ahead. Shopkeepers came out of their doors, and a few children began to giggle. "Hodja!" called one man at last. "Have you forgotten how to sit on a donkey?" His students, who knew their teacher well, said nothing and kept walking. Hodja did not seem embarrassed in the least. "Not at all," he replied. "My students are walking behind me, you see. If I sat the usual way, I would be turning my back on them, and that would be rude. If they walked in front of me instead, they would be turning their backs on their teacher. This way, we can all look each other in the face." The man opened his mouth to argue, found that he could not, and simply laughed. Then Hodja rode on, still facing backward, and his students followed him down the street, smiling.

Hodja's house stood next to the house of a neighbor who owned a large cooking pot. One day Hodja found that he needed a big pot, but he did not have one of his own. So he walked next door and knocked politely. "Good morning, neighbor," he said. "Could you lend me your big pot for a few days?" "Of course, Hodja," said the neighbor, and he brought out the heavy pot and handed it over. Hodja thanked him and carried it home, holding it carefully in both arms.

A few days later, Hodja went down to the market. It was noisy and crowded, as it always was, and the smell of fresh bread hung in the air. He walked past the fruit sellers and the cloth sellers until he found a stall full of kitchen things. There he bought a very small pot, so small that it fit easily in one hand. When he got home, he placed the little pot carefully inside the big one. Then he carried them both next door and knocked again. "Thank you for lending me your pot," said Hodja with a warm smile. "And I have some wonderful news. While your pot was staying with me, it gave birth to a baby!"

The neighbor reached into the big pot and lifted out the little one. He turned it over in his hands, and then he looked at Hodja. For a moment he wondered whether Hodja was joking, but Hodja's face gave nothing away. "Gave birth?" he repeated. "Yes," said Hodja, as serious as a judge. "This little one is its child, and a child belongs with its mother. So naturally, it is yours." The neighbor was quite sure that Hodja was talking nonsense, because pots do not have babies. On the other hand, a free pot was a free pot, and he saw no reason to refuse it. So he kept his doubts to himself. He thanked Hodja happily and carried both pots into his house, feeling rather pleased with his luck.

Two weeks later, Hodja knocked on the neighbor's door once more. "Neighbor, could I borrow your big pot again?" he asked. This time the neighbor did not need to be asked twice. "Take it, Hodja, take it!" he said, and he almost pushed the pot into Hodja's arms. Hodja thanked him politely, lifted the heavy pot and carried it home without hurrying. As the neighbor closed the door, he was already wondering how many little pots he might receive this time.

But days passed, then a week, then another week, and the pot did not come back. Each morning the neighbor watched Hodja's door, hoping to see his pot come back with a family of little ones. Each evening, he went to bed disappointed. At last, his patience ran out, and he went over to Hodja's house. He found Hodja sitting quietly in front of his door. "Hodja," he said, "where is my big pot?" Hodja lowered his eyes and let out a long, sad sigh. "Ah, my dear neighbor," he said. "I hardly know how to tell you this. Your poor pot has died."

"Died?" cried the neighbor. "What kind of nonsense is that? Everyone knows that a pot cannot die!" Hodja looked at him calmly, with the faintest smile at the corner of his mouth. "How strange," said Hodja. "When I told you that your pot had given birth, you believed me at once. Why, then, can you not believe me now, when I tell you that it has died?"

The neighbor opened his mouth, then closed it again. He thought of the little pot sitting happily in his kitchen, and he realized that he had no answer at all. Without another word, he turned and walked slowly home, leaving his big pot behind for good.

Even today, the people of Aksehir love to tell this story. Whenever someone believes a thing only because it suits him, someone else is sure to remind him of Hodja's pot.

## Level C1

Long ago, in the small town of Aksehir, where the houses leaned close together and news traveled faster than any messenger, there lived a teacher called Nasreddin Hodja. There was hardly a soul in town who did not know him, from the children chasing one another through the lanes to the old men drinking tea in the market square. He was a gentle man with an unexpectedly sharp mind, and the answers he gave were almost never the ones his listeners had prepared themselves to hear. Some of his neighbors considered him a harmless fool, while others swore he was the wisest man in the whole of the country. The truth, as is so often the case with Hodja, probably lay somewhere in between, and it was precisely this uncertainty that made people so fond of him.

Take, for example, the morning he rode across town with his students trailing behind him on foot. Nobody in Aksehir was surprised to see Hodja on his donkey, since the two of them were a familiar sight in every street. What made the shopkeepers step out of their doorways that morning was the way he was sitting: firmly in the saddle, but facing the donkey's tail rather than the road ahead. A few children began to giggle, and before long a small crowd had gathered along the way. "Hodja!" one man finally called out, unable to contain himself. "Have you forgotten how a person sits on a donkey?" Hodja turned his head toward the man with the patient air of someone explaining something perfectly obvious. "Not in the least," he said. "My students are walking behind me, you see, and if I sat the usual way, I would be showing my back to them, which would hardly be polite. Were they to walk in front of me instead, they would be showing their backs to their teacher, which would be no better. Sitting like this, I can see them, they can see me, and nobody is insulted." The man opened his mouth to object, discovered that he had nothing to say, and joined in the laughter instead.

Next door to Hodja lived a neighbor who owned, among other useful things, a large cooking pot. One day, for reasons the old tellers never trouble to explain, Hodja found himself in need of a big pot, and he did not have one of his own. So he went next door, knocked politely, and waited. "Good morning, neighbor," he said, when the door opened. "I wonder if you might lend me your big pot for a few days." "Of course, Hodja," replied the neighbor, who went inside, came back with the heavy pot in both arms, and handed it over without a second thought.

A few days later, Hodja made his way down to the market. He strolled past the stalls piled high with fruit and the ones hung with bright cloth until he reached a seller of kitchen things. Pans, spoons and bowls of every size lay spread out there in the morning sun, and Hodja studied them for some time. There, after some friendly bargaining, he bought a very small pot, so small that it fit comfortably in the palm of one hand. Back at home, he set the little pot carefully inside the big one, the way a mother might tuck a baby into its cradle. Then he carried the pair of them next door and knocked once more. "Thank you for lending me your pot," said Hodja, with the warmest of smiles. "And I have some wonderful news to share with you. While it was staying under my roof, your pot gave birth!"

The neighbor peered into the big pot, reached inside, and lifted out the tiny newcomer, turning it this way and that as though it might explain itself. "Gave birth?" he repeated slowly. "Indeed," said Hodja, as solemn as a judge delivering a verdict. "This little one is its child, and since a child belongs with its mother, it is naturally yours." The neighbor was quite certain that Hodja was talking nonsense, for no pot in the history of the world had ever produced a baby. Yet a free pot was a free pot, and he could see no sensible reason to refuse one, especially when it was offered so graciously. So he kept his doubts to himself, thanked Hodja with great warmth, and carried both pots into his house, privately congratulating himself on his good fortune. That evening he set the little pot on a shelf where no visitor could fail to notice it, and more than once he caught himself smiling at it.

Two weeks later, there was another knock at the neighbor's door. "Neighbor," said Hodja, "might I borrow your big pot once again?" This time the neighbor hardly let him finish the question. "Take it, Hodja, take it!" he cried, all but pushing the pot into Hodja's arms. As he shut the door, he was already imagining the little pots that would soon be lining his shelves. He even caught himself wondering, not without a certain greed, how many there might be this time.

Days passed, however, and then a week, and then another week, and no pot came back through his door, large or small. Each morning he glanced across the lane at Hodja's door, half expecting to see his pot come proudly home with a whole family of little ones trailing behind it. The neighbor waited, then waited a little longer, telling himself that the birth of so many pots must naturally take time. At last his patience ran out altogether, and he marched over to Hodja's house to ask what had become of his property. "Hodja," he said, a little sharply, "where is my big pot?" Hodja lowered his eyes, folded his hands, and let out a long, mournful sigh that seemed to rise from the very bottom of his heart. "Ah, my dear neighbor," he said. "I hardly know how to break this to you. Your poor pot has died."

"Died?" cried the neighbor, his voice climbing so high that people across the lane turned to look. "What kind of nonsense is that? Everyone in the world knows that a pot cannot die!" Hodja raised his eyes and regarded him calmly, with only the faintest smile playing at the corner of his mouth. "How very strange," said Hodja. "When I told you that your pot had given birth, you believed me at once, and you carried its child home with a smile. Why, then, can you not believe me now, when I tell you that the poor thing has died?"

For a long moment the neighbor stood perfectly still. He opened his mouth and closed it again, and somewhere at the back of his mind he saw the little pot on his kitchen shelf. It sat there like a small and silent witness against him, and it had nothing at all to say in his defense. There was, he realized, no answer he could give that would not make him look even more foolish than he already felt. Without another word, he turned on his heel and walked slowly home, leaving his big pot behind him for good.

To this day, the people of Aksehir delight in telling the story, and their laughter has traveled far beyond the town. Across Turkey, whenever someone swallows a convenient tale simply because it suits him, a friend is sure to remind him that a pot which can give birth can just as easily die.
