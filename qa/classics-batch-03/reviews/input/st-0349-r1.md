# The Story of the Treasure Seekers

Summary: Six children in Lewisham try funny and clever ways to make money for their poor father.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): treasure, uncle, newspaper

## Level A1

The Bastable children lived in Lewisham. There were six of them. Oswald and Dora were the oldest. Then came Dicky, Alice and Noel. They had no mother. Their father had very little money.

"We can find treasure for Father!" said Oswald. Albert lived in the house next door. He came and helped the children. They made a big hole in the garden. Then the sides fell in. Albert could not get out. Albert's uncle came and helped him out. Then he stayed and worked with them. Later, Oswald found two coins there. "Maybe Albert's uncle put them there," thought Oswald.

Next, the children made a newspaper. They wrote it all by hand. It took a very long time. In the end, they had only one newspaper. So they could not sell it. Albert's uncle read it and laughed.

Then Oswald had a new idea. "Let us play kind robbers," he said. Albert wanted to play the game too. He sat in their house and ate cake. Oswald and Dicky wrote a letter. It was for Albert's mother. "Please send money for our father," it said. But soon Albert wanted to go home. Albert's uncle came and took him home. He gave the children no money.

One evening, Father's old uncle came over. He came from a country far away. His coat was very old. "He is poor, like us," said Alice. There was little food on the table. So the children used their own money. They bought oranges and nuts for him. Oswald also gave him the two coins.

The old uncle looked at them quietly. "You are good children," he said. The next day, he came back with presents. He was not poor at all. He was very rich! He helped Father with his business. Soon the family lived in his big house.

## Level A2

The six Bastable children lived in an old house in Lewisham. Dora was the oldest, and Oswald came next. After them came Dicky, then Alice and Noel, and the youngest brother. Their mother died when they were small. Now Father's business was in trouble, and there was little money. Father looked worried all the time.

"People in books always find treasure," said Oswald one day. "We can look for it in the garden!" said Dicky. Albert, the boy from next door, came to help them. They worked all morning and made a deep hole. Then Albert went down into the hole. Suddenly the sides fell in around him. Albert could not move, and he started to cry.

His uncle heard him and ran into the garden. Albert's uncle pulled him out of the hole. Then he stayed and helped the children. Soon Oswald found two coins in the ground. "Treasure!" said Noel happily. But Albert's uncle had a little smile on his face. Oswald thought, "He put them there himself."

Next, Dora had an idea. "Let us make a newspaper and sell it," she said. Everybody wrote something for it. Alice and Noel wrote stories, and Oswald wrote the news. They wrote every word by hand, and it took days. At the end, they had only one newspaper. One newspaper was not enough to sell. So they gave it to Albert's uncle. He read every page and laughed. "This paper is very good, but it cannot make money," he said.

Oswald still wanted money for Father. "Let us be robbers, but very kind robbers," he said. Albert said yes to the game at once. The children kept him in their kitchen and gave him cake. Then Oswald and Dicky wrote a letter to Albert's mother. "Dear Madam, Albert is with us, and he is happy. Please send a little money for our poor father." But Albert soon wanted to go home, and he began to cry. Then Albert's uncle came to the door with the letter. "Robbers do not usually say please," he said with a laugh. He took Albert home, but he gave them no money.

Weeks later, Father's old uncle came back from a far country. Father asked him to dinner on Friday. The uncle had an old coat, and he never talked about money. "He is poor, like us," said Alice. There was not much food in the house. So the children used their own money. They bought oranges and nuts for him. Then Oswald gave him the two coins from the garden. "Please take these," said Oswald. The old uncle looked at them for a long time.

The next day, he came back with a lot of presents. He was not poor at all. In fact, he had a lot of money. "You were very kind to a poor old man," he said. He helped Father with his business. Then the whole family moved into his big house. In the end, the treasure came to the Bastable children.

## Level B1

The six Bastable children lived in a tall, old house on a road in Lewisham. Dora was the eldest, and she tried hard to be like a mother to the others. Oswald came next, then Dicky, then the twins, Alice and Noel. Last of all came the youngest brother. Their mother died a few years before, and after that the house was very quiet. Then their father became ill. While he was in bed, his business partner ran away with the money. Now Father was always busy and worried, and there was never enough money for anything.

"Somebody must save this family," Oswald said one wet afternoon, "and it should be us." The children sat on the floor and talked about ways of getting rich. "In stories, people always find treasure," said Dicky, "so why don't we dig for some?"

The next morning they took spades into the garden. They started a hole near the back wall. Albert, the boy who lived next door, climbed over the wall to help. He did not like most games, but he liked digging. By lunchtime the hole was deep, and Albert was standing at the bottom of it. Suddenly the sides fell in, and the earth held his legs tight. Albert could not move, and he began to cry loudly. His uncle heard the noise, ran into the garden, and pulled him out. Then, instead of being cross, Albert's uncle stayed and dug with them. After a while, Oswald found two silver coins in the earth. "Treasure!" shouted Noel, and everyone cheered. But Oswald noticed a strange little smile on the face of Albert's uncle. He wondered about those coins for a long time.

Their next plan came from Dora. "Newspapers make a lot of money," said Dora, "so we will write one and sell it." Everybody wrote something for it. Noel wrote poems, Alice wrote a story about a brave knight, and Dicky wrote about business. Oswald wrote the news, although there was not much news on their road. They copied every page by hand, and it took them nearly a week. When it was finished, there was only one copy, and nobody wanted to pay for it. In the end they gave it to Albert's uncle. He read every word and laughed in a kind way. "It is a fine paper," he said, "but it will never make you rich."

Oswald still did not want to give up. "Robbers always have money," said Oswald, "but we will be polite robbers and never hurt anyone." Albert agreed to play the game and to be their prisoner. The children kept him in the kitchen, gave him cake, and let him read their books. Meanwhile, Oswald and Dicky wrote a very polite letter to Albert's mother. The letter said, "Dear Madam, Albert is safe and happy with us. Please send a little money for our father, and we will send him home after tea." But the game was slower than they expected, and Albert soon grew tired of it. He wanted to go home, and at last he began to cry. Just then Albert's uncle arrived with the letter in his hand. He was trying very hard not to laugh. "Real robbers don't say please," he told them, and he took Albert home. He did not send them any money.

A few weeks later, Father had news. His uncle, an old man who lived abroad for many years, was back in the country. Father asked him to dinner on Friday, and the children watched the old gentleman arrive. His coat was old, and he did not say a word about money. "Poor old thing," said Alice quietly, "he is just as poor as we are."

Father's dinner was small that evening, because there was very little money for food. So the children emptied their money boxes and ran to the shop. They came back with oranges and nuts and shared them with their guest. Then Oswald took out the two silver coins from the garden. "Please have these," he said, "because we know what it is like to be poor." The old uncle looked at the coins and then at the children's faces. For a moment he could not speak.

The next day he returned in a cab full of presents. He was not poor at all; in fact, he was very rich. "You were kind to a man with nothing," he said, "so now it is my turn." He helped Father with his business, and soon the whole family moved into his big house. The children never found treasure in the ground, but in the end the treasure found them.

## Level B2

The six Bastable children lived in a tall, shabby house on a road in Lewisham. For some time, however, that house had been growing quieter and poorer. Their mother had died, and soon afterwards their father fell seriously ill. While he was in bed, his business partner ran off with most of the money. Father never quite recovered from the blow. Now he came home late, looked gray and tired, and talked about business in a voice that silenced the whole house.

Dora, the eldest, did her best to look after the others, though she worried far too much. Oswald, who came next, considered himself the natural leader, and he was usually the first to have an idea. After him came Dicky, who was good at sums, and then the twins, Alice and Noel. Last came the youngest brother, who followed everybody everywhere.

"Somebody has to save this family," Oswald announced one rainy afternoon, "and since nobody else is doing it, it must be us." The others agreed at once, and they spent the evening making a list of ways to become rich. Most of the ideas came from books, and the very first one was the oldest of all: buried treasure.

The next morning they marched into the back garden with spades and began a hole beside the wall. Albert, the boy next door, climbed over to join them, since digging seemed far more sensible to him than most games. By midday the hole was so deep that Albert stood in it up to his chest. That was when the sides gave way. Earth slid down around him and held his legs so firmly that he could not move. He began to howl. His uncle, who was writing at an open window, heard the noise, came running, and pulled him out. Instead of scolding them, Albert's uncle took off his coat and helped them dig, telling them stories about pirates as he worked. Not long afterwards, Oswald found two silver coins in the earth. Noel shouted "Treasure!" so loudly that the neighbors looked over their fences. Oswald, however, had caught a strange little smile on the face of Albert's uncle. He always suspected that the coins had come out of that gentleman's own pocket.

Dora's plan was more modern. "Newspapers make fortunes," she said, "so we will produce one ourselves and sell it." Each child contributed something. Noel supplied poems, Alice wrote an exciting tale about a knight, and Dicky offered advice on business. Oswald, as editor, collected the news. Since they had no printing press, every page had to be copied out by hand. After nearly a week of ink and arguments, they had produced exactly one copy. It soon became clear that nobody would pay for a single newspaper, so they presented it to Albert's uncle instead. He read every word with great seriousness and laughed in all the right places. Then he told them it was an excellent paper, but it would never make them a penny.

Oswald refused to give up. "Robbers always have money," he reasoned, "but we shall be honorable robbers, who are polite to everybody and never hurt a soul." Albert agreed to be their prisoner, mostly because the job seemed to come with cake. They settled him in the kitchen with a plate of buns and a pile of their best books. Meanwhile, Oswald and Dicky wrote a letter to his mother. "Dear Madam," it began, "Albert is quite safe and happy with us. We only ask for a small sum of money to help our father, and we will send him home after tea." Unfortunately, being a prisoner was much duller than Albert had expected, and before long he was crying for his own home. At that moment his uncle appeared at the door with the letter in his hand, trying very hard not to laugh. "In my experience," he said, "real robbers seldom say please." Then he led Albert home without paying a single penny.

Some weeks later Father mentioned that his own uncle had come back to the country after many years abroad. The old man was going to dine with them on Friday. When he arrived, the children studied him closely. His coat was worn, he spoke little, and he never once mentioned money. The children decided that he must be as poor as they were. "Poor old dear," Alice whispered, "perhaps nobody is ever kind to him."

Father's dinner that evening was plain and small, because there was little money left for the kitchen. Ashamed of it, the children emptied their money boxes and ran to the shop on the corner. They came back with oranges and nuts for dessert. They served their guest first and gave him the biggest orange. Then Oswald took the two silver coins from his pocket and pushed them across the table. "Please take these, sir," he said, "because we know what it is like when there isn't enough." The old uncle looked from the coins to the six serious faces. For a long moment he did not say anything at all.

The next day a cab stopped outside the house, piled high with presents, and the uncle climbed out, smiling. He was not poor in the least; on the contrary, he was extremely rich. "You shared all you had with a man you thought was poor," he said, "and that means more than any fortune." He took over Father's troubles and set the business on its feet again. Then he invited the whole family to live with him in his great house.

So the Bastable children never dug up a real treasure, never sold their newspaper, and never earned a penny as robbers. Yet in the end, the fortune they wanted arrived by itself, carried in by the guest they had tried to help.

## Level C1

The six Bastable children lived in a tall, shabby house on one of the long roads of Lewisham. It had once been a comfortable house, but now, as Oswald put it, it was going down in the world by inches. Their mother had died some years before, and not long afterwards their father fell so ill that for months he could barely leave his bed. It was during that illness that his business partner quietly vanished, taking most of the money with him, and Father had never really recovered from the blow. He came home late now, ate little, and spoke about business in a tone that made even the youngest go silent.

The children noticed far more than grown-ups imagined. They noticed that the shopkeepers grew short with them, that the good silver quietly disappeared from the sideboard, and that the cook muttered about wages whenever she thought nobody was listening. Dora, the eldest, tried to be a mother to the rest and worried about everything, while Oswald, who came next, considered himself the natural leader. Dicky was clever with sums, the twins, Alice and Noel, did everything together, and the youngest brother trailed after all of them like a small, determined shadow.

"Somebody," Oswald declared one dripping afternoon, "has got to rescue this family, and as nobody else seems inclined to try, the task falls to us." The motion was carried without a single objection, and the rest of the afternoon went on drawing up a list of ways to get rich, nearly all of them borrowed from books. Father, they agreed, was not to be troubled with any of it; the rescue would come as a surprise. At the very top, naturally, stood the most respectable method of all: digging for buried treasure.

Equipped with spades and boundless optimism, they set to work the following morning beside the back wall of the garden. Albert, the boy next door, climbed over to join them; he had no talent for games of pretend, but digging struck him as a thoroughly sensible occupation. By midday the hole was deep enough for Albert to stand in it up to his chest. It was precisely then that the walls of earth gave way and slid in around him, gripping his legs so firmly that he could not stir. His howls brought his uncle, a writer who happened to be working at an open window, and that gentleman hauled him out and dusted him down. Rather than scolding anybody, Albert's uncle rolled up his sleeves and joined the excavation, entertaining them with tales of pirates as he dug. Some time later Oswald's spade turned up two silver coins, and Noel shrieked "Treasure!" with such conviction that heads appeared over three separate fences. Oswald, however, had glimpsed a peculiar smile on the face of Albert's uncle. Ever afterwards he suspected that the treasure had traveled from that gentleman's pocket to the ground only minutes before it was found.

The second scheme was Dora's, and it had the advantage of sounding modern. "Newspapers make enormous fortunes," she pointed out, "so the obvious thing is to produce one of our own and sell it." Contributions poured in. Noel supplied poems of great feeling and uncertain rhyme, Alice wrote a breathless romance about a knight, and Dicky provided sober advice on business. Oswald, as editor, collected whatever news the road could offer, which was not much. Lacking a printing press, they copied every column out by hand, and after nearly a week of blotted ink and quarrels about spelling they possessed exactly one copy. Even Dora could see that a single newspaper, however excellent, would not go far among the reading public of Lewisham, so they presented it to Albert's uncle instead. He read it from beginning to end with perfect gravity and laughed only where laughter was intended. Then he pronounced it a first-rate paper that would never, in this world, make them a penny.

Oswald, though, was not a boy to be discouraged by a single setback, or even by two. "Robbers are never short of money," he reasoned, "but we shall be honorable robbers, the polite sort, who never frighten anyone and always say thank you." Albert consented to be their prisoner, chiefly because the position appeared to involve cake. Dora had doubts about the whole business, but she was outvoted, and she consoled herself by making sure the prisoner had the best cushion. They installed him in the kitchen with a plate of buns and a heap of their best books, while Oswald and Dicky composed, in their finest handwriting, a letter to his mother. "Dear Madam," it ran, "we beg to inform you that Albert is perfectly safe and very comfortable. We ask only a modest sum to assist our father, and we shall send him home directly after tea."

Captivity, unfortunately, proved far duller in practice than in theory, and before the buns were finished Albert was weeping for his own home. At that moment his uncle appeared in the doorway with the letter in his hand and the strained expression of a man determined not to laugh. "I have met a good many robbers in books," he remarked, "and not one of them ever said please." With that he led Albert away, without paying so much as a farthing.

A few weeks later, Father mentioned at breakfast that his own uncle, who had spent most of his life abroad, was back in the country and would dine with them on Friday. The children, peering over the banisters, examined the visitor with professional interest. His coat was worn at the cuffs, he spoke very little, and he never once alluded to money. From these clues the children concluded, with complete confidence, that he was every bit as poor as they were. "Poor old dear," Alice murmured, "I don't suppose anyone is ever kind to him."

Father's dinner that night was plain and scanty, for there was little left to spend on the kitchen, and the children felt its poverty keenly on their guest's behalf. They emptied their money boxes, raced to the shop on the corner, and returned with oranges and nuts, which they arranged as handsomely as they could for dessert. Dicky, who kept the accounts, announced that the dessert had cost them every penny they possessed, and nobody minded in the least. They served the old gentleman first and pressed the largest orange upon him. Then Oswald, after a whispered conference with Dora, drew the two silver coins from his pocket and slid them across the tablecloth. "Please take these, sir," he said, rather red in the face, "because we know what it's like when there isn't enough." The old man looked from the coins to the six solemn faces turned towards him, and for a long moment he seemed to find nothing whatever to say.

The next afternoon a cab drew up at the gate, so laden with parcels that the driver could scarcely see over them, and out climbed the uncle, beaming. He was not poor in the least; he was, it emerged, extremely rich. "You shared everything you had with an old man you believed had nothing," he told them, "and I find I value that more than all my money." He took Father's difficulties into his own hands, set the business firmly on its feet again, and insisted that the whole family come to live with him in his great house.

So, in the end, the Bastable children never unearthed a genuine treasure, never sold a single newspaper, and never earned a farthing as robbers, however courteous. The fortune they had pursued so energetically arrived instead of its own accord, carried through the front door by the very guest they had set out to rescue.
