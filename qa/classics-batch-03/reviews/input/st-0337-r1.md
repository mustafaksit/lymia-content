# The Arrest of Arsène Lupin

Summary: A famous gentleman thief is somewhere on a ship to New York, and every passenger wants to find him.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): ship, camera, jewels

## Level A1

I was on a big ship. Its name was the Provence. It went across the sea to New York.

On the second day, a message came. It said, "Arsene Lupin is on your ship." Everyone knew the name Lupin. He took money and jewels from big houses. But no one knew his face. The message gave one letter of his name. The letter was R. Then the message stopped.

Now people talked about Lupin all day. They looked at every man on the ship. They asked, "Who is Lupin? Is he here with us?"

I met a young woman on the ship. Her name was Nelly. She was kind. We walked and talked every day. I had a small camera. Nelly often held my camera for me. She liked to ask about Lupin.

Some people looked at a man called Rozaine. His name began with R. But Rozaine showed his papers to everyone. He was not Lupin.

Then a woman lost her jewels. Someone took them from her room at night. In her room, people found a card. The card said, "Arsene Lupin." Now everyone wanted to find him.

That evening, Nelly asked me, "Who is Lupin?" I laughed and said, "Maybe it is me!"

At last, the ship came to New York. A man stood near the ship. His name was Ganimard. He worked for the police. He knew the face of Lupin very well. Nelly and I walked off the ship. She had my camera in her hand. Ganimard put his hand on my arm. "Hello, Arsene Lupin," said Ganimard. Yes, I was Lupin!

Nelly looked at my camera. The jewels and money were in it. Now she understood everything. She walked to the side of the ship. She opened her hand. The camera fell into the water. Now the police could not find the jewels. Nelly walked away and did not look back. Ganimard took me away. But I never forgot Nelly.

## Level A2

I remember that trip very well. I was on a big ship, the Provence. It crossed the sea to New York. The food was good, and the people on the ship were happy.

On the second day, a message came to the ship. It said, "Arsene Lupin is on your ship. He travels alone. His name begins with R." Then the message stopped, and no more words came.

Everyone knew the name Arsene Lupin. He took money and jewels from the best houses in the city. He often changed his face and his name. So the police never caught him. And now he was here, on our ship!

No one could leave the ship. People looked at each other with fear. They watched every man with light hair. They asked, "Who travels alone? Is he here with us?"

I spent many hours with a young woman, Nelly. She traveled alone, and she wanted to see her father. She was clever and kind, and I liked her very much. We walked on the ship every morning. I had a small camera, and I took pictures of the sea. Nelly often carried the camera for me. She wanted to know everything about Lupin.

Soon people found an answer. A man called Rozaine traveled alone, and his name began with R. Everyone watched him and talked about him. But Rozaine went to the officers of the ship with his papers. The papers showed his real name and his home. So Rozaine was not Lupin.

Then, one night, something bad happened. A woman on the ship lost her beautiful jewels. Someone took them from her room in the dark. On her table, she found a small card. The card said, "Arsene Lupin." Now everyone was sure about it. Lupin was really on the ship! The officers looked in every room, but they found nothing.

That evening, Nelly and I sat together and looked at the sea. "Can you guess the name of Lupin?" Nelly asked me. I smiled. "Maybe it is my name," I said. She laughed, but she looked at my face for a long moment.

After many days, we arrived in New York. Hundreds of people stood near the ship. One of them was Ganimard, a famous police officer. For years, he looked for Lupin. He knew the face of Lupin better than anyone.

Nelly and I walked down from the ship together. She held my camera in her hands. Ganimard came to me and put his hand on my arm. "Hello, Arsene Lupin," said Ganimard. It was true. I was Arsene Lupin.

Nelly's face went white. She looked at me, and then at the camera. She understood. The jewels and the money were inside it! I put them there, and she carried them for me every day. Nelly took one step to the side. She opened her hands, and the camera fell into the water. Now no one could find the jewels. Then she walked past me and did not say a word. Ganimard took me away, but I thought only of Nelly.

## Level B1

I still remember that journey on the Provence. She was a fine ship, and she was carrying us across the ocean to New York. The sea was calm, the food was excellent, and the passengers were enjoying themselves. For the first day, our biggest worry was what to wear at dinner.

On the second evening, the ship received a message by wireless. Within an hour, everyone on board knew what it said. "Arsene Lupin is among your passengers. He has fair hair and travels alone in first class. His name begins with R." Just then a storm broke the connection, and the rest of the message never arrived.

Arsene Lupin was the most famous thief of his time. He took jewels from great houses and paintings from rich collectors. He always did it with style. Nobody knew his real face, because he could change it as easily as his coat. The police chased him everywhere, but he always escaped. Now he was here, somewhere among us, and we were still five days from land.

Suddenly every man on board was studying every other man. Who was travelling alone? Who had fair hair? Whose name began with R? Even the ladies were playing this game at dinner, and they seemed to enjoy it.

For my part, I spent most of my time with a young woman called Nelly. She was travelling alone to meet her father on the other side of the ocean. She was clever and lively, and many men on the ship wanted her attention. I was happy because she seemed to prefer my company. We walked on deck together, and I took photographs with my small camera. Often Nelly carried the camera for me while we talked. She loved the mystery and kept asking me questions about Lupin.

Soon the passengers found a suspect. A man called Rozaine was travelling alone, and he had fair hair. His name began with R, and people started to whisper when he passed. Rozaine was angry, so he went to the captain with his papers. The papers showed exactly who he was, and the captain believed him. Rozaine was not Lupin, and the game started again.

Then, on the third night, a lady lost her jewels. She was a wealthy lady who owned a large and beautiful collection. Someone entered her cabin while she was sleeping and took the best pieces. The thief left only one thing behind. It was a small card with the name Arsene Lupin. Now nobody was laughing. The captain searched the cabins, but he found nothing.

That evening Nelly and I were standing by the rail, watching the sea. "Who do you think Lupin is?" she asked suddenly. I looked into her eyes and smiled. "Perhaps he is standing next to you," I said. She laughed, but for a moment she did not take her eyes off my face.

At last, we reached New York on a bright morning. A crowd was waiting on the dock. Among them, I noticed a face that I knew well. It belonged to Ganimard, the great detective who was Lupin's oldest enemy. He was there for one reason, and I knew what it was.

Nelly and I walked down to the dock together. She was carrying my camera, as she often did. Ganimard stepped forward and put his hand on my arm. "Arsene Lupin, I believe," said Ganimard calmly. There was no point in lying. Yes, I was Arsene Lupin.

Nelly turned pale and looked at me, then at the camera in her hands. In that moment she understood everything. The jewels and the money were inside it, because I put them there myself. Every day she carried them for me without knowing it. Without a word, she took a step towards the edge of the dock. She let the camera fall into the water between the dock and the ship. Then she walked past me and did not look back. Ganimard took me away, but I was thinking only of her.

## Level B2

It was a perfect crossing, at least at the start. The Provence was a fast and comfortable ship. The passengers who filled her decks were the kind of people who expect pleasant company and excellent dinners. We were heading for New York, and the sea was calm and blue. For the first day, the most serious question on board was who would sit next to whom at dinner.

On the second evening, everything changed. A message arrived by wireless, and the officer who received it took it straight to the captain. Within an hour, every passenger knew what it said. "Arsene Lupin is on board your ship. First class, fair hair, travelling alone under a name that begins with R." Just as the last letter came through, a storm cut the connection. The rest of the message was lost somewhere above the waves.

There was hardly a person alive who had not heard of Arsene Lupin. He had taken jewels from the safes of princes and paintings from the walls of millionaires. He had always done it with charm and perfect manners, and he had never once been caught. The newspapers loved him, and the police hated him. Nobody could describe his face, since he changed it as easily as other men change their gloves. And now this gentleman thief was among us, with five long days of open sea ahead.

Within a day, the ship became a theater of suspicion. Men who had been friends at lunch were watching each other at dinner. Every passenger with fair hair felt the eyes of the room upon him. Every name beginning with R was repeated in whispers. The ladies, I noticed, found the whole affair thrilling. Several of them secretly hoped for a visit from such a famous man.

As for me, I spent most of my days in the company of a young woman named Nelly. She was travelling alone to join her father, who lived on the other side of the ocean. She was clever, lively and quite lovely. Many of the men on board would have liked her attention, but she seemed to prefer mine. We walked the decks together, and I took photographs with my small camera, which she often carried for me. Like everyone else, she was fascinated by Lupin, and she asked me endless questions about him.

Before long, the passengers decided they had found their man. A gentleman called Rozaine was travelling alone, he had fair hair, and his name began with R. People stopped talking when he entered a room. Rozaine, deeply offended, went straight to the captain and showed him his papers and letters. He even offered a large reward to anyone who could find the real thief. They proved beyond doubt who he was, and the captain declared publicly that Rozaine was not Lupin. The hunt, it seemed, had to start again.

Then, on the third night, the thief showed that he was real. A wealthy lady, who was travelling with a famous collection of jewels, woke up to find that the best pieces were gone. Someone had entered her cabin while she slept, opened her case, and taken the finest stones without making a sound. On her table there was a small white card, and on the card was a name: Arsene Lupin. After that, nobody treated the affair as a game. Ladies locked their jewels away, and gentlemen kept their wallets close. The captain had every cabin searched, but his men found nothing at all.

That evening Nelly and I stood by the rail, watching the dark water slip past. "Tell me honestly," she said, "who do you think Lupin is?" I looked into her eyes and smiled. "Perhaps he is standing right next to you," I replied. She laughed, as I expected, but I noticed that for a long moment she did not take her eyes from my face.

The last two days of the voyage passed slowly. The passengers grew tired of guessing, and they began to count the hours until New York. There, everyone agreed, the police would surely be waiting. Only Nelly kept returning to the subject on our evening walks, and I was more than happy to let her talk.

Finally, on a bright and windy morning, the coast rose out of the sea. The Provence moved slowly towards the city of New York. A great crowd was waiting on the dock. Among all those faces, I recognized one that I knew too well. It was Ganimard, the famous detective, who had spent years trying to catch Arsene Lupin. He had crossed the ocean for one reason only, and I knew exactly what it was.

Nelly and I walked down to the dock together, and as usual she was carrying my camera. Ganimard pushed through the crowd, stepped in front of me and laid his hand on my arm. "Arsene Lupin, I believe," said Ganimard quietly. It was pointless to deny it. Yes, I was Arsene Lupin, and I had been Lupin the whole time.

Nelly went pale. She looked at me, then at the camera in her hands, and I saw the truth reach her. The jewels and the money were inside that little box, because I had hidden them there myself. For days, she had carried them around the ship for me, without knowing it. If Ganimard found that camera, he would have all the proof he needed. Nelly said nothing. She took one step towards the edge of the dock. Then she let the camera drop into the water between the dock and the ship. Then she walked past me without a single glance. Ganimard led me away through the crowd, but my thoughts stayed with her.

## Level C1

It promised to be a perfect crossing, and for one day it was exactly that. The Provence was as swift and comfortable a ship as any that sailed for New York. The people who filled her decks belonged to that fortunate class which expects pleasant company, excellent dinners and nothing more troubling than the question of where to sit. On that first evening, the gravest matter on board was whether the captain's table had been arranged with sufficient tact.

On the second evening, however, the peace of the ship came to an abrupt end. A message arrived by wireless, and the officer who received it carried it to the captain with an expression that did not escape anyone. Within the hour, every passenger knew its contents. "Arsene Lupin is on board your ship. First class, fair hair, travelling alone under a name beginning with R." At that very moment a storm broke over the ocean, the connection failed, and whatever else the message might have told us vanished somewhere among the clouds.

There could hardly have been anyone on board who did not know the name. Arsene Lupin had emptied the safes of princes and lifted paintings from the walls of millionaires. He had done it all with such charm, such wit and such perfect manners that half the world admired him even as the other half cursed him. The newspapers adored him; the police despised him. What made him truly dangerous was that no one could describe him, for he changed his face as easily as other men change their gloves. And this gentleman thief, it now appeared, was sharing our voyage, with five days of open sea between us and the nearest land.

In a matter of hours the ship became a small theater of suspicion. Men who had laughed together over lunch were studying one another across the dinner table, measuring the color of a neighbor's hair and silently testing his name against that single, maddening letter. Every man with fair hair felt the room turn towards him when he entered it. The ladies, I observed, found the whole affair delightful, and more than one of them seemed secretly to hope that so famous a thief might honor her with a visit.

For my own part, I spent the greater share of those days with a young woman named Nelly. She was travelling alone to join her father on the far side of the ocean. She possessed every quality a long voyage requires: intelligence, high spirits and a face that was a pleasure to look at. A good many gentlemen on board would gladly have claimed her attention, yet for reasons I did not fully deserve she seemed to prefer mine. We walked the decks for hours, and I took photographs with my small camera, which she very often carried for me. Like everyone else, she was enchanted by the mystery of Lupin, and she would question me about him with an eagerness that amused me more than I let her see.

It was not long before the passengers settled on a culprit. A gentleman called Rozaine was travelling alone, his hair was fair, and his name, unfortunately for him, began with R. Conversations stopped whenever he came near, and people stepped aside to let him pass as though he carried some infection. Rozaine, deeply offended, marched to the captain and laid before him his papers, his letters and every other proof of his identity he could find. They settled the question entirely, and the captain announced to the whole ship that Rozaine was certainly not Arsene Lupin. The hunt, to the general disappointment, had to begin all over again.

Then, on the third night, the thief made his presence felt. A wealthy lady, whose collection of jewels was almost as famous as Lupin himself, woke to discover that the finest pieces had disappeared. Someone had slipped into her cabin as she slept, opened her case and removed the best stones without disturbing so much as a curtain. On her table lay a small white card bearing a single name: Arsene Lupin. From that hour, nobody treated the matter as a game. The captain ordered every cabin searched from end to end, but his men came back with empty hands and tired faces.

That evening Nelly and I leaned against the rail, watching the dark water slide past the hull. She was silent for a while, and then she turned to me. "Tell me honestly," she said, "who do you think he is?" I met her eyes and smiled. "Perhaps he is standing right beside you," I answered. She laughed, exactly as I had intended, and yet I noticed that for a long moment afterwards she did not take her gaze from my face.

The remaining days passed in a curious mixture of nervousness and boredom. Passengers locked their valuables away, bolted their doors at night and agreed, over endless cups of tea, that the police would surely be waiting in New York to settle the matter. Nelly alone refused to lose interest. On our evening walks she would return to the subject again and again, turning it this way and that, as if Lupin were a puzzle set for her alone. I found, rather to my surprise, that I enjoyed her questions far more than was wise.

At last, on a bright and windy morning, the coast rose out of the sea, and the Provence crept slowly towards the harbor of New York. An immense crowd had gathered on the dock to greet her. Among those hundreds of faces I recognized one at once, and I confess that my heart sank a little. It belonged to Ganimard, the celebrated detective, a patient and stubborn man who had spent years of his life pursuing Arsene Lupin. He had not crossed the ocean to admire the view, and I knew perfectly well why he was there.

Nelly and I walked down to the dock together, and, as she so often did, she was carrying my camera. Ganimard made his way through the crowd without hurry, placed himself squarely in front of me and rested his hand on my arm. "Arsene Lupin, I believe," said Ganimard, in the quiet tone of a man stating the weather. There was no sense in denying it. I was Arsene Lupin, and I had been Lupin from the first hour of the voyage, sitting beside her at dinner, laughing at the very rumors I had caused.

Nelly turned pale. Her eyes moved from my face to the camera in her hands, and I watched the truth arrive in them, piece by piece. Inside that small, harmless box lay the lady's jewels and a good deal of money besides, for I had hidden them there myself. For days, she had carried my whole fortune about the ship without the faintest idea of it. Had Ganimard opened that camera, he would have held every proof he needed. Nelly said nothing at all. She took a single step towards the edge of the dock. Then, with a movement so natural that no one else noticed it, she let the camera fall into the narrow strip of water between the dock and the ship.

Then she walked past me, her head high, without so much as a glance in my direction, and disappeared into the crowd. Ganimard led me away, well pleased with his morning's work, and I went with him calmly enough. Yet it was not the detective, nor the prison that waited for me, that filled my thoughts as we left the harbor behind. It was the girl who had known exactly what I was, and who had chosen, in one silent moment, to save me all the same.
