# The Swiss Family Robinson

Summary: After a shipwreck, a father, a mother and their four sons build a new life on a lonely island.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): island, raft, tree house

## Level A1

A family lived on a big ship. There was a father and a mother. They had four children. Their names were Fritz, Ernest, Jack and Franz. Two dogs, Turk and Juno, were with them. The family wanted a new life far away.

One night, a great storm came. The ship hit a rock and broke. The other men left in small boats. They did not wait for the family. "What can we do?" asked Jack. "We must not lose hope," said the father.

In the morning, the father looked out. He saw land not far away. "We can go there," said the father. "But how?" asked Fritz. So the family made a raft. They all went to the land on it. The dogs came after them.

The land was an island. No people lived there. "This is our home now," said the mother. Later, the father and Fritz went back. They took the raft out to the ship. They brought food, tools and clothes. They brought some animals too.

The family needed a good home. Then they found a very big tree. "Let's live up there!" said the father. They made a tree house high in it. They called it Falconhurst. The children loved their new house.

The family worked hard every day. They put seeds in the ground. Soon food began to grow. Ernest liked to learn about everything. Jack and Franz played with the animals. Fritz went far across the island.

Many years went by. The children grew big and strong. Life on the island was good.

Then one morning, a ship came. A man came to see them. "You can come home with us," he said. The family talked for a long time. "The island is our home," said the father. "We want to stay here." So the ship went away without them. The family stayed on their island together.

## Level A2

Long ago, a family left their country and went to sea. The father and mother had four sons. Fritz was the oldest and the strongest. The second son, Ernest, loved books and learning. Jack loved games and fun. Franz was the youngest, still a little boy. Their two big dogs, Turk and Juno, were with them.

One night, a storm came. For days the wind did not stop. Then the ship hit some rocks near land. Water started to come in. The other men got into the small boats and left. In the dark, they forgot the family. The mother held little Franz close. "We are still together," said the father. "God is with us."

In the morning the wind stopped. The father saw an island not far away. He and the boys made a raft from things on the ship. The whole family got on it. Together they moved across the water. The dogs came after them.

On the island they found good water to drink. That night they slept on the ground near the sea. The father and Fritz went back to the ship many times. They brought tools, food, seeds and clothes. They brought the animals too, like the cow and the goats. Each trip was hard work, but it helped the family a lot.

The mother did not like sleeping on the ground. "We need a better place for the night," she said. Near the forest, the family found some very big trees. The father chose the biggest one. They built a tree house high up in it. They made a long ladder to get up and down. They called their new home Falconhurst. At night, everyone slept well up in the tree.

Every day there was new work. The family planted the seeds, and soon they had a big garden. The cow and the goats ate the green plants near the house. Ernest learned the names of plants and stars. Jack and Franz looked after the animals. Fritz went across the island and found new places.

One year the rain came for many weeks. The tree house was too cold. Then Fritz and Jack found a big cave in the rocks. The family made it into a second home. They lived there during the cold months.

The years went by, ten in all. The boys grew into strong young men. Even Franz was not little now. The family worked hard, and they were happy.

Then one morning, they saw a ship near the island. It was an English ship. Its captain came to see them. "I can take you all home," he said. The family talked about it for a long time. "Our home is here now," said the father. "We are happy on this island," said the mother. "We want to stay." So the ship left without them, and the family stayed together.

## Level B1

Many years ago, a Swiss family set out on a long journey across the sea. The father was a pastor, a calm man who trusted in God. His wife was kind and sensible, and she was always ready to work. They had four sons who were very different from each other. Fritz, the oldest, was strong and quick with his hands. The second son, Ernest, was thoughtful and loved to read. Jack was bold and full of jokes. Little Franz was the youngest, and everyone looked after him. Two large dogs, Turk and Juno, traveled with them.

For six days a wild storm was blowing, and the ship was far from its course. One morning, while huge waves were crashing over the deck, the ship struck a rock. Water began to pour in through the broken wood. In the confusion, the sailors jumped into the boats and rowed away. Nobody remembered the family below deck. When the father came up, the boats were already disappearing into the dark sea. "Be brave," he told his wife and sons. "The ship is sitting firmly on the rock, and it will not sink yet."

By evening the storm was over, and they could see an island with green trees. In the ship, the father and the boys found some empty barrels. They sawed them in half and fixed them to long boards. In this way they made a raft of tubs. "It looks like a row of washtubs!" laughed Jack, but it floated well. The next morning the whole family climbed in, and the dogs swam beside them. Carefully they rowed through a gap in the rocks and reached a quiet bay.

On the shore they found a stream of fresh water. They built a tent out of an old sail. The next day, the father and Fritz returned to the wreck. Over many trips, they brought back tools, seeds, food and clothes. They also saved the animals which were still on board. There was a cow, a donkey, some goats, sheep, pigs and hens. The father tied floats to the larger animals, and they swam behind the raft. Every trip was hard work, but each one made life on the island easier.

The mother wanted a safer place to sleep than the open beach. While he was exploring with Fritz, the father found some giant trees. Their roots were like walls, and their lowest branches were high above the ground. "We will build our home up there," he said. The family used ropes, wood and tools from the ship. They built a tree house in the strongest tree. Every night they climbed up a long rope ladder and pulled it up behind them. They named their new home Falconhurst. The boys thought it was the best house in the world.

Soon life on the island had a steady rhythm. The family planted seeds, built fences and made a small farm. The cow gave milk, the hens laid eggs, and the goats wandered among the trees. Ernest studied the plants and animals and often told the others their names. Jack and Franz looked after the animals. A young monkey also came to live with them and became the family's favorite pet. Fritz explored far along the coast and found forests, rivers and green valleys. Each discovery brought something useful, like salt, cotton, fruit or a new kind of wood.

In the first rainy season, the tree house became cold and damp. The family needed a stronger shelter. One day, Fritz and Jack were digging into a rock wall near the shore. Suddenly their tools broke through into a large cave. Inside, the walls shone like glass, and the air was dry. The family turned the cave into their winter home. It had rooms for sleeping, cooking and storing food. After that, they spent the wet months in the cave and the dry months at Falconhurst.

Year after year passed, and ten years went by. The boys grew into tall, skilled young men. Even little Franz was now nearly grown. The farm was rich, the store rooms were full, and the family was content. Sometimes they talked about Switzerland, but the island felt more and more like home.

One morning Fritz heard a loud bang from the sea. Soon an English ship appeared and dropped anchor in the bay. The captain was amazed to find a whole family there. "I can take you all back to Europe," he offered. The family talked for a long time that evening. At last the father said, "We have built our life here, and we are happy." "Falconhurst is our home, and we will stay," said the mother. The ship sailed away the next day. The family stayed together on the island they loved.

## Level B2

Long ago, a Swiss pastor sold everything he owned and set out with his family for a distant colony. With him traveled his wife, a practical and steady woman, and their four sons. Fritz, the eldest, was strong, quick and eager for adventure. Ernest was a quiet boy who preferred books to hard work. Jack was bold and cheerful and never stayed still for long, and little Franz followed his brothers everywhere. Two big dogs, Turk and Juno, completed the party.

Their voyage ended in disaster. For six days a violent storm drove the ship far off its course. No one on board knew where they were. Then, with a terrible crash, the ship ran onto a reef and stuck there. It leaned to one side as the sea poured in. In the panic, the crew lowered the boats and rowed away without a backward look. When the father climbed on deck, he saw them vanishing among the waves. He realized that his family had been forgotten. He hid his fear and spoke calmly to his wife and sons. "The ship is wedged firmly between the rocks, so it will not sink tonight," he said. "Let us rest, trust in God and think clearly in the morning."

At dawn the wind had dropped, and through the mist they could see a green coast. Searching the ship, the father and the boys found several large barrels. They sawed them in half and fastened the eight tubs side by side between long planks. In this way they built a strange but sturdy raft. Jack joked that they were going to sea in their washing, yet the little craft floated well. The family climbed in with their most precious supplies. The two dogs refused to be left behind and swam alongside. Carefully the father steered through a gap in the reef into a calm bay. There a clear stream ran down to the sand.

That first night they slept in a tent made from a sail. While the others stayed on the shore, the father and Fritz rowed back to the wreck again and again. They carried away tools, seeds, cooking pots, clothes, sacks of grain and anything else that might be useful. The animals on board were rescued as well. The father tied floats around the cow, the donkey and the goats. The creatures swam to shore behind the raft, while the hens traveled in baskets. Each journey was exhausting, but each one made the family less afraid of the months ahead.

The mother soon decided that the beach was no place to raise four boys. On an early expedition, the father and Fritz had discovered a grove of enormous trees along the coast. Now the whole family moved there. The trees were so wide that their roots formed walls, and their lowest branches spread far above the ground. Using rope, planks and tools from the ship, the family built a platform high in the largest tree. They covered it with sailcloth to keep off the sun and rain. A long rope ladder led up to it, and every evening the last climber drew it up. They called their airy new home Falconhurst. From its height they could watch the sea and the forest at the same time.

The months that followed were full of work, but also of discovery. The family cleared land, sowed their seeds and fenced in fields, so the island slowly became a farm. The cow gave milk, the hens laid eggs, and young goats and piglets were soon running about. Ernest studied every new plant and creature. His careful knowledge often saved the others from eating something harmful. Jack and Franz were put in charge of the animals. A young monkey wandered out of the forest and attached itself to the family. Fritz ranged further than the others, crossing rivers and climbing ridges. He returned from each journey with news of forests, salt, cotton plants and new fruit.

When the first rainy season arrived, the family learned how harsh the island could be. For weeks the rain fell without stopping, and Falconhurst became cold, damp and miserable. The father decided they needed a shelter cut into stone. So the boys began to dig into a rock face near the shore. After a few days, Jack's tool suddenly broke through into a vast cave. To their astonishment, its walls glittered with crystals. They made the cave into a winter house, with rooms for sleeping, working, cooking and storing their harvest. After that they spent the wet season in the cave and returned to Falconhurst whenever the weather was fine.

Years passed in this way, and the island gave the family everything they needed. Ten years after the shipwreck, the boys had become capable young men. Franz, once the baby of the family, was nearly as tall as his father. Their fields were green, their stores were full, and they had built bridges, paths and gardens. They still spoke of Switzerland, but the old life now seemed like a dream. More and more, the island felt like home.

One morning, Fritz heard a deep boom across the water and rushed to tell the others. A few hours later, an English ship sailed into the bay and dropped anchor. Its captain was rowed ashore, and he could hardly believe what he saw. There was a farm, a cave house, a home in the trees and a healthy, cheerful family. They had built it all with their own hands. "If you wish, I will take every one of you back to Europe," he said. That evening the family talked for a long time about what they should do. Finally, the father spoke for them all. The island had given them a life they would not exchange for any other, he said. "This is our home now," said the mother quietly, "and we would like to stay." When the English ship sailed away, the family stood together on the shore and watched it go. They knew they were exactly where they belonged.

## Level C1

In the days when ships still took months to reach the far colonies, a Swiss pastor sold his house and embarked with his family for a new world. He was a patient, thoughtful man whose faith rarely wavered, and his wife was the kind of woman who met every difficulty by rolling up her sleeves. Their four sons could hardly have been less alike. Fritz, the eldest, was strong and restless, always the first to pick up a tool, while Ernest was a dreamy scholar who would rather read about a thing than do it. Jack was a cheerful, reckless boy who treated life as a game, and little Franz, the youngest, trotted after his brothers wherever they went. Two great dogs, Turk and Juno, completed the household.

The voyage that was meant to carry them to a new life nearly ended it. For six days a storm drove the ship before it, so far off its course that even the officers could no longer say where they were. Then, with a jolt that threw everyone off their feet, the vessel struck a reef and lodged there, its timbers groaning as the sea forced its way inside. In the chaos the crew lowered the boats and pulled away into the spray, and not one of them remembered the passengers below. When the father reached the deck, the boats were already small shapes among the waves, and he understood that his family was alone. He kept his dismay to himself. "The ship is jammed between the rocks and will hold until morning," he told them, with a steadiness he did not entirely feel. "Let us pray, and sleep if we can, and in daylight we shall see what God has left us."

Dawn brought a calmer sea and, through the thinning mist, the sight of a green coast fringed with palms. Rummaging through the hold, the father and the older boys found a number of empty casks, which they sawed in half and fastened side by side between long planks. The result was the oddest raft any of them had ever seen, a row of eight tubs bobbing in the water. Jack declared that they were setting out to sea in the laundry. Yet the strange craft floated, and when the family had loaded it with food, tools and a few precious belongings, they climbed in and pushed off, the dogs swimming stubbornly behind. Steering through a narrow gap in the reef, the father brought them into a sheltered bay where a clear stream ran down across white sand.

They passed their first night under a tent stitched from sailcloth, and it was there, around a small fire, that the real work of survival began to be planned. Since the wreck still held most of what they owned, the father and Fritz returned to it again and again over the following days. Load after load came back on the raft: hammers and saws, sacks of seed and grain, cooking pots, clothing, canvas, rope and all the small treasures of a ship's stores. Nor were the animals forgotten. Having tied floats of cork around the cow, the donkey, the goats and the sheep, the father coaxed them overboard, and the little herd paddled after the raft. The hens, rather more dignified, made the crossing in baskets. Each trip left father and son exhausted, yet with every load the island seemed a little less like a prison and a little more like a place where people might live.

It was the mother who insisted that the open beach was no place to bring up four boys. On one of their early explorations, the father and Fritz had come upon a grove of colossal trees a few miles along the coast. Their roots rose from the earth like the buttresses of a cathedral, and their first branches spread far overhead. The family moved there, and with timber and rope saved from the wreck they built a wide platform among the boughs of the largest tree. They roofed it with sailcloth against the sun and rain. A long rope ladder was the only way up, and each night the last climber hauled it in, so that the whole household slept suspended between earth and sky. They named the place Falconhurst, and to the boys it was simply the finest house that had ever existed.

The months that followed were crowded with labor, but they were also, unexpectedly, a time of wonder. Land was cleared, seed was sown and fences went up, and by degrees the wild shore became a working farm. The cow gave milk, the hens laid eggs, and before long kids and piglets were tumbling about among the roots of Falconhurst. Ernest, who had once seemed more interested in books than in the world, turned out to be the family's naturalist. His patient knowledge of plants spared the others more than one unwise meal. Jack and Franz took charge of the animals, to which they soon added a young monkey that wandered out of the forest and adopted the family with alarming confidence. Fritz, meanwhile, ranged ever further, fording rivers and climbing ridges. He came home from each expedition with news of salt pans, cotton bushes, unfamiliar fruits and valleys no one had ever farmed.

The island's gentleness, however, had its limits, and the first rainy season taught them so. For weeks the rain came down without pause, and Falconhurst, so delightful in the sun, became a damp, dripping perch where nothing ever dried. Determined to find something sturdier, the father set the boys to cut a storeroom into a rock face near the shore. They had been working for some days when Jack's tool suddenly met no resistance at all and plunged through into darkness. Behind the broken stone they found a vast cave whose walls glittered with crystals like frost. Over the following seasons they turned it into a true winter house, with rooms for sleeping, cooking, working and storing the harvest. A front wall of stone and timber kept out the weather. From then on the family wintered in the rock and summered in the trees, moving between their two homes as naturally as the birds.

So the years went by, until ten had passed since the night of the wreck. The boys had grown into capable young men, each with his own gifts, and even Franz, once the baby of the household, now stood almost level with his father. Bridges spanned the streams, paths linked the fields to the cave and the cave to Falconhurst, and the storerooms held more than the family could eat in a year. Switzerland still came up in their conversations, but it had become a gentle memory rather than a longing. None of them could say exactly when the island had stopped being a refuge and become simply home.

Then one morning Fritz, out early along the shore, heard a dull boom rolling across the water, and ran back to raise the alarm. Before noon an English ship had entered the bay and dropped anchor. Her captain was rowed ashore to find out who on earth had lit fires on an island that appeared on no chart. What he found left him almost speechless: fields and orchards, a house cut into living rock, a dwelling in the treetops. Stranger still was the sunburnt, contented family who had built it all with their own hands and the remains of a wreck. He offered, without hesitation, to carry every one of them back to Europe. That evening, long after the boys had fallen silent, the father and mother sat talking under the great tree, weighing the old world against the new. In the morning the father gave the captain their answer: Providence had brought them to this shore, and they had no wish to leave the life it had given them. "Everything we love is here," said the mother simply, looking up at the platform in the branches. When the English ship weighed anchor a few days later, the whole family stood together on the sand to watch her go. Then, without a backward glance, they turned and walked home.
