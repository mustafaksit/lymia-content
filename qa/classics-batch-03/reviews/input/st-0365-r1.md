# The Swineherd

Summary: A poor prince sends a proud princess a rose and a nightingale, but she does not want them.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): swineherd, rose, nightingale, pot, rattle

## Level A1

A young prince lived in a small country. He did not have much money. But he wanted to marry the princess. Her father was the Emperor.

The young man had a rose tree. It gave only one rose every five years. He also had a nightingale. It knew all the music in the world. He sent both to the princess.

But she did not like them. "It is only a real rose!" she said. "And it is only a real nightingale. Send it away." She did not want the young man.

But he did not stop. He made his face black. So no one knew him. Then he asked the Emperor for work. "Look after my pigs," said the Emperor. So the young man became the Emperor's swineherd.

Then he made a little pot. It could play an old tune.

The princess walked by with her women. She heard the pot. "I know that one!" she said. "Go and ask the price of the pot." One of her women went to the swineherd. "Ten kisses from the princess," said the swineherd. The woman went back and told her. The princess did not like this. But she really wanted the pot. So her women stood around her. The swineherd got ten kisses on the cheek. And she got the pot.

Next, the swineherd made a rattle. It played every dance in the world. She wanted it, too. But now he wanted one hundred kisses. She said yes. Again, her women stood around her.

The Emperor saw the women and went down. There he saw the princess and the swineherd. It was kiss number eighty-six! The Emperor was very angry. He sent them both out of his country.

Now she stood there with nothing. "Why did I say no to the prince?" The swineherd took the black off his face. Now he looked like a prince again. "I do not want you now," he said. "You did not like a real rose. But you kissed a swineherd for a toy!" Then he went home and closed his door. She stood outside, all on her own.

## Level A2

There was once a prince with a small country. He was poor, but he was a good man. He wanted to marry the daughter of the Emperor.

The young man had a rose tree. It gave only one rose every five years. He also had a nightingale. It could sing every song in the world. He sent them to the princess in two boxes.

She opened the first box and saw the rose. "But it is real!" she said. She wanted a made rose, not a real one. Then the nightingale began to sing. "Is this a real nightingale?" asked the princess. "Yes, it is real," said the young man's people. "Then let it fly away," said the Emperor's daughter. She did not want to meet the young man.

But he did not stop there. He made his face black and put on old clothes. Now nobody could see a prince in him. He asked the Emperor for a job. "I need someone for my pigs," said the Emperor. So the young man became the Emperor's swineherd.

In the evening, he made a little pot. When the water in it got hot, it played an old song.

The princess walked past with her women. She heard the song. "I know that song!" she said. "I can play it myself. Go and ask the price of the pot." One of her women went to the swineherd. "Ten kisses from the princess," said the swineherd. The woman went back and told her. "He asks too much!" said the Emperor's daughter. But she wanted the pot very much. So her women stood around her, and nobody could see. The swineherd got ten kisses on the cheek. And the Emperor's daughter got the pot.

The next day, the swineherd made a rattle. When you turned it, it played every dance in the world. "How lovely!" said the Emperor's daughter. "Go and ask the price. But tell him no more kisses." The woman went to him and came back. "He wants one hundred kisses from you," she said. "Then I give him ten," said the Emperor's daughter. "My women can give him the rest." "But we do not want to!" said her women. And the swineherd said no to this plan. So once more, the women stood around her.

The Emperor saw a lot of women near the animals. He went down, but the women did not see him. Then the Emperor saw his daughter and the swineherd. It was kiss number eighty-six! The Emperor was very angry. He sent them both out of his country.

Now she stood outside in the rain. "Why did I say no to the good prince?" she said. Then the swineherd went behind a tree. He took the black off his face and changed his clothes. Now he was a prince again. "I do not think much of you now," he said. "You did not want a good prince. You did not understand the rose or the nightingale. But you kissed a swineherd for a toy!" Then he went into his own country and closed the door. She stood outside, all alone.

## Level B1

There was once a poor prince who had a very small kingdom. Still, he wanted to marry, and he decided to ask the Emperor's daughter.

On the grave of the prince's father, there grew a rose tree. It had only one flower every five years. But its smell was so sweet that people forgot all their troubles. The prince also had a nightingale which could sing every lovely tune in the world. He put the rose and the bird into two silver boxes for the princess.

The Emperor told his servants to carry the boxes into the great hall. The princess was playing there with her ladies, and she clapped her hands. She was hoping for a little cat, but out came the rose. The princess touched it and almost started to cry. "Oh, Papa!" she said. "It is not a made rose at all. It is just a real one!" Then the nightingale sang so beautifully that the old Emperor cried like a child. "I hope this is not a real bird," said the princess. "Yes, it is a real bird," said the men who brought the gifts. "Then let it fly away," said the princess. She refused to let the prince visit her.

But the prince did not give up so easily. He made his face brown and black and pulled his cap down low. Then he went to the Emperor and asked for a job. "I need someone to look after the pigs," said the Emperor. So the prince became the Emperor's swineherd and got a poor little room by the pigs.

He worked all day, and by the evening he made a pretty little pot. It had bells around it, and when it boiled, the bells played an old song. With a finger in the steam, you could smell the dinner in every kitchen in town.

The princess was walking past with her ladies when she heard the song. She looked very pleased, because it was the only tune she could play on the piano. "That is my song!" she said. "Go and ask the swineherd the price of the pot." One of the ladies went into his room. "Ten kisses from the princess," said the swineherd. "Good heavens!" said the lady, and she ran back to tell the princess. "How rude!" said the princess, and she walked on. But after a few steps, the bells played again. "Ask him to take ten kisses from my ladies," she said. "No, thank you," said the swineherd. "Ten kisses from the princess, or I keep my pot." "How annoying!" said the princess. "But you must all stand around me, so that nobody sees." The ladies stood around her. The swineherd got his ten kisses on the cheek, and the princess got the pot.

The pot was boiling all evening and all the next day. Soon the princess and her ladies knew what every family in town was having for dinner. "We know who is having soup and pancakes!" the ladies laughed.

Next the swineherd made a rattle, which played every dance in the world. "That is wonderful!" said the princess. "Go and ask the price, but no more kisses." The lady came back. "He wants one hundred kisses from the princess," she said. "He must be mad!" said the princess, and she walked away. But after a few steps, she stopped. "He can have ten kisses from me, like yesterday," she said. "My ladies will give him the rest." "But we do not want to!" said the ladies. "Nonsense!" said the princess. "Remember that I give you food and money." But the swineherd did not change his mind. "One hundred kisses from the princess, or we each keep our own things," he said. "Stand around me!" said the princess, and the kissing began.

The Emperor came out on his balcony. "What are all those ladies doing down by the pigs?" he said. He hurried down and walked very quietly into the yard. The ladies were busy counting the kisses, so they did not see him. Then the Emperor saw his daughter with the swineherd. It was the eighty-sixth kiss! "Out!" said the Emperor, who was very angry. He sent both the princess and the swineherd out of his empire.

There the princess stood in the rain, crying. "Why did I not take that handsome prince?" she said. The swineherd went behind a tree and washed the brown and black from his face. He threw off his old clothes and stepped out in the fine clothes of a prince. "I think very little of you now," he said. "You did not want an honest prince. You did not understand the rose or the nightingale. But you were happy to kiss a swineherd for a toy." Then he went into his kingdom and closed the door behind him. She could stand outside in the rain and sing the old song from the pot.

## Level B2

There was once a poor prince whose kingdom was very small, though it was just large enough to marry on. He was bold enough to ask the Emperor's daughter to be his wife. Hundreds of princesses would gladly have said yes to him, but listen to what this one did.

On the grave of the prince's father grew a rose tree that flowered only once every five years. Even then it carried just one rose, but its smell was so sweet that anyone who breathed it forgot every sorrow. The prince also owned a nightingale that could sing as if every lovely tune in the world lived in its little throat. He placed the rose and the nightingale in two large silver boxes and sent them to the princess.

The Emperor had the boxes carried into the great hall, where the princess was playing with her ladies. When she saw the boxes, she clapped her hands, hoping that a little kitten was inside, but out came the rose. "Oh, how prettily it is made!" cried all the ladies. The princess touched it and was close to tears. "Oh, Papa, it is not made at all," she said. "It is just a real rose!" "Let us see what is in the other box before we lose our tempers," said the Emperor. Then the nightingale came out and sang so sweetly that the old Emperor cried like a child. "I hope it is not a real bird," said the princess. "Yes, it is a real bird," said the messengers who had brought it. "Then let it fly away," said the princess, and she refused to let the prince come to see her.

The prince, however, was not so easily put off. He rubbed his face with brown and black, pulled his cap down low and asked the Emperor for work. "I need someone to look after the pigs, because we have a great many of them," said the Emperor. So the prince was made the Emperor's swineherd and was given a poor little room down by the pigs. He worked there all day, and by evening he had made a pretty little pot with bells all around it. As soon as the pot began to boil, the bells rang out a sad old tune which says that everything is gone. Better still, a finger held in its steam let you smell what was cooking in every kitchen in town.

Soon afterwards the princess came walking past with her ladies, and when she heard the song, she stopped. She looked delighted, because it was the only tune she could play, and she played it with one finger. "Why, that is my song!" she said. "Go in and ask the swineherd what the pot costs." One of the ladies put on wooden shoes and went in. "Ten kisses from the princess," said the swineherd, "and I will not take less." "Good heavens!" said the lady, and she carried the answer back. "How rude he is!" said the princess, and walked on. But after a few steps the bells rang out so sweetly that she had to stop. "Ask him if he will take ten kisses from my ladies instead," she said. "No, thank you," said the swineherd. "Ten kisses from the princess, or I keep my pot." "How tiresome!" said the princess. "But then you must all stand around me, so that nobody can see." The ladies stood around her and spread out their skirts, and the swineherd got his ten kisses on the cheek. The princess got the pot.

What fun they had with it! The pot was kept boiling all evening and all the next day, and soon they knew every family's dinner. The ladies danced and clapped their hands, crying, "We know who is having soup and pancakes today!" "Yes, but keep it to yourselves," said the princess, "for I am the Emperor's daughter."

The swineherd did not let a single day pass without making something new. Next he made a rattle, and when it was swung around, it played every waltz and polka since the world began. "That is superb!" said the princess as she was passing. "Go and ask what it costs, but there will be no more kisses." The lady soon returned. "He wants one hundred kisses from the princess," she said. "He must be mad!" said the princess, and she walked away, but after a few steps she stopped. "One must encourage art," she said, "and I am the Emperor's daughter. Tell him he can have ten kisses from me, as yesterday, and the rest from my ladies." "Oh, but we would rather not!" said the ladies. "Nonsense! If I can kiss him, so can you," said the princess. "Remember that I give you food and wages." So the lady went back once more, but the swineherd would not change his mind. "One hundred kisses from the princess," he said, "or each of us keeps what we have." "Stand around me!" said the princess, and the kissing began.

The Emperor stepped out onto his balcony and saw a crowd of ladies down by the pigs. He rubbed his eyes, put on his glasses and hurried down to see what they were doing. When he reached the yard, he walked very softly. The ladies were so busy counting the kisses, to make sure it was all fair, that they did not notice him. "What is this?" said the Emperor, as he saw the princess and the swineherd together. It was exactly the eighty-sixth kiss. "Out with you both!" he said, for he was very angry, and the two were sent out of his empire.

There stood the princess, crying, while the swineherd grumbled and the rain poured down. "Oh, how unhappy I am!" she said. "If only I had taken that handsome prince!" The swineherd stepped behind a tree, wiped the brown and black from his face and threw off his ragged clothes. Then he came out in the fine clothes of a prince, looking so splendid that the princess had to curtsy. "I have come to think very little of you," he said. "You would not have an honest prince, and you did not understand the rose or the nightingale. But you were willing to kiss a swineherd for a toy. Now you have what you deserve." With that he went into his own kingdom, shut the door and bolted it. She could stand outside and sing the old song about everything being gone.

## Level C1

There was once a prince who had very little money and a kingdom so small that it hardly deserved the name, though it was still quite large enough to marry on. Marry he meant to, and he was bold enough to set his heart on nobody less than the Emperor's daughter. There were hundreds of princesses who would have accepted him gladly, but it is worth hearing what this particular one had to say.

On the grave of the prince's father there grew a rose tree, and a most remarkable tree it was. It bloomed only once in every five years, and even then it bore a single flower, yet that flower gave off a scent so sweet that whoever breathed it forgot every sorrow. The prince also owned a nightingale which sang as though all the loveliest melodies in the world were kept inside its small throat. Both of these treasures, he decided, should go to the princess, so he laid them in two large silver caskets and sent them off to her.

The Emperor had the caskets carried into the great hall, where the princess was playing at receiving visitors with her ladies-in-waiting. At the sight of the caskets she clapped her hands in delight, quite certain that a little kitten was hidden inside one of them, but what came out was the rose. "Oh, how prettily it has been made!" cried the ladies. "It is more than pretty," said the Emperor, "it is positively charming." The princess, however, touched it, and her lip began to tremble as though she might burst into tears. "Oh, Papa, how dreadful!" she said, "it has not been made at all, it is just a real rose!" "How dreadful," echoed every one of the ladies, "it is just a real one!"

"Let us look in the other casket before we lose our tempers," said the Emperor. Out came the nightingale, singing so sweetly that for a moment not a single unkind word could be found to say about it. The ladies, who all spoke French, each a little worse than the next, praised it in that language. An old gentleman of the court remarked that the bird reminded him of the late Empress's music box, with precisely the same tone and precisely the same phrasing. "Yes, indeed," said the Emperor, and he wept like a little child. "I should hope it is not a real bird," said the princess. "Oh yes, it is a real bird," said the messengers who had brought it. "In that case, let it fly away," said the princess, and she would not hear of the prince being allowed to call on her.

The prince, however, was not a young man to be put off so easily. He smeared his face with brown and black, pulled his cap well down over his eyes, and knocked at the palace door. "Good day, Emperor," he said; "might there be some work for me here at the palace?" "Well, a great many people come asking that," replied the Emperor. "But let me think, for I do happen to need someone to look after the pigs, of which we have an extraordinary number." So the prince was appointed imperial swineherd and given a wretched little room down beside the pigsty, where he sat and worked from morning until night. By evening he had made a charming little pot, hung all around with tiny bells. The moment it came to the boil, the bells began to ring out an old, sad tune whose words say that everything is gone. Yet the cleverest thing about it was that anyone who held a finger in its steam could smell at once what was being cooked on every stove in the town.

Not long afterwards the princess came strolling by with all her ladies, and on hearing the tune she stopped and looked thoroughly pleased, for she could play that very piece herself. It was, in fact, the only piece she could play, and she managed it with one finger. "Why, that is my tune!" she exclaimed, "so he must be a remarkably well-educated swineherd. Go in, one of you, and ask him what the instrument costs." So one of the ladies had to go in, though first she put on a pair of wooden clogs. "What do you want for that pot?" she asked. "Ten kisses from the princess," said the swineherd. "Heaven preserve us!" said the lady. "I will not take a single one less," said the swineherd. When the lady brought back this reply, the princess tossed her head. "What a rude creature he is!" she said, and walked on. She had gone only a few steps, however, when the little bells rang out so prettily that she was obliged to stop. "Ask him whether he will take ten kisses from my ladies instead," she said. "No, thank you," said the swineherd; "ten kisses from the princess, or I keep my pot." "How very tiresome!" said the princess, "but then you must all stand around me, so that nobody can possibly see." The ladies stood around her and spread out their skirts, and behind that wall of skirts the swineherd received his ten kisses on the cheek, while the princess received the pot.

What delight there was after that! The pot was kept boiling all evening and the whole of the following day. Before long there was not a stove in the town, from the chamberlain's to the shoemaker's, whose secrets they did not know. The ladies danced about, clapping their hands and crying, "We know who is having sweet soup and pancakes, and who is having porridge and chops!" "How extremely interesting," said the chief lady-in-waiting. "Yes, but hold your tongues about it," said the princess, "for I am the Emperor's daughter."

The swineherd, who was of course really the prince, never let a day go by without making something, and next he made a rattle. Whenever anyone swung it around, it played every waltz, jig and polka that had been heard since the creation of the world. "Now that is superb," said the princess as she passed by; "I have never heard a lovelier composition. Go in and ask him what it costs, but mind, there are to be no more kisses." Presently the lady returned and reported, "He wants a hundred kisses from the princess." The princess declared that the man must be mad and walked away, though after a few steps she came to a halt. "One really ought to encourage the arts," she said, "and after all, I am the Emperor's daughter. Tell him he shall have ten kisses from me, just as yesterday, and he may collect the rest from my ladies." "Oh, but we would much rather not!" said the ladies. "What nonsense," said the princess; "if I can kiss him, then so can you, and please remember that I am the one paying for your board and your wages." So the lady had to trudge back across the yard once again. "A hundred kisses from the princess," said the swineherd, "or each of us keeps what we have." "Stand around me, then!" said the princess, and so they stood, and the kissing began.

"Now what is all that commotion down by the pigsty?" asked the Emperor, who had stepped out onto his balcony. He rubbed his eyes and put on his spectacles. "Why, it is the ladies-in-waiting at some game or other," he said; "I had better go down and see." He pulled up the backs of his slippers, which he had trodden down at the heels, and off he went in a great hurry. Once in the yard, he crept along very quietly, while the ladies were far too busy counting the kisses, so as to make sure everything was done fairly, to notice anyone approaching. "What on earth is this?" he cried, for he had caught sight of his daughter and the swineherd, and at that exact moment the swineherd was receiving his eighty-sixth kiss. "Out with you!" said the Emperor, who was very angry indeed, and both the princess and the swineherd were sent out of his empire.

So there the princess stood, weeping, while the swineherd grumbled beside her and the rain came pouring down. "Oh, what a miserable creature I am!" she said. "If only I had taken that handsome prince! Oh, how unhappy I am!" The swineherd stepped behind a tree, wiped the brown and black from his face and threw off his ragged clothes. When he stepped out again, he was dressed as a prince and looked so splendid that the princess could not help dropping a curtsy. "I have come to think very little of you," he said. "You would not have an honest prince, you had no understanding of the rose or the nightingale, yet you were ready to kiss a swineherd for a toy. Now you have exactly what you deserve." With that he went into his own kingdom and shut the door behind him, and he bolted it, too. She was left free to stand outside and sing, if she wished, the old tune about everything being gone.
