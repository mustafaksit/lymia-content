# Mrs. Wiggs of the Cabbage Patch

Summary: A poor but cheerful mother in Kentucky always finds the bright side, even in a hard winter.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): cabbage, basket, horse

## Level A1

Mrs. Wiggs lived in a town in Kentucky. Her part of town was very poor. People called it the Cabbage Patch. Four children lived with her. They were Billy, Asia, Australia and Europena.

The family did not have much money. But Mrs. Wiggs was a happy woman. "There is always a good side," she said.

It was a cold day before Christmas. There was no food in the house. Billy went out to look for work. Mrs. Wiggs told the girls a story. The girls laughed and forgot about food.

Then a young woman came to the door. She had a big basket in her hands. "My name is Lucy Olcott," she said. Lucy's family had a lot of money. In the basket was good food. "Thank you!" said Mrs. Wiggs. After that, Lucy often came to visit.

But Lucy had a problem. She and a young man loved each other. Then they said bad things to each other. Now they did not talk at all. Lucy told Mrs. Wiggs about it. "Wait and see," said Mrs. Wiggs.

Later, Billy found an old horse. It was very weak and could not stand. Nobody wanted the horse. So Billy took it home. He gave it food and water every day. Slowly the horse got strong. Then Billy and the horse carried things. People paid Billy for this work.

One evening the family went to a show. It was the first show for the children. Europena laughed and laughed.

The young man came to the Cabbage Patch. He wanted to help the family too. Lucy was there on the same day. "Sit down and talk," said Mrs. Wiggs. So Lucy and the young man talked. Then they were friends again. Soon Lucy married him.

Life became better for the Wiggs family too. Billy worked with his horse every day. There was food on the table every night. Mrs. Wiggs looked at her children. "Good things come in the end," she said.

## Level A2

Mrs. Wiggs lived in a poor part of a town in Kentucky. People called this place the Cabbage Patch. Her little house stood next to the train line. Her husband died many years before. Her oldest child worked far away in another town. Four children lived with her: Billy, Asia, Australia and Europena. The family never had much money. But Mrs. Wiggs had a happy heart. That year the cold came early. Just before Christmas, the house had almost no food. Billy went out every morning to look for work. He was only a boy, but he worked hard. The girls sat by the small fire and kept warm. Mrs. Wiggs sang songs to them and told them stories.

Then one afternoon a young woman came to the door. She held a big basket in her arms. "My name is Lucy Olcott," she said with a smile. Lucy came from a rich family in the city. Her basket was full of food for a Christmas dinner. There were warm clothes and small presents for the children too. The children shouted and danced around the room. "You are very kind," Mrs. Wiggs said. After that day, Lucy visited the family often. She liked Mrs. Wiggs and her happy way of seeing the world.

But Lucy had a sad secret. She loved a young man, and he loved her. One day they had a fight and said hard words. Now they did not speak to each other. Lucy told Mrs. Wiggs the whole story. "Wait and see. Things often come right in the end," said Mrs. Wiggs.

Some time later, there was a big fire in the town. The next day, Billy found an old horse in the street. It was very thin and weak, and nobody wanted it. Billy brought the horse home. Every day he gave it food and clean water. Slowly the old horse grew strong again. Soon Billy used the horse for work. He carried things for people in the town, and they paid him.

One evening, the family went to a play in the city. It was the first play of their lives. They saw bright lights, beautiful clothes and a big stage. Europena, the youngest girl, laughed from start to finish. On the way home, the children talked about the play.

In the spring, the young man came to the Cabbage Patch. He heard about the Wiggs family and wanted to help them too. Lucy was at the house that same day. Mrs. Wiggs looked at the two young people and smiled. "Sit down, both of you, and talk," she said. Lucy and the young man talked for a long time. At last, they were friends again. Soon after, they married.

Life became better for the Wiggs family too. Billy worked with his horse every day. The girls went to school in warm clothes. There was food on the table every night. Mrs. Wiggs looked at her happy children. "You see? Good things come in the end," she said.

## Level B1

In a town in Kentucky, there was a poor neighborhood that everyone called the Cabbage Patch. The houses there were small and crooked, and the train line ran right beside them. In one of these little houses lived Mrs. Wiggs. Her husband died years before, and money was always short. Her oldest boy was working far away in another town. At home, she had four younger children: Billy, Asia, Australia and Europena. Mrs. Wiggs liked big names, so she took the girls' names from her old school map.

Life in the Cabbage Patch was hard, but Mrs. Wiggs never let it make her sad. Whatever happened, she looked for the bright side. When the roof leaked, she said the rain was washing the floor for free. When there was no meat, she said a little soup was good for everyone. Her children laughed at her jokes even when they were hungry.

That winter was the hardest they could remember. Snow lay on the ground, and the wind came in through every crack. A few days before Christmas, there was almost nothing left to eat. Billy was going out every morning to look for small jobs. He was only a boy, but he carried coal and ran errands for a few cents. The girls stayed near the stove. Mrs. Wiggs told them stories to keep their minds off the cold.

One afternoon a young woman in a fine coat appeared at their door. She was carrying a large basket with a white cloth over it. "Good afternoon," she said shyly. "My name is Lucy Olcott, and I brought you a few things for Christmas." Inside the basket there was a whole dinner, with bread, fruit and cake. There were also warm stockings, mittens and small presents for each child. The children shouted with joy and danced around the kitchen. Mrs. Wiggs held Lucy's hand and thanked her again and again.

Lucy came from one of the richest families in the city, but she was lonely. In the Wiggs house she found something she was missing. She began to visit often, and soon she and Mrs. Wiggs were real friends. One day Lucy told her a secret. She was in love with a young man, and he loved her too. But they quarreled, and each of them was too proud to speak first. Mrs. Wiggs listened kindly. "Don't give up on him," she said. "Things have a way of coming right, sooner or later."

Not long after that, a big fire broke out in town. The next morning Billy found an old horse lying in the street near the burned stables. The poor animal was thin and weak, and its owner did not want it any more. Billy asked if he could have it, and the man said yes. With a lot of pushing and pulling, Billy brought the horse home. For weeks, he fed it, brushed it and gave it fresh water. His sisters helped him, and slowly the old horse grew strong again. Soon Billy was using it to deliver goods, and people paid him well.

One evening Mrs. Wiggs took the children to a play in the city. It was the first time any of them saw a real theater. They stared at the red seats, the bright lights and the huge stage. Europena, the youngest, laughed so much that people turned around to look at her. On the long walk home, the children acted out every part of the play.

In the spring, the young man came to the Cabbage Patch. He heard about the Wiggs family and wanted to help them too. By chance, Lucy was visiting on the same day. For a moment the two of them just stood there, saying nothing. Mrs. Wiggs looked from one to the other and smiled. "Sit down, both of you," she said. "I will go out and look after the horse, and you can talk." They talked for a long time, and when they stood up, they were holding hands. Before the summer ended, Lucy and the young man got married.

Things got better for the Wiggs family as well. Billy was earning steady money with his horse. The girls went to school in warm clothes. There was always food on the table now. One evening Mrs. Wiggs sat on her doorstep and watched the sun go down. "You see, children?" she said. "Good things always come in the end, as long as we keep on hoping."

## Level B2

On the edge of a town in Kentucky, between the railroad tracks and the river, lay a crowded neighborhood. Everyone called it the Cabbage Patch. Its houses were small, crooked and patched with whatever boards their owners could find. Nobody there had much money, and nobody had less than Mrs. Wiggs. Her husband had died years earlier, leaving her with a pile of debts and a house full of children. Her oldest boy had gone to work in another town. Four children were still at home with her: Billy, Asia, Australia and Europena. Mrs. Wiggs was proud of the girls' names, which she had chosen from the geography book she used as a child.

What made Mrs. Wiggs remarkable was not her poverty but her cheerfulness. She believed that every trouble had a bright side, and that it was her job to find it. If the roof leaked, she put out a pan and said they were lucky to have water delivered free. If supper was only bread and soup, she called it a light meal, the kind rich ladies ate. Her children did not always believe her, but they laughed, and the laughing helped.

That winter tested even her good humor. The snow came early and stayed, and the wind found every gap in the walls. A few days before Christmas, the last of the coal was gone, and the cupboard was nearly empty. Billy, who was barely old enough to be called a young man, went out every morning in search of odd jobs. He shoveled snow, carried bundles and ran messages for a few cents at a time. Meanwhile, the girls huddled around the stove. Their mother told them long, silly stories to keep them from thinking about their stomachs.

One afternoon, a few days before Christmas, there was a knock at the door. On the step stood a young woman in an elegant coat, holding a basket almost too heavy for her. "I hope I'm not disturbing you," she said a little shyly. "My name is Lucy Olcott, and I thought you might like a few things for the holiday." The basket held a complete Christmas dinner, with bread, fruit, a cake and a box of candy. Beneath the food were warm stockings, mittens and a small present for each child. The children shouted so loudly that the neighbors came to see what had happened. Mrs. Wiggs pressed Lucy's hand between her own and told her she was an angel sent straight from heaven.

Lucy belonged to one of the wealthiest families in the city. Yet she had seldom felt as welcome anywhere as she did in that cramped little kitchen. She came back the following week, and the week after that, and gradually she and Mrs. Wiggs became close friends. On one of these visits, Lucy admitted that she was unhappy. She was in love with a young man, and he had loved her too, but they had quarreled bitterly over something small. Now each was waiting for the other to apologize, and neither would. Mrs. Wiggs listened without interrupting. "Pride is a cold blanket, honey," she said at last. "But don't you lose hope. Things have a way of working out if you give them time."

Some weeks later, a fire broke out in a stable on the other side of town. The next morning, while Billy was walking past the ruins, he noticed an old horse lying in the street. It was thin and weak, and its owner had already decided that it was not worth the trouble of keeping. Billy asked whether he could take it, and the man shrugged and said the animal was his. Getting the horse back to the Cabbage Patch took most of the day and every bit of Billy's patience. For weeks afterward he fed it, brushed its coat and made it a bed of straw in the shed. His sisters carried water and talked to it, and slowly the old horse grew strong again. By spring Billy had built a small cart, and he was earning real money delivering goods for the shopkeepers.

One evening Mrs. Wiggs announced that the family was going to the theater. None of the children had ever been inside one, and they could hardly sit still on the way there. They stared at the red velvet seats, the golden lamps and the great curtain that rose to show a painted world. Europena, the youngest, laughed so loudly at the funny parts that people in front of them turned around to look. All the way home, the children acted out the play in the street, each of them insisting on the best part.

One spring day, the young man Lucy loved came to the Cabbage Patch. He had heard about the Wiggs family and wanted to see whether he could help them as well. As it happened, Lucy was sitting in the kitchen at that very moment. For a long, awkward minute, the two of them simply looked at each other. Mrs. Wiggs, who understood everything at once, wiped her hands on her apron and smiled. "Now, you two sit right down," she said. "I'm going out to see to the horse, and I may be gone quite a while." When she came back, they were sitting side by side, and both of them were smiling. Before the summer was over, Lucy and the young man were married.

By then life had become easier for the Wiggs family too. Billy's horse and cart brought in steady money, and the girls went to school in warm coats and good shoes. There was food on the table every night, and coal in the shed for the next winter. One warm evening Mrs. Wiggs sat on her doorstep, watching the sun go down behind the railroad tracks. "Look at that, children," she said. "It seems that everything in the world comes right, if we only wait long enough."

## Level C1

On the ragged edge of a town in Kentucky, wedged between the railroad tracks and the muddy river road, sprawled a neighborhood known as the Cabbage Patch. Nobody could remember who had first called it that, but nobody had ever thought of a better name. Its cottages leaned against one another like tired old friends, their walls patched with scraps of tin, their yards given over to cabbages and washing lines. Of all the households there, few were poorer than that of Mrs. Wiggs, and none, it was generally agreed, was more cheerful.

Her husband had died some years before, leaving her little beyond a stack of unpaid bills and a house full of children. The eldest boy had gone off to work in another town, sending home what he could. It was therefore with four younger children that she faced the world: Billy, Asia, Australia and Europena. The girls' names were a matter of great pride to her, having been chosen from the geography book she had treasured as a schoolgirl. As she explained to anyone who asked, she could not give her daughters much, but she could at least give them the whole wide world.

What set Mrs. Wiggs apart was not her poverty, which she shared with everyone around her, but her stubborn conviction that every misfortune hid a blessing somewhere. One had only to be patient enough to search for it. When the roof leaked, she set a pan beneath the drip and remarked that it was a fine thing to have water brought right into the kitchen without carrying a single bucket. When supper shrank to bread and thin soup, she declared it the sort of light, elegant meal that fashionable ladies in the city paid good money for. Her children rarely believed a word of it, yet they laughed, and the laughter, as their mother well knew, did them more good than another spoonful of soup would have.

That winter put even her philosophy to the test. The snow arrived early and refused to leave, and the wind seemed to have learned the exact location of every crack in the walls. By the week before Christmas, the coal had run out, the cupboard held little more than a few potatoes, and the stove gave off more smoke than heat. Billy, scarcely old enough to be called a young man, set out every morning before dawn in search of odd jobs. He shoveled snow, carried bundles and ran errands for whatever few cents a shopkeeper felt like parting with. At home the girls huddled close to the stove, while Mrs. Wiggs spun stories so long and so ridiculous that, for an hour or two, nobody noticed how empty their stomachs were.

It was on one such afternoon, a few days before Christmas, that a knock sounded at the door. On the step stood a young woman in an elegant coat, her cheeks pink with cold, struggling with a basket so large that it seemed in danger of pulling her over. "Forgive me for coming without warning," she said, with a shyness that sat oddly on someone so well dressed. "My name is Lucy Olcott, and I hoped you might accept a few things for the holiday." Out of the basket came a whole Christmas dinner, followed by bread, oranges, a frosted cake and a box of candy. Beneath all that lay warm stockings, woolen mittens and a small wrapped present for each child. The shrieks that followed brought half the neighbors to their windows. Mrs. Wiggs, for once almost lost for words, took Lucy's gloved hand between her own rough ones. She told her visitor that she must surely be an angel, sent down by special arrangement.

Lucy belonged to one of the wealthiest families in the city. Yet it struck her, walking home that evening, that she had seldom felt so welcome anywhere as in that smoky little kitchen. She returned the following week, and the week after that, and before the winter was out the rich young lady and the poor widow had become the firmest of friends. It was during one of these visits that Lucy, staring into her teacup, confessed the reason for the sadness Mrs. Wiggs had already noticed in her face. She was in love with a young man who had loved her in return, but the two of them had quarreled over something trivial. Now each was waiting, with equal stubbornness, for the other to apologize. Mrs. Wiggs heard her out without a single interruption. "Pride makes a mighty poor blanket on a cold night, honey," she said at last. "But don't you go giving up. Things have a way of coming right, sooner or later, when folks give them half a chance."

Some weeks afterward, a fire swept through a stable on the far side of town, and by morning nothing was left of the building but blackened timbers. Passing the ruins on his way to work, Billy found an old horse lying in the street, thin and weak. Its owner had already written it off as not worth the cost of feeding. When Billy asked whether he might have it, the man merely shrugged and told him he was welcome to it. Coaxing the exhausted animal all the way back to the Cabbage Patch took most of the day and every scrap of patience Billy possessed. For weeks, he fed it, brushed its dull coat and made it a bed of clean straw in the shed. His sisters carried water and chattered to it as though it were a member of the family. Slowly, almost against the odds, the old horse grew strong again. By spring Billy had knocked together a small cart and was earning honest wages delivering goods for the shopkeepers who had once paid him in pennies.

One evening, with an air of tremendous mystery, Mrs. Wiggs announced that the family was going to the theater. None of the children had ever set foot inside one, and the walk into the city passed in a fever of questions. They gazed at the red velvet seats, the golden lamps and the great curtain. When it rose to reveal a painted world of castles and gardens, Europena, the youngest, gasped so loudly that the people in front turned around to stare. Throughout the funny scenes she laughed until she could hardly breathe. All the way home the children acted out the play on the frozen sidewalks, each of them loudly claiming the best part.

One mild spring day, the young man whom Lucy loved turned up in the Cabbage Patch. He had heard of the Wiggs family and wished to see whether he too might be of some use to them. As fate would have it, Lucy was sitting in the kitchen at that very moment. For one long, dreadful minute the two of them simply stared at each other across the table. Mrs. Wiggs, who understood the whole situation at a glance, wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation. "Now, you two sit right down," she said. "I'm going out to look after the horse, and I expect it'll take me a good long while." When at last she came back, she found them sitting side by side, both smiling, and neither of them able to explain quite what the quarrel had been about. Before the summer was over, Lucy and the young man were married.

By then the fortunes of the Wiggs family had turned as well. The horse and cart brought in steady money, and the girls went to school in warm coats and sound shoes. For the first time in years there was coal in the shed before the first frost. On a warm evening late that summer, Mrs. Wiggs settled on her doorstep with her children around her and watched the sun sink, red and enormous, behind the railroad tracks. "Just look at that," she said, with deep satisfaction. "Seems to me that everything in this world comes right in the end, as long as we have the patience to wait for it."
