# Hans in Luck

Summary: After seven years of work, a cheerful young man sets off for home with a lump of gold.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): gold, cow, pig, goose, grindstone

## Level A1

Hans worked for a man for seven years. Then he wanted to go home. The man gave Hans some gold. It was as big as his head! Hans put it on his back. Then he began to walk home.

The gold was very heavy. Soon he met a man on a horse. "I want a horse," said Hans. The man said, "Take my horse. Give me your gold." Hans liked this very much.

Hans got on the horse. It began to run. Soon Hans fell off. Then a man with a cow came along. Hans said, "Please take my horse. Give me your cow." The man said yes. "Now I have milk every day!" thought Hans.

Then Hans wanted some water. He tried to get milk from the cow. But nothing came. The cow moved, and Hans fell down. Then a man came by with a pig. He gave Hans some water. The man said, "Your cow is very old. Take my pig for her." Hans said yes again.

Next Hans met a young man. He had a goose. He looked at the pig. He said, "Someone took a pig near here. People are angry. Maybe it is this pig!" Hans was afraid. Hans said, "Please take my pig. Give me your goose."

Hans came to the next town. There a man worked at a grindstone. The man said, "This grindstone makes money. Give me your goose for it." Hans gave him the goose. The man also gave him a big stone.

Now Hans had two heavy stones. At last, he came to an old well. There was water deep down in it. He put the stones next to the well. Then he bent down to drink. His arm hit the stones. They fell into the well!

He said, "Now I have nothing to carry. I am the luckiest man in the world!" Hans felt light and free. He ran home to his mother.

## Level A2

Hans worked for a master for seven long years. Then he said, "My time is over. I want to go home to my mother." "You worked well," said the master. He gave Hans a piece of gold as big as his head. Hans put it on his back and started for home.

It was a hot day, and the gold was heavy. Soon a man on a fine horse came along. Hans said, "Riding is a fine thing! You just sit there, and the horse does the work." "Then why do you walk?" asked the man. "I must carry this gold," said Hans. The man said, "Let's trade. You can have my horse, and I can take your gold." Hans agreed at once. The man helped him up and walked away with the gold.

Hans sat on the horse and called out, "Go, go!" The horse began to run very fast. Soon Hans fell off at the side of the road. A man with a cow stopped the horse. Hans said to him, "Riding is not for me. Your cow is much better. She gives milk every day." "Then take her, and give me your horse," the man answered. "Now I have milk for life!" thought Hans.

Hans walked on with his cow, and the day got hotter. Soon he wanted a drink very much. He tried to get some milk from his cow. But no milk came, because the cow was old. The cow did not like this and pushed Hans over.

Just then, a man came along with a young pig. He gave Hans a drink of water. "Your cow is too old for milk," said the man. Hans said, "Then I want a young pig. Can I have your pig for my cow?" The man agreed, and Hans walked on with the pig.

Soon a young man with a white goose joined him. He looked at the pig for a long time. Then he said, "Someone took a pig from a man near here. People want it back. Maybe it is this pig!" Hans did not want any trouble. "Please take my pig and give me your goose," said Hans. The young man took the pig and quickly walked away.

In the next village, Hans saw a man with a grindstone. The man sang a happy song while he worked. "You look very happy," said Hans. The man answered, "My work brings me money every day. Give me your goose, and you can have this grindstone." Hans gave him the goose at once. The man also gave him a big stone from the road.

The two stones were heavy, and Hans walked more and more slowly. At last, he came to a well. He put the stones on the side of the well. When he looked down at the water, his arm touched them. The stones fell into the water. Hans stood up and called out, "Now I have nothing to carry! No man in the world is as lucky as me!" Then he ran home to his mother with a light heart.

## Level B1

Hans served his master well for seven years. Then he decided it was time to go home. He went to his master and said, "My time is over. Please give me my pay, and I will go back to my mother." The master was pleased with his work. He gave Hans a lump of gold as big as his head. Hans wrapped the gold in a cloth and put it on his shoulder. Then he set off along the road.

The sun was hot, and the heavy gold pressed on his shoulder with every step. Before long, a rider came towards him on a lively horse. "Riding is a fine thing!" Hans said to himself. "You sit there as comfortably as in a chair, and your shoes never wear out." The rider heard him and stopped. "Why are you walking, then?" he asked. "I have to carry this lump home," said Hans. "It is so heavy that my head hurts." The rider said, "Let's swap, then. I will give you my horse, and you can give me your gold." Hans agreed with delight, and the rider helped him into the saddle. "Just click your tongue and shout 'hop, hop' to go faster," the rider told him. Then he walked away with the gold.

For a while Hans rode along happily. Soon he wanted to go faster, so he clicked his tongue and shouted, "Hop, hop!" The horse began to gallop, and before Hans knew it, he was lying in a ditch. A farmer who was driving a cow along the road caught the horse. Hans climbed out and said, "Riding is no fun on a horse like that. I much prefer a cow like yours. You walk behind her in peace, and she gives you milk every day." "Then I will give you my cow for your horse," said the farmer. Hans was delighted, and the farmer rode off on the horse.

Hans walked on slowly, thinking about bread with butter and cheese. At an inn he ate everything he had with him and spent his last coins. Then he continued towards his mother's village. The afternoon grew hotter, and soon Hans was terribly thirsty. "I will milk my cow and have a good drink," he thought. He tied her to a tree and held his cap under her. But not a single drop came. He was so clumsy that the cow grew cross. She pushed him over with her back leg. Hans sat on the ground for a while, feeling dizzy.

Just then a butcher came along with a young pig in a wheelbarrow. He helped Hans up and gave him a drink of water from his bottle. "That cow will never give milk," said the butcher. "She is far too old for that." Hans scratched his head and said, "A young pig like yours is much better. Will you take my cow for it?" The butcher agreed, and Hans went on with the pig on a rope.

Soon he met a young man who was carrying a fine white goose under his arm. They walked together, and Hans talked about all his good trades. The young man looked at the pig with a worried face. "Someone took a pig from the mayor's farm in the village behind us," he said. "I am afraid it is this one. They are searching everywhere, and they will be very angry with the man who has it." Hans grew frightened and said, "Please help me. Take my pig and give me your goose." The young man agreed and hurried away down a side path with the pig.

In the last village before home, Hans saw a man at a grindstone. The man sang a cheerful song while he worked. "You seem very happy," said Hans. The man answered, "Yes, because my work always puts money in my pocket. Where did you get that fine goose?" Hans told him the whole story, from the gold to the goose. The man said, "You should become a grinder like me. Give me your goose, and I will give you this grindstone." He also picked up an ordinary heavy stone from the ground and handed it to Hans.

Hans walked on, but the two stones were very heavy, and he was tired and hungry. At last, he came to a well and wanted a drink of fresh water. He put the stones carefully on the edge of the well and bent down. By accident, he pushed them, and they fell into the deep water. Hans jumped up for joy and thanked heaven. "There is no man under the sun as lucky as I am!" he cried. With a light heart and empty hands, he ran all the way home to his mother.

## Level B2

For seven years Hans had served his master faithfully. When the time was up, he told him that he wished to go home to his mother. "You have served me well," said the master, "and you shall have an honest reward." With that he gave Hans a lump of gold as big as the young man's head. Hans tied it up in a cloth, lifted it onto his shoulder and set off for home.

The road was long and dusty, and with every step the gold seemed to grow heavier. Before long a rider on a lively horse came trotting towards him. "What a fine thing riding is!" Hans said aloud. "You sit as comfortably as on a chair, you never trip over stones, and you save your shoes." The rider, who had heard every word, pulled up his horse. "Then why are you going on foot?" he asked. "I have to drag this lump home," Hans explained, "and it is so heavy that I can hardly hold my head straight." "Let's exchange," said the rider at once. "I will give you my horse, and you can give me your lump." Hans gladly agreed, and the rider helped him into the saddle. He advised Hans to click his tongue and call out "hop, hop" when he wanted to go fast. Then he went off with the gold.

Hans was delighted as he sat high above the road. After a while, however, he wanted to go faster, so he clicked his tongue and shouted, "Hop, hop!" The horse broke into a wild gallop, and a moment later Hans found himself lying in a ditch beside the road. The horse would have run off if a farmer, who was driving a cow along the same road, had not caught it. Hans picked himself up, rather shaken, and declared that riding was no pleasure at all. "A cow like yours is far better," he told the farmer. "You can walk behind her at your ease, and every day you get milk, butter and cheese." "If you like her so much," said the farmer, "I will gladly take your horse for her." Hans accepted with joy, and the farmer swung himself onto the horse and rode quickly away.

Driving his cow ahead of him, Hans went on and thought about his excellent bargain. At an inn along the way he ate up all the food he had brought and spent his last few coins. Then he set off again towards his mother's village. Around midday the heat became terrible, and Hans found himself on a wide, open heath where there was no water at all. "I will milk my cow," he thought, "and that will cure my thirst." He tied her to a dry tree and held his leather cap underneath. But no matter how he tried, not a single drop appeared. Because he was so clumsy, the cow lost her patience and pushed him over with her back leg. For a while Hans sat on the ground, too dizzy to stand up.

Luckily, a butcher came along just then, pushing a young pig in a wheelbarrow. He helped Hans to his feet and offered him a drink of water from his bottle. When Hans told him about the cow, the butcher shook his head. "This animal will never give you milk," he said. "She is old, and she is only good for pulling a cart now." "Well, well," said Hans, smoothing his hair. "A young pig like yours would suit me much better." The kind butcher agreed to the exchange. Hans walked on with the pig on a rope, feeling that everything was going his way.

Soon a young fellow with a beautiful white goose under his arm joined him on the road. Hans happily described all the good trades he had made that day. The young fellow listened, then glanced nervously at the pig. "In the village I have just come from, someone stole a pig from the mayor's yard," he said in a low voice. "I am afraid that this is the very pig, and they have sent people out to look for it." Hans turned pale and begged him for help. "You know this area better than I do," he said, "so take my pig and give me your goose." The young fellow agreed, though he pretended to be taking a great risk. Then he quickly disappeared down a side path with the pig.

In the last village before home, Hans came upon a man who was working at his grindstone. He was singing merrily as the wheel turned. "You must be doing well, since you are so cheerful," said Hans. "Indeed," replied the grinder, "a good grinder finds money in his pocket every time he puts his hand in it. But where did you buy that fine goose?" Hans explained how he had traded his way from the gold to the goose. "You have always looked after yourself well," said the grinder. "Now you only need to hear money in your pocket every day. That is easy if you become a grinder like me. Give me your goose, and this grindstone is yours." He also picked up an ordinary heavy stone that was lying nearby. He said Hans could use it to hammer old nails straight.

Hans went on, his eyes shining, but before long the two stones began to feel extremely heavy. He was tired and hungry, and he could only walk very slowly. At last, he reached a well and decided to rest and refresh himself with cool water. So that he would not damage the stones, he laid them carefully on the edge of the well. As he bent down to drink, however, he pushed them by accident, and both fell into the deep water. When Hans saw them sink, he jumped for joy. He thanked heaven for freeing him from his heavy load without any fault of his own. "There is no man under the sun as lucky as I am!" he cried. Then, with a light heart and nothing to carry, he ran on until he was home with his mother.

## Level C1

Hans had served his master for seven long years. When the last of them came to an end, he announced, with the simple directness that was his nature, that he wished to go home to his mother. "You have served me faithfully and honestly," said the master, "and the reward shall match the service." So saying, he handed the young man a lump of gold as large as his head. Hans accepted this fortune with a cheerful nod, as though he had been given a loaf of bread. He knotted it into a handkerchief, hoisted it onto his shoulder and set out along the road that would lead him, sooner or later, to his mother's door.

It was a long road, however, and a hot one, and with every mile the gold seemed to double in weight. Plodding along with his head bent sideways under the load, Hans caught sight of a rider trotting briskly towards him on a fine, high-spirited horse. "Ah, what a splendid thing riding is!" he exclaimed to nobody in particular. "A man sits there as if he were in an armchair, never stubs his toe on a stone, saves his shoes and still gets where he wants to go." The rider, who had heard every word, reined in beside him and asked, with an interest that Hans took for kindness, why he was trudging on foot. "Because I have to carry this lump home," sighed Hans, "and although it is gold, it weighs so much that I can scarcely keep my head straight." "In that case we should swap," the rider suggested with remarkable speed, "my horse for your lump." Hans, overjoyed, accepted at once. The stranger helped him into the saddle and explained that a click of the tongue and a cry of "hop, hop" would make the animal go faster. Then, with the gold on his own shoulder, the rider strolled off and was soon out of sight.

For a time Hans sat proudly in the saddle, delighted with the world and with himself. It occurred to him, though, that a man on a horse ought to travel faster than this, so he clicked his tongue and called out "hop, hop" as he had been taught. The horse burst into a furious gallop, and before Hans had time to wonder what was happening, he found himself sitting in a ditch that separated the field from the road. Had a farmer not been coming the other way with a cow, the horse would have vanished altogether, but the farmer caught it by the bridle and held it fast. Climbing out of the ditch and brushing himself down, Hans announced that riding was a poor joke, especially on a beast that threw its master off. "Give me a cow like yours any day," he said, eyeing the farmer's animal with admiration. "A man can stroll along behind her in perfect peace, and every morning he is sure of his milk, his butter and his cheese." The farmer, seeing an opportunity that he had no intention of letting slip, declared himself willing to part with his cow as a special kindness. Before Hans could thank him twice, he was in the saddle and riding away.

Hans drove his cow on in the highest of spirits, already tasting bread spread thickly with butter and cheese. At an inn by the roadside he ate up every crumb of food he had carried with him. Generous to the last, he spent his remaining few coins as well, reasoning that a man with a cow would never again be short of a meal. The road then took him across a wide, bare heath. As the midday sun climbed higher, the heat grew so fierce that his tongue seemed to stick to the roof of his mouth. "There is a simple remedy for this," he thought. "I shall milk my cow and refresh myself." Tying her to a withered tree and holding his leather cap beneath her, he set to work, yet however hard he tried, not a single drop rewarded his efforts. His hands were so clumsy that the cow at last lost all patience and pushed him over with her back leg. For some time Hans sat on the ground, too dazed to remember where he was.

As luck would have it, a butcher came along at that very moment, pushing a young pig in a wheelbarrow. "What has happened to you?" he asked, helping Hans to his feet and offering him a drink of water from his bottle. When Hans had told him everything, the butcher shook his head with an air of great wisdom. "That cow of yours will never give milk again," he said; "she is far too old, and she is good for nothing now except pulling a cart." "Well, well," said Hans, running his fingers through his hair, "who would have thought it? A young pig like yours, now, would suit me far better." The butcher, declaring that he was making a great sacrifice purely out of friendship, handed over the pig and took the cow. Hans walked on with the pig on a rope, marvelling at how smoothly everything in his life seemed to go.

Before long he was joined by a lad who carried a handsome white goose under his arm. As they walked side by side, Hans told him at length about his remarkable run of good fortune. The lad listened politely, but his eyes kept straying towards the pig, and at last, lowering his voice, he confided a troubling piece of news. "In the village I have just left," he said, "a pig was stolen from the mayor's own yard, and I very much fear that it is the one you are leading. They have sent men out to search the roads, and it would go badly for anyone found with the animal." Poor Hans turned quite pale. "You know this country far better than I do," he pleaded, "so for pity's sake take the pig and let me have your goose instead." With a show of great reluctance, as if he were risking his own neck for a stranger, the lad agreed. He hurried off down a side path with the pig, leaving Hans to walk on, much relieved, with the goose under his arm.

In the last village before home Hans came upon a scissors-grinder who was turning his wheel and singing a merry tune to its rhythm. "Things must be going well for you, since you sing so cheerfully at your work," said Hans. "Indeed they are," replied the grinder; "a grinder worth his trade finds money in his pocket every time he puts his hand into it. But tell me, where did you buy that splendid goose?" Hans, nothing loath, recounted his whole journey: gold for horse, horse for cow, cow for pig and pig for goose. "You have known how to look after yourself at every turn," observed the grinder, nodding gravely. "All you need now is to hear coins in your pocket whenever you stand up, and that you can easily have, provided that you take up my trade. I will let you have this grindstone for your goose, and you shall be set up for life." To seal the bargain, he also picked up an ordinary heavy stone from the ground beside him, explaining that it was just the thing for hammering crooked nails straight.

Hans set off once more, his eyes shining with happiness, but before long the two stones began to drag at him with a weight that grew heavier at every step. He had eaten nothing since the inn, and he was tired out. By the time he reached a well at the side of the field, he could think of nothing but a long, cool drink. Anxious not to damage his precious stones, he laid them with great care on the very edge of the well, and then he bent over to drink. In doing so, he gave them the slightest push, and with two heavy splashes both stones plunged into the water and sank out of sight. Far from despairing, Hans leapt up with joy, his eyes filling with tears of gratitude. He thanked heaven for having freed him from the last of his burdens without his having to reproach himself in any way. "There is not a man under the sun as lucky as I am," he cried. And so, light of heart and free of every load, he ran on until he stood at last in his mother's house.
