# The Queer Feet

Summary: A quiet priest in an expensive hotel hears strange footsteps and slowly understands a clever plan.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): footsteps, silver, waiter

## Level A1

The Twelve True Fishermen were twelve important men. Every year, they met at the Vernon Hotel. Their silver was very old and very good. They ate fish with it.

One night, a hotel waiter was not well. He asked for Father Brown, a priest. Father Brown came and talked with him. Then he sat down in a small room. He began to write for the waiter. The room next to it was for coats.

Father Brown heard footsteps outside his door. They went up and down, again and again. First, the footsteps were short and fast. Then the same footsteps were long and slow. Then they were fast again. "This is one man," thought Father Brown. "But he walks in two ways!"

A waiter works hard, so he walks fast. An important man walks slowly. Here, waiters and important men all wear black. So they all look the same!

Now Father Brown understood the plan. Near the twelve men, the man walked fast. They saw a waiter. Near the waiters, he walked slowly. They saw an important man. So no one stopped him. He took the silver from the table. Now he wanted his coat.

No one was in the room next door. So Father Brown went in and waited. The footsteps came near. A man in black came in. "Give me my coat," said the man. "Give me the silver first," said Father Brown.

The man was Flambeau, a famous thief. He looked at Father Brown for a minute. Then he gave the silver back. He left the hotel.

Soon the twelve men came running. Their silver was not on the table! They found Father Brown with the silver. "Where is the man?" asked one of them. "He is not here," said Father Brown.

Father Brown told them about the footsteps. "You and your waiters all wear black. Only the walk is different," he said. Then he went back to his work.

## Level A2

The Twelve True Fishermen were a club of twelve important men. Once a year, they ate together at the Vernon Hotel. It was a small but very good hotel. On this evening, they always ate fish. They ate it with special silver knives and forks. The silver was very old, and the club loved it.

That year, one of the waiters was not well. He was in bed, and he asked for a priest. So the hotel sent for Father Brown. Father Brown talked with the waiter for a long time. Then the waiter asked him to write some important things on paper. The hotel gave Father Brown a small room for this work. It was next to the room for coats, near the front door.

Father Brown began to write. Soon he heard footsteps outside his door. After a while, Father Brown stopped writing and listened. Something about the footsteps was not right. First, the steps were fast and light. Then the same feet walked slowly. Then they were fast again, and then slow again.

Father Brown thought hard about this. "A waiter always has a lot of work. So he walks fast. An important man has a lot of time. So he walks slowly. But this is only one man," thought Father Brown. Then he remembered something. That evening, the waiters wore black clothes. The twelve men wore black clothes too. So a waiter and an important man looked almost the same.

Now Father Brown understood the plan. Near the twelve men, the man walked fast, like a waiter. Near the waiters, he walked slowly, like an important man. No one stopped him, and no one asked him a question. After the fish, he took the silver from the table. Now he wanted to leave with it, so he needed his coat.

There was no one in the room for coats that night. So Father Brown went in and stood at its little window. Soon the footsteps came down to the front door. A man in black clothes came up to the window. "My coat, please," he said. "Give me the silver first," said Father Brown.

The man was Flambeau, the most famous thief of his time. He looked at Father Brown for a long moment. Then, slowly, he gave all the silver back to Father Brown. Father Brown said a few kind words to him. Then Flambeau went out into the night.

Back at the table, the twelve men saw a big problem. Their silver was not on the table! They ran out of the room to look for it. They found Father Brown near the front door with the silver.

"Where is the thief?" asked one of the men. "He is not here now," said Father Brown. He put the silver on the table in front of them. Then Father Brown told them about the footsteps. "You and your waiters all wear black clothes. So you look the same. Only your walk is different," he said. Father Brown went back to his little room and his work.

## Level B1

The Twelve True Fishermen were a very old and very proud club. Its twelve members were rich gentlemen, and they met once a year for dinner. They always held this dinner at the Vernon Hotel, a quiet and expensive place. The most important part of the evening was the fish course. The club ate it with special silver knives and forks, which had pearl handles. Nobody outside the club ever used this silver.

On the day of the dinner, one of the hotel waiters suddenly became seriously ill. He asked for a priest, so the hotel sent for Father Brown. Father Brown was a small, quiet man with a round face. He sat with the sick waiter for some time. Afterwards, the waiter asked him to write down a few important things. The owner of the hotel did not want a stranger walking about on such a night. So he gave the priest a small office near the front door. Next to the office was the cloakroom, where the gentlemen left their coats.

Father Brown sat down and began to write. Outside, a long passage led from the dining room to the front of the hotel. For a while he heard nothing except the sound of his own pen. Then he noticed the footsteps. Someone was walking up and down the passage, again and again. At first Father Brown paid no attention, but slowly the sound began to trouble him.

The footsteps were strange because they were not always the same. For a few seconds they were quick and light, like the steps of a busy person. Then they became slow and heavy, like the steps of a man with nothing to do. After that they were quick again, and then slow again. "Why does the same man walk in two different ways?" Father Brown asked himself.

He put down his pen and thought about it carefully. Who walks quickly in a hotel? A waiter, of course, because there is always more work for him. Who walks slowly? A gentleman who is waiting for his dinner and has plenty of time. Then Father Brown remembered something else. At an evening dinner, a gentleman and a waiter wear almost the same black clothes. "A gentleman can easily look like a waiter," he thought.

Now he understood the whole trick. The man in the passage was playing two parts. Near the gentlemen, he walked fast and looked busy, so they took him for a waiter. Near the real waiters, he walked slowly, so they took him for a guest. Nobody stopped him, and nobody asked him a single question. After the fish course, he cleared away the plates and the silver, just like a waiter. Now he wanted to leave the hotel with it, and for that he needed his coat.

Father Brown stood up and went into the cloakroom next door. The man who worked there was away for a moment, so the little room was empty. The priest stood behind the counter and waited. Soon slow, heavy footsteps came down the passage, and a tall man in black appeared. "Get me my coat, please," he said shortly. "First, please give me the silver in your pockets," said Father Brown quietly.

The tall man looked down at the little priest in great surprise. He was Flambeau, the most famous thief of his time, and few things surprised him. For a long moment he did not move. Then, with a strange smile, he took the silver knives and forks out of his pockets. He put them on the counter, one by one. Father Brown spoke to him quietly for a minute, and nobody knows what he said. Then Flambeau took his coat and walked out into the night.

Meanwhile, at the big table, the Twelve True Fishermen saw that their silver was missing. There was a great noise of chairs and voices. The head waiter ran to the front of the hotel, and the gentlemen followed him. In the cloakroom they found a small priest with their silver in front of him.

"Did you catch the thief?" cried one of the gentlemen, an old colonel. "Yes, I caught him, and then I let him go," said Father Brown. The colonel stared at him, and the priest explained about the footsteps. "You gentlemen and your waiters wear the same black clothes. The only difference is the way you walk. A waiter always hurries, but a gentleman takes his time," said Father Brown. Then the little priest went back to finish his work for the sick waiter.

## Level B2

From the street, the Vernon Hotel looked almost like a private house, and most people walked past it without a second look. Inside, however, it was one of the most expensive hotels in the city. Its owner was proud that only the right sort of people ever dined there. Once a year he closed his doors to everyone except the Twelve True Fishermen, a club of twelve wealthy gentlemen.

The high point of their annual dinner was always the fish course. The members ate it with silver knives and forks that belonged to the club alone. Each piece had a handle made of pearl. The silver was never used on any other night, and the owner guarded it more carefully than anything else in the building.

On the afternoon of the dinner, one of the waiters suddenly fell seriously ill. He asked for a priest, and since the hotel could hardly refuse such a request, someone was sent to fetch Father Brown. The little priest, with his round face and his large umbrella, sat with the sick man for some time. Afterwards the waiter asked him to write down certain things on paper, because they mattered a great deal to him. The owner was not happy to have a stranger in the building on such an important night. He gave Father Brown a small office near the front entrance and asked him to stay there until he had finished. Next to the office was the cloakroom, where the gentlemen had left their coats and hats.

Father Brown sat down at the desk and began to write. Beyond his door ran a long passage that connected the dining room with the front of the building. From time to time, people passed along it. At first, he paid no attention to them, for he was writing slowly and carefully. Gradually, however, one particular set of footsteps caught his ear. Someone was walking up and down the passage, and the walk kept changing.

For a while the steps were quick and light, like those of a person with a hundred small jobs to do. Then, quite suddenly, they became slow and heavy, like the steps of a man with the whole evening free. A few moments later they were quick again. Father Brown laid down his pen and listened. "Those are the feet of one man," he murmured, "but he walks like two different people."

He began to think the problem through, as calmly as if he were adding up a column of numbers. In a hotel, who walks quickly? A waiter, because he is always wanted somewhere else. Who walks slowly? A gentleman, because he can afford to wait while others hurry for him. Then he remembered something about this particular evening that made everything clear. At a formal dinner, the gentlemen wear black evening clothes, and the waiters wear black evening clothes as well. A stranger dressed in that way could easily be taken for either one.

Now the whole trick lay open before him. The man in the passage was playing two parts at once. Whenever he passed one of the gentlemen, he hurried along like a waiter, so the gentleman never looked at him twice. Whenever he passed a real waiter, he strolled like a guest, and no waiter would dare to question a guest. In this way he had walked right up to the club's table without anyone stopping him. When the fish course was over, he had cleared away the plates himself, quickly and neatly. The precious silver had gone with them straight into his pockets. Now he only needed his coat, and then he could disappear into the night.

Father Brown stood up and went next door. The cloakroom attendant had stepped away for a moment, so the priest took his place behind the counter. Before long, the slow, heavy footsteps came down the passage, and a tall man in perfect evening dress appeared. "My coat, please," the man said impatiently, "and be quick about it." "Please give me back the silver knives and forks in your pockets first," said Father Brown gently.

The tall man stared down at the little priest. He was Flambeau, the most famous thief of his day, and very few things in the world could surprise him. This did. For a long moment he did not move at all. Then something in the priest's quiet, steady face seemed to change his mind. Without a word, he took the knives and forks out of his pockets and laid them on the counter. Father Brown spoke to him for a minute in a low voice, and nobody ever learned what was said between them. Then Flambeau took his coat and walked out into the dark street.

Meanwhile, in the dining room, the club had discovered its loss. A waiter came to clear the fish plates and found that there was nothing left to clear. The gentlemen rose to their feet, and the head waiter turned pale. The owner rushed out into the passage with the whole club behind him. At the front of the hotel, they found a small priest sitting quietly behind the cloakroom counter. The silver lay neatly in front of him.

"Where is the thief, and did you catch him?" demanded one of the gentlemen, an old colonel with a sharp face. "Yes, I caught him," replied Father Brown, "and then I let him go." The colonel stared at him in astonishment, and the priest explained the mystery of the footsteps. "You see, you gentlemen and your waiters dress exactly alike. The only real difference between you is the way you walk," said Father Brown. The colonel was silent for a long time, thinking about how often he had looked at a waiter without really seeing him. Then Father Brown picked up his pen and went back to finish his work for the sick waiter.

## Level C1

There are hotels that advertise themselves with bright lights and grand staircases, and there are hotels that prefer to be overlooked, and the Vernon Hotel belonged firmly to the second kind. From the street it might have passed for a private house, yet inside it was so expensive that the very difficulty of getting in became its chief attraction. Once a year its owner closed the doors to the rest of the world and gave the whole place to the Twelve True Fishermen. They were a club of twelve wealthy gentlemen with a long history and a great deal of pride.

The ceremony at the heart of their annual dinner was the fish course. It was eaten with a set of silver knives and forks that belonged to the club alone, each handle crowned with a pearl. The silver never appeared on any other night of the year. The owner, a nervous man who lived in constant fear of disappointing his guests, watched over it as anxiously as a bank manager watches over a vault.

On the afternoon of the dinner, however, something happened that no one had planned for: one of the waiters was taken seriously ill. He asked for a priest, and since a hotel of such respectability could hardly refuse, a message was sent to Father Brown. The little priest arrived with his round face and his shapeless umbrella, sat with the sick man for some time, and listened with complete attention to what he had to say. Afterwards the waiter begged him to put certain matters down in writing, and Father Brown agreed without hesitation.

The owner was far from delighted to have a shabby clergyman wandering about on the most important evening of his year. Being polite to the point of pain, he showed Father Brown into a small office near the front entrance and suggested that he remain there until his work was done. On one side of the office lay the cloakroom, where the gentlemen's coats and hats now hung in a silent row. On the other side ran a long passage that connected the dining room with the front of the building.

Father Brown sat down, spread out his paper, and began to write in his slow, careful hand. For some time the only sounds were the scratch of his pen and the distant murmur of the dinner, rising and falling like the sea. Then, almost without meaning to, he began to listen to the footsteps in the passage outside. They belonged to one person, and that person was walking up and down, up and down, as if waiting for something. What troubled the priest was not that the steps came and went, but that they never seemed able to decide what kind of steps they were.

For a while they were quick, light and tapping, the steps of someone with a hundred small duties and no time for any of them. Then, at almost the same point in the passage each time, they turned slow and heavy, the unhurried tread of a man who owned the evening and intended to enjoy it. A little later the quick steps returned, and after them the slow ones again, with a regularity that was almost comic. Father Brown put down his pen. "Those are the feet of one man," he said softly to the empty room, "but they belong to two different characters."

He began to work the problem out as calmly as he might have worked out a sum on a blackboard. Who in a hotel walks quickly? A waiter, obviously, because a waiter is always wanted somewhere he is not. Who walks slowly? A gentleman, because a gentleman is paying for the privilege of letting other people hurry on his behalf. Then he remembered the one fact about this particular evening that turned the puzzle into an answer. At a formal dinner the guests wear black evening clothes, and so do the waiters, and the difference between the two is far smaller than either group likes to believe.

At that moment the whole scheme lay open before him, as plain as a map. The man in the passage was playing two parts at once, and playing each to the audience that mattered. Whenever one of the gentlemen came by, he hurried past with the bent head of a busy waiter. The gentleman, who had never looked closely at a waiter in his life, did not begin now. Whenever a real waiter appeared, he strolled along with the easy air of an important guest, and no waiter would have dreamed of asking a guest his business. Seen as a guest by the servants and as a servant by the guests, he had walked right up to the club's table without a single question. When the fish course ended, he had cleared away the plates himself with the speed of a professional, and the precious silver had travelled with them into his pockets. All he needed now was his coat, and after that the night would swallow him.

Father Brown rose and went quietly next door. By good fortune the cloakroom attendant had stepped away, so the priest slipped behind the counter and stood there among the hanging coats, like a man waiting for a train. Presently the slow, heavy footsteps came down the passage, and a tall figure in faultless evening dress appeared at the counter. "My coat," said the stranger, with the impatience of someone who has never been kept waiting, "and be quick about it." "Before I find your coat," said Father Brown mildly, "perhaps you would be kind enough to return the silver knives and forks you are carrying in your pockets."

The tall man stood perfectly still and looked down at him. This was Flambeau, the most celebrated thief of his generation, a man who had escaped from more traps than most people have ever heard of, and he was not easily astonished. For a long moment the two of them faced each other across the counter without a word. Then something in the priest's plain, steady face appeared to settle the matter. With a strange half-smile, Flambeau drew the silver from his pockets and laid it piece by piece on the counter. Father Brown spoke to him for a minute in a low voice, and what passed between them neither of them ever repeated. Then Flambeau took his coat, bowed slightly, and stepped out into the darkness of the street.

In the dining room, meanwhile, the club had discovered its loss in the most humiliating way possible. A waiter came to collect the fish plates and found that there was nothing left to collect. The gentlemen sprang to their feet, the head waiter went as white as the tablecloth, and the owner ran into the passage with all twelve Fishermen crowding behind him. At the front of the hotel they found, of all people, the shabby little priest, sitting behind the cloakroom counter. The silver was arranged before him as neatly as if he had been asked to polish it.

"Where is the thief, and did you catch him?" demanded one of the gentlemen, an old colonel with a sharp face and a sharper voice. "I did catch him," said Father Brown, "and then I let him go, which is sometimes the more difficult part." While the colonel was still recovering from this, the priest explained, quite simply, the riddle of the footsteps and the two ways of walking. "You gentlemen and your waiters dress exactly alike, and so, in the evening, the only real difference between you is the way you move. A waiter walks as though he has too much to do, and a gentleman walks as though he has nothing to do at all," Father Brown said. The colonel looked for a long time at the waiters standing along the wall, as if he were seeing their faces for the first time. Father Brown, meanwhile, picked up his umbrella, went back to his little office, and quietly finished the work he had come to do for the sick waiter.
