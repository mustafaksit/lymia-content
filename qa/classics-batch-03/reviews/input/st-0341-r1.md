# Northanger Abbey

Summary: A young girl who loves dark old novels visits a real abbey and looks for its secrets.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): abbey, novels, chest

## Level A1

Catherine Morland was seventeen years old. She loved books, and most of all novels. In her novels, old houses had dark secrets.

Some old family friends took Catherine to Bath. At a dance there, she met Henry Tilney. Henry was kind and funny. Catherine liked him.

She also found a friend, Isabella Thorpe. Isabella's brother, John Thorpe, talked a lot. Catherine did not like John. Henry's sister, Eleanor Tilney, became her friend.

Henry's father was General Tilney. He was very kind to Catherine. He asked her to visit his home. Its name was Northanger Abbey. "A real abbey!" said Catherine. "It is just like in my novels!"

Catherine's room had a big old chest. "Maybe it has a secret!" she thought. She opened it. In it was only a white bed cover.

Henry's mother died nine years before. The general never talked about her. "Maybe this house has a secret," Catherine thought. "Maybe the general knows something about her."

Catherine went to the mother's room alone. She found nothing there. Then Henry came in. He saw her face and knew her thoughts. "My mother was ill," said Henry. "My brother and I were with her. Life is not like your novels, Catherine." But Henry was still kind to her.

Then Henry went to his own house. The general went away for some days. He came back late one night. Then Eleanor came to Catherine's room. "My father says you must go," said Eleanor. "You must leave early in the morning." So Catherine went home by herself.

Some days later, Henry came to see her. "John Thorpe talked to my father," said Henry. "In his story, you had lots of money. Later, he told a new story. Now you had no money at all. So my father sent you away. But I love you, Catherine. I want to marry you." Catherine said yes. In the end, the general said yes too. So Henry and Catherine married and were happy.

## Level A2

Catherine Morland was seventeen years old. She was one of ten children in a quiet country family. Catherine loved to read, and she loved novels most of all. Her favorite novels were full of old buildings and dark secrets.

Two old friends of the family planned a stay in Bath. They asked Catherine to come with them. At a dance in Bath, Catherine met a young man, Henry Tilney. Henry was clever and funny, and he liked books too. Catherine liked him from the first evening.

Catherine also found a friend in Bath, Isabella Thorpe. Isabella's brother, John Thorpe, talked only about himself and his horses. John wanted Catherine's attention, but she did not like him. Henry had a sister, Eleanor Tilney. Eleanor was kind, and she became Catherine's good friend.

Then Henry's father, General Tilney, noticed Catherine. He was very polite and asked about her family. Soon he asked her to visit their home, Northanger Abbey. "A real abbey!" Catherine said to herself. "There must be secret rooms!"

The general liked new things, so the rooms were modern and bright. In her bedroom, Catherine saw a big old chest. Her heart beat fast, and she opened it. Inside, there was only a white cover for a bed. That night, she found some old papers in a black cupboard. In the morning, she read the papers. They were only lists of clothes and some old bills. Catherine felt very silly.

Henry's mother died nine years before. The general never talked about her. "Perhaps there is a sad secret about Henry's mother," Catherine thought.

One afternoon, Catherine went to the mother's room alone. There was nothing strange in it. Then Henry came in. He was home early that day. He saw her face and understood her thoughts. "My mother was ill for a few days," said Henry. "My brother and I were with her at the end. My father loved her in his own way. Life is not like a novel, Catherine." Catherine went to her room and cried for a long time. But Henry was still kind to her.

Some days later, Henry went to his own house in another village. The general also went away for a few days. He came back very late one night. Then Eleanor came to Catherine's room. "My father says you must leave," said Eleanor. "You must go early tomorrow morning, and alone. I do not know the reason." Catherine did not understand. The next morning, she traveled home alone.

Some days later, Henry came to see her. "John Thorpe told my father a lie about you," said Henry. "In his story, your family had lots of money. Later, John told a new story. Now you had no money at all. So my father sent you away. But I love you, Catherine, and I want to marry you." Catherine said yes with all her heart. At first, the general said no. But then Eleanor married an important man with lots of money. The general was pleased, so he finally said yes. So Henry and Catherine married and were very happy.

## Level B1

Catherine Morland was seventeen years old. She lived in a quiet country village with her parents and nine brothers and sisters. Now, however, she had a new love: novels. She especially enjoyed stories about lonely old buildings, dark storms and terrible secrets.

Two old family friends, a kind husband and wife, were going to Bath for some weeks. They had no children of their own, so they invited Catherine to come with them. Bath was a busy, fashionable town, and every evening there were parties and dances. At one dance, Catherine met a young man who soon became very important to her. His name was Henry Tilney. He was clever and funny, and he could talk about novels as happily as she could.

In Bath, Catherine also made a close friend, Isabella Thorpe. Isabella loved novels too. Isabella's brother, John Thorpe, was a loud young man who talked only about himself. He wanted Catherine's attention, but she found him boring and a little rude. She much preferred the company of Henry and his gentle sister, Eleanor Tilney.

Soon Henry's father, General Tilney, began to show a great interest in Catherine. To Catherine's surprise, he was extremely polite to her and asked many questions about her family. Before long, he invited her to stay with them at their home, Northanger Abbey. "A real abbey!" she thought. "There will be long dark passages, old towers and maybe even a secret room!"

On the journey, Henry drove Catherine in his own small carriage. Henry decided to tease her a little. "You will have a room far from everyone else," he said seriously. "At midnight, a storm will start, and you will find a hidden door." Catherine laughed, but her heart was beating fast.

Northanger Abbey was a surprise. The general loved comfort and new things, so most of the rooms were modern and bright. Still, Catherine's bedroom held a large, old chest. With shaking hands, she lifted its heavy top. Inside, there was nothing except a folded white cover for a bed. That night, a strong wind was blowing around the old walls. Catherine noticed a tall black cupboard in the corner of her room. In one of its drawers, she found a roll of old papers. At that moment, her candle went out, and she lay awake in the dark for hours. In the morning, she read them. They were only lists of clothes for washing and an old bill. Catherine felt extremely foolish.

Henry's mother died nine years before Catherine's visit. The general rarely spoke about her. He never walked along her favorite path in the woods. "Perhaps this house is hiding a sad old secret," she thought. "Perhaps the general knows more about his wife's last days than he says."

One afternoon, while the others were busy, Catherine went to the mother's room alone. It was a pleasant, bright room, and there was nothing strange in it at all. As she came out, she met Henry on the stairs. He was home earlier than everyone expected. Henry saw which room she was leaving, and slowly he understood her thoughts. "My mother became ill very suddenly," Henry said gently. "My brother and I were with her at the end. The doctors did everything they could. My father loved her in his own way. This is England, and these are modern times. Real life is not like a novel." Catherine ran to her room and cried with shame. But Henry never spoke about it again, and he was as kind to her as before.

Some days later, Henry went to his own house in another village. The general also went away for a few days. He came back unexpectedly late one night. Soon afterwards, Eleanor came to Catherine's room, and her face was pale. "My father says you must leave," Eleanor said unhappily. "You will go early tomorrow morning, and you will travel alone. He did not give me any reason." Catherine felt confused and deeply hurt. The next morning, she began the long journey home by herself.

At home, her family welcomed her warmly, but Catherine was quiet and sad. A few days later, Henry arrived at her parents' house. "John Thorpe wanted to marry you himself," said Henry. "So in Bath, he told my father that your family was very rich. Later, he understood that you did not love him, and he became angry. Then he told my father a new story, and now your family was poor. That is why my father sent you away so suddenly. But I am not my father, and I love you. Will you marry me?" Catherine happily said yes. At first, the general refused to agree. Then Eleanor married a rich and important man. This put the general in a very good mood, so at last he agreed. Soon after, Henry and Catherine married, and they were very happy together.

## Level B2

Catherine Morland was seventeen, the eldest daughter in a large, cheerful family from a quiet country village. As a little girl, she had preferred running about outside to music or drawing lessons. By seventeen, however, she had discovered novels, especially the frightening kind full of ruined buildings, stormy nights and terrible family secrets. Her own life was peaceful and ordinary, and she secretly hoped that something more exciting might happen to her.

Her chance came when two old friends of the family, a kind but rather dull couple, planned some weeks in Bath. They had no children of their own, and they invited Catherine to come with them. Bath was a busy, fashionable town, where people went to parties and dances every evening. At one of these dances, Catherine was introduced to a young clergyman called Henry Tilney. He was clever, amusing and full of gentle jokes, and he could talk about novels as happily as she could. Catherine went home that night thinking of nothing else.

In Bath, Catherine also became close friends with Isabella Thorpe, a pretty girl a few years older than herself. Isabella loved novels too, and the two girls spent whole mornings reading together and discussing their favorite stories. Isabella's brother, John Thorpe, was a loud, boastful young man who talked endlessly about his horses and his carriage. He was determined to win Catherine's attention, but she found him tiresome and rude. Once he even tricked her into a carriage ride, so she missed a promised walk with Henry and his sister. That sister, Eleanor Tilney, was quiet and gentle, and Catherine soon grew very fond of her. Meanwhile, Isabella became engaged to Catherine's brother, but she soon began to give her attention to Henry's older brother instead. Little by little, Catherine realized that Isabella was not a true friend at all.

Then something wonderful happened. Henry's father, General Tilney, a tall and proud man, began to treat Catherine with surprising warmth. He was extremely polite to her and asked many questions about her family. Before long, he invited her to stay with them at their home, Northanger Abbey. Catherine could hardly believe her luck. A real abbey, she thought, would surely have long dark passages, old towers and perhaps a secret room.

On the journey, Henry drove Catherine in his own small carriage. Seeing how excited she was, he decided to tease her. "They will give you a room far from everyone else," he said with a perfectly serious face. "At midnight a storm will break, and behind a curtain you will find a hidden door." Catherine laughed, but her heart was beating faster than usual.

Northanger Abbey was not at all what she had imagined. The general loved comfort and modern things, so most of the rooms were bright, warm and newly furnished. Still, Catherine's bedroom contained a large, old-fashioned chest. With trembling hands she lifted its heavy lid, only for Eleanor to come in at that very moment. Inside, there was nothing but a neatly folded white cover for a bed. That night, a strong wind howled around the old walls. Catherine noticed a tall black cabinet in a corner, and in one of its drawers she found some old papers. Just then her candle went out, and she lay awake in the dark for hours. In the morning, she examined the papers in the daylight. They were nothing more than lists of clothes for washing and an old bill. Catherine felt extremely foolish.

Henry's mother had died nine years earlier. The general rarely mentioned her, and he never walked along the path through the woods that she had loved. Slowly, Catherine's imagination began to work like one of her novels. Perhaps, she thought, the house was hiding a sad old secret. Perhaps the general knew more about his wife's last days than he was willing to say.

One afternoon, while the others were busy, Catherine slipped into the mother's room alone. It was a pleasant, sunny room, and there was nothing strange in it at all. As she came out, she met Henry on the stairs, home earlier than anyone had expected. He saw where she had been, and slowly he understood what she had been thinking. "My mother became ill very suddenly," he said gently. "My brother and I were with her at the end. The doctors did everything they could. My father loved her in his own way. Catherine, look at the world around you. This is England, and we live in modern times. Our neighbors, our servants and our newspapers would never allow such dark secrets. Real life is not like a novel." Catherine ran to her room and cried with shame. Yet Henry never spoke of it again, and he was as kind to her as before.

Some days later, Henry went to his own house in another village. The general also went away for a few days, and he came back unexpectedly late one night. Soon afterwards, Eleanor came to Catherine's room, pale and unhappy. "My father says you must leave," she said. "You are to go early tomorrow morning, and you will travel alone. I am so sorry, Catherine. He has not given me any reason." Catherine was confused and deeply hurt, but she did not argue. The next morning, she began the long journey home by herself.

At home, her family welcomed her warmly, but Catherine was quiet and sad. A few days later, Henry arrived at her parents' house, looking tired, and he explained everything. "John Thorpe wanted to marry you himself," he said. "So in Bath, he told my father that your family was very rich. Later, when he understood that you did not love him, he became angry. Then he told my father that your family was poor. That is why my father sent you away so suddenly. But I am not my father, and I love you. Will you marry me?" Catherine happily said yes. At first, the general refused to agree. Then Eleanor married a rich and important man, and the delighted general finally gave his permission. Soon after, Henry and Catherine were married, and they were very happy together.

## Level C1

Catherine Morland was seventeen, the eldest daughter of a large, cheerful family in a country village, and as a child she had shown no sign whatever of becoming anyone's heroine. She had once preferred rolling down green slopes to practicing music, and she had shown no great love for books of any kind. By seventeen, however, she had discovered novels, and in particular the frightening kind, crowded with ruined buildings, stormy nights, mysterious strangers and terrible family secrets. Her own life was entirely peaceful, which made her wish, now and then, that something more exciting might happen to her.

Her opportunity arrived when two old friends of the family, a kind but rather dull husband and wife, decided to spend some weeks in Bath for the husband's health. Having no children of their own, they invited Catherine to come with them, and her parents agreed without much fuss. Bath was crowded and fashionable, a town where people seemed to do nothing but walk, shop, visit the famous baths and attend parties and dances every evening. At one of these dances, Catherine was introduced to a young clergyman named Henry Tilney. He was clever, amusing and fond of gentle teasing, and, to her delight, he could talk about novels with as much pleasure as she could. She went home that night thinking of very little else.

In Bath, Catherine also formed a close friendship with Isabella Thorpe, a pretty young woman a few years older, who declared that she loved novels quite as passionately as Catherine did. The two girls spent whole mornings reading together, promising eternal friendship and discussing which mysterious stories they would read next. Isabella's brother, John Thorpe, was a loud and boastful young man who talked endlessly about his horses, his carriage and his own cleverness. He was determined to win Catherine's attention, yet she found him tiresome. On one occasion he even tricked her into a carriage ride, so that she missed a walk she had promised to take with Henry and his sister. That sister, Eleanor Tilney, was quiet, sensible and gentle, and Catherine soon grew very fond of her. Meanwhile, Isabella became engaged to Catherine's brother, only to turn her attention almost at once to Henry's older and more fashionable brother. Little by little, and with real sorrow, Catherine came to understand that Isabella had never been a true friend at all.

Then, quite unexpectedly, Henry's father, General Tilney, began to treat Catherine with remarkable warmth. A tall, proud man who was used to being obeyed, he was nevertheless extremely polite to her, and he asked a surprising number of questions about her family. Before long, he invited her to stay with them at their home, Northanger Abbey. Catherine could hardly believe her good fortune. A real abbey, she told herself, would surely be full of long dark passages, crumbling towers and, if she were lucky, a secret room that nobody had entered for a hundred years.

On the journey, Henry drove her in his own small carriage, and, seeing how excited she was, he could not resist teasing her. "They will give you a room far from everyone else," he told her with a perfectly serious face, "and an old servant will leave you there with a single candle. At midnight a storm will break, and behind a heavy curtain you will discover a hidden door, which of course you will open." Catherine laughed, but she noticed, a little ashamed, that her heart was beating faster than usual.

Northanger Abbey bore little resemblance to the place she had imagined. The general loved comfort and every modern improvement, so most of the rooms were bright, warm and newly furnished, and even the windows, to her disappointment, were large and clean. Still, Catherine's bedroom contained one promising object: a large, old-fashioned chest standing in a corner. With trembling hands she lifted its heavy lid, only for Eleanor to walk in at that very moment, and inside there was nothing but a neatly folded white cover for a bed. That night, however, a strong wind howled around the old walls, and Catherine noticed a tall black cabinet that she had somehow missed before. In one of its drawers she found a roll of old papers, but at that very moment her candle went out. She lay awake in the darkness for hours, listening to the wind and imagining the worst. When morning came, she examined the papers in the cheerful daylight. They were nothing more than lists of clothes for washing and an old bill, and Catherine felt more foolish than she had ever felt in her life.

Henry's mother had died nine years earlier, and the general rarely mentioned her. He never walked along the path through the woods that she had loved, and her portrait, Catherine learned, did not hang in his part of the house. Fed by these small facts, Catherine's imagination began to work exactly like one of her novels. Perhaps, she thought, the house was hiding a sad old secret; perhaps the general knew far more about his wife's last days than he was willing to tell.

One afternoon, while the others were busy, Catherine slipped into the mother's room alone, half expecting to find some dreadful clue. Instead she found a pleasant, sunny room, neatly kept, with nothing strange in it at all. As she came out, she met Henry on the stairs, home earlier than anyone had expected, and from the direction she had come he slowly understood what she had been thinking. "My mother became ill very suddenly," he said, more gently than she deserved. "My brother and I were with her at the end. The doctors did everything they could. My father loved her in his own way, even if it was not always an easy way. Catherine, look at the world around you. This is England, and we live in modern times, where neighbors talk, servants notice everything and newspapers print every scandal. Real life, you know, is not like a novel." Catherine ran to her room and cried with shame, convinced that he could never think well of her again. Yet Henry never spoke of the matter afterwards, and he was, if anything, kinder to her than before.

Some days later, Henry went to his own house in another village, and the general also went away for a few days. He came back unexpectedly late one night, and soon afterwards Eleanor came to Catherine's room, pale and unhappy. "My father says you must leave," she said, hardly able to look at her friend. "You are to go early tomorrow morning, and you will travel alone. I am so sorry, Catherine. He has not given me any reason, and I cannot ask him for one." Catherine was confused and deeply hurt, but she was too proud to argue, and the next morning she began the long journey home by herself.

At home, her family welcomed her warmly, though they could not understand why she had become so quiet. A few days later, Henry arrived at her parents' house, tired from his journey, and he explained everything at last. "John Thorpe wanted to marry you himself," he said, "and so in Bath he boasted to my father that your family was very rich. Later, when he finally understood that you did not care for him, he grew angry, and he told my father that your family was almost poor. That is the whole reason my father sent you away so suddenly, and I am ashamed of it. But I am not my father, and I love you. Will you marry me?" Catherine, who had loved him for a long time, said yes with all her heart. At first the general refused to agree, but when Eleanor married a rich and important man, his good mood was so complete that he finally gave his permission. Soon afterwards Henry and Catherine were married, and the girl who had come to Northanger in search of dark secrets found a happy ending of her own instead.
