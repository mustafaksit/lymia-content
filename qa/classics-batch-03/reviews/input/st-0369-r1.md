# Ricky of the Tuft

Summary: A plain prince with a brilliant mind offers a beautiful princess the gift of wisdom.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): tuft, fairy, promise

## Level A1

A queen had a little child. He had a small tuft on his head. So people called him Ricky of the Tuft. Ricky was not good-looking. But a fairy gave him a great mind. He could share it with his true love.

Later, another queen had a girl. The little princess was very beautiful. But the fairy said, "She cannot think well." So the fairy gave her a power too. She could make her true love good-looking. The princess grew up. But her words often made no sense.

One day, she went into a forest. There she met Ricky, now a young prince. He saw her picture and loved her. "I cannot think well," said the princess. Ricky said, "I can help you. But you must marry me in one year." A year seemed very long to her. So she made a promise. At once, she could think very well.

Back home, everyone loved to talk with her. Many young men wanted to marry her. She liked one of them. Her father said, "You can choose." She went back to the forest to think.

There she heard people under the ground. Then the ground opened. Many people made food for a big party. The princess asked, "Who is the party for?" One man said, "It is for Prince Ricky. He marries tomorrow." Then she remembered her promise. It was one year since that day. She made it with her old mind. So she forgot it.

Then Ricky came. Ricky said, "I keep my word. Are you here to keep your promise?" "I am not sure," said the princess. "Do you like my mind?" asked Ricky. "Yes, very much," said the princess. Ricky said, "Then you can help me. The same fairy gave you a power too. You can make your true love good-looking." "Then I give you my power," she said.

At once, Ricky looked very good to her. Some people say love did this. She knew how good and kind he was. So his face did not matter to her. Her father agreed. The next day, Ricky and the princess married.

## Level A2

A queen had a baby boy. He was not good-looking. He also had a small tuft of hair on his head. So everyone called him Ricky of the Tuft. A fairy gave the baby a great mind and a special power. He could give a great mind to his true love too.

Some years later, another queen had two daughters. The first girl was very beautiful, but she could not think well. The second girl was not pretty, but she had a great mind. Their mother was very worried about the first girl. So the same fairy gave this girl a special power. She could make her true love good-looking.

The two sisters grew up. The older one often broke things, and her words made no sense. So people soon went to talk to her sister instead.

One day, the beautiful princess went alone into a forest. There she met Ricky, now a young prince, in very fine clothes. He saw pictures of the princess at home and loved her. So he left his country to find her. "I want a great mind like yours," said the princess. Ricky said, "I can give you one. But you must promise to marry me in one year." A year seemed very far away to her, so she said yes. She talked with Ricky for a long time. Now her words were full of sense.

When she came home, everyone was very surprised. Many young men came from far away to marry her. At last, she liked one of them very much. Her father said, "You can choose your husband." She wanted to think about it alone.

So she went for a walk in the same forest. Suddenly she heard many voices under the ground. Then the ground opened. Under it, many people made food for a great party. The princess asked, "Who is all this food for?" One of them said, "It is for Prince Ricky. He marries tomorrow." The princess remembered her promise. It was exactly one year since that day. She made the promise with her old mind, so she forgot it.

Then Ricky came to her. Ricky said, "I keep my word. Are you here to keep your promise?" The princess said, "I am not ready to say yes. Before, I could not choose. Now I have a great mind from you. So it is even harder." Ricky asked, "Is there something wrong with my mind or my ways?" "No, I like those things very much," said the princess. Ricky said, "Then you can make me happy. The same fairy came to you when you were born. She gave you a power too. You can make your true love good-looking." The princess said, "Then I wish it with all my heart."

At once, Ricky became the finest man in her eyes. Some people say love did this, not the fairy. The princess thought about his good mind and his kind heart. So his looks did not matter to her now. Her father knew Ricky's great mind, and he happily agreed. The next day, Ricky and the princess married, just as Ricky planned.

## Level B1

A queen gave birth to a little boy who was not at all handsome. On top of his head grew a small tuft of hair. Because of it, everyone called him Ricky of the Tuft. His mother was worried, but a fairy who came to see the baby comforted her. She said, "Your son will have a wonderful mind, and people will love him for it. He will also be able to give the same mind to the person he loves most."

Seven or eight years later, the queen of a nearby country had two daughters. The first baby was more beautiful than a summer morning. The same fairy was there, and she gave the queen some sad news. "This child will be as slow as she is lovely," the fairy said. The second daughter was very plain, but the fairy promised her a wonderful mind. The queen asked the fairy to help her first daughter as well. The fairy answered, "I cannot give her a good mind. But she will be able to make the person she loves beautiful."

The two sisters grew up. The younger one became cleverer every year, and everyone enjoyed her company. The older one stayed beautiful, but she could not say anything interesting. She often broke cups and spilled water on her dress. Visitors looked at her for a moment, then went to talk with her sister. The beautiful princess was very unhappy about this. She wanted to give away all her beauty for half of her sister's mind.

One day she went alone into a wood to cry about her bad luck. There she saw a small man in fine clothes walking toward her. It was Ricky, now a young prince. He saw her pictures at home and fell in love with them. So he left his father's kingdom to meet her. "Why is such a beautiful lady so sad?" he asked politely. "I would rather have a mind like yours than this pretty face," said the princess. Ricky smiled and said, "Only someone with a good mind believes that she has none. I can give you as much mind as you want. But you must promise to marry me in one year." The princess knew very little, and a year seemed like forever to her. So she gave her promise. She talked with Ricky for a long time, and every word she said was bright. Ricky almost believed that he gave her more mind than he kept for himself.

When she returned to the palace, the whole court was amazed. Her father the king began to ask for her advice about everything. Many princes came from distant lands to ask for her hand. At last a powerful, rich, clever and handsome prince arrived, and she liked him. "You are free to choose your husband," the king told her. To think quietly, she walked in the same wood where she met Ricky.

As she was walking, she heard a loud noise under the ground. Voices were shouting, "Bring me that pot!" and "Put more wood on the fire!" Suddenly the ground opened at her feet. She saw a huge kitchen full of cooks who were preparing a great feast. "Who are you cooking for?" she asked them. "For Prince Ricky," one cook replied. "His wedding is tomorrow." The princess suddenly remembered her promise. It was exactly one year since that day in the wood. She forgot it because she was foolish when she made it. Her new mind forgot all her old foolish days.

She walked on, and soon Ricky appeared, dressed like a prince on his wedding day. "I always keep my word," he said, "and I believe you are here to keep yours." The princess answered, "I must be honest with you. When I had no mind, I could not decide. Now I have a good mind from you, and choosing is even harder." Ricky asked, "Is there anything about me that you dislike? Is it my family, my mind, my temper or my manners?" "No, I like all of those things," said the princess. Ricky said, "Then you can make me the happiest of men. The same fairy also visited you when you were born. She gave you the power to make the person you love handsome." The princess said, "Then I wish with all my heart for you to be handsome."

At once, Ricky seemed to her the finest and most handsome man in the world. Some people say that it was not the fairy's magic at all, but love. The princess thought about his patience, his kindness and his wonderful mind. Soon she no longer noticed anything wrong with his face. She promised to marry him, but only with her father's agreement. The king knew that Ricky was a wise and good prince, so he agreed gladly. The next day, the wedding took place, just as Ricky planned it long before.

## Level B2

Once there was a queen who gave birth to a son who was far from handsome. He came into the world with a small tuft of hair on top of his head. Because of it, he was called Ricky of the Tuft. His mother might have grieved, but a fairy who was present at his birth told her not to worry. The fairy said, "He will be loved, because he will have a brilliant mind. I am also giving him one more gift. He will be able to share that mind with the person he loves best." As soon as the little prince could speak, he said such charming things that everyone was delighted with him.

Seven or eight years later, the queen of a neighboring kingdom had two daughters. The first was more beautiful than the dawn, and her mother was overjoyed. The same fairy was present, and to calm the queen's joy, she made a warning. "This princess will have no wit at all," she said. "She will be as dull as she is beautiful." The queen was deeply hurt, but then her second daughter was born, and this child was very plain. "Do not be troubled," said the fairy. "This one will be so clever that no one will mind her looks." "Then could you not give my elder daughter a little wit?" begged the queen. "I can do nothing for her mind," the fairy replied, "but I can do anything where beauty is concerned. I give her the power to make beautiful anyone she truly loves."

As the princesses grew, their gifts grew with them. The younger became wittier every year, while the elder grew lovelier and more awkward. She could not set four cups on a table without breaking one. She could not drink a glass of water without spilling half of it on her dress. At first, every visitor hurried to admire the beautiful princess. Within an hour, however, they had all drifted away to listen to her clever sister. The elder princess noticed this, and it made her miserable. She would gladly have traded all her beauty for half of her sister's wit.

One day she slipped away into a wood to weep over her bad luck. There she saw a little man coming toward her, dressed in magnificent clothes. It was young Prince Ricky, who had fallen in love with her portraits. He had left his father's kingdom just to meet her. He greeted her with great respect and asked why someone so lovely looked so sorrowful. "I would rather be plain and wise like you than beautiful and foolish like me," she said. "Nothing shows good sense more than believing you have none," Ricky answered gently. He explained that he could give her as much wit as anyone could wish for. "But you must agree to marry me," he said. "I will give you a full year to prepare." The princess wanted wit so badly that she imagined the year would never end. So she accepted, and the moment she gave her promise, she felt completely changed. She talked with Ricky easily and gracefully for a long time. Their conversation was so lively that Ricky wondered whether he had kept any wit for himself.

When she returned to the palace, the whole court was astonished by the change. Where she had once said foolish things, she now spoke with wisdom and charm. The king began to follow her advice and even held his councils in her rooms. Princes from every nearby kingdom came to ask for her hand. None of them seemed clever enough for her, until at last one arrived who was powerful, rich, witty and handsome. She felt drawn to him, and her father told her that the choice was hers. Wishing to think carefully, she went for a walk in the same wood where she had met Ricky.

As she walked, she heard a strange noise under her feet, like many people hurrying back and forth. "Bring me that pot," said one voice. "Put more wood on the fire," cried another. At that moment the ground opened, and she looked down into a vast kitchen. It was full of cooks who were preparing a splendid feast. "Who is all this for?" she asked. "For Prince Ricky of the Tuft," the head cook replied. "His wedding is tomorrow." The princess was startled, and then she remembered that exactly a year had passed since her promise. She had forgotten it because she had made it while she was still foolish. With her new mind, she had forgotten all her old follies.

She had not gone thirty steps further when Ricky appeared, dressed like a prince on his wedding day. "Here I am, as I promised," he said, "and I am sure you have come to keep your word too." "I must be frank with you," the princess replied. "I have not made up my mind. If you were a man without sense, you could simply say that a princess must keep her promises. But you are the cleverest man in the world, so you will understand me. When I was foolish, I could not bring myself to marry you. Now you have given me a mind that makes me harder to please. How can you expect me to decide today what I could not decide then?"

"Is there anything about me, apart from my looks, that you dislike?" Ricky asked. "My family, my mind, my temper, my manners?" "Not at all," said the princess. "I admire all of those things in you." "Then I can be happy," said Ricky, "because you can make me the most attractive of men. The fairy who gave me my gift at my birth also gave you a gift at yours. You can make the person you love beautiful, if only you wish it." "Then I wish with all my heart," said the princess, "that you become the most handsome prince alive."

The moment she said this, Ricky appeared to her as the finest man she had ever seen. Some say that no fairy's charm was needed; in their view, love alone made the change. The princess thought about his patience, his good sense and his kind heart. After that, she no longer saw anything wrong with his face. Whatever the reason, she promised to marry him, as long as her father agreed. The king knew Ricky to be a wise and worthy prince, and he accepted him gladly. The wedding took place the very next day, exactly as Ricky had arranged it long before.

## Level C1

There was once a queen who brought into the world a son so far from handsome that, for a while, people hardly knew what to say about him. Nature had given him one curious ornament, a small tuft of hair on the crown of his head. It gave him the name he carried all his life: Ricky of the Tuft. A fairy had been present at his birth, however, and she assured the anxious queen that the child would be loved for his truly remarkable mind. "That is not all," she added. "By my gift, he will be able to bestow as much wit as he has on the person he comes to love best." Indeed, no sooner had the boy learned to talk than he was saying such delightful things that everyone was enchanted.

Seven or eight years later, the queen of a neighboring kingdom gave birth to two daughters. The first was lovelier than the morning itself, and her mother's joy was so great that her attendants began to fear it might do her some harm. The same fairy happened to be present, and, wishing to temper that joy, she announced that the little princess would be exactly as foolish as she was beautiful. This was a bitter blow, but a sharper one followed, for the second daughter turned out to be strikingly plain. "Do not distress yourself, madam," said the fairy. "This child will have such a sparkling mind that no one will ever notice what she lacks in looks." "Heaven grant it," sighed the queen, "but is there truly no way to give my elder girl a little sense?" "Where wit is concerned, I can do nothing for her," replied the fairy, "but where beauty is concerned, I can do everything. I shall give her the power to make beautiful whomever she truly loves."

The years passed, and the two princesses grew up exactly as the fairy had foretold. The younger became more delightful in conversation every season, while the elder grew ever more beautiful and ever more awkward. She could not set four pieces of fine china on a shelf without breaking one, nor drink a glass of water without spilling half of it down her gown. Visitors to the court would gather around the beautiful sister first, gazing at her as one gazes at a painting. Before long, however, they would all drift toward the clever one, who could make them laugh. Slow as she was, the elder princess understood perfectly well what was happening, and she would willingly have traded all her beauty for half of her sister's wit.

One day, unable to bear it any longer, she slipped away into a wood to weep over her misfortune in private. There she saw a little man coming toward her, plain in face and figure, but so magnificently dressed that he could only be a person of great rank. It was young Ricky of the Tuft, who had fallen in love with her portraits and left his father's kingdom for the sole pleasure of seeing her. He greeted her with every mark of respect, and then, noticing her tears, asked how one so lovely could possibly be so unhappy. "I would far rather be as plain as you and have a good mind," she answered with touching honesty, "than remain as beautiful and as foolish as I am." "Nothing proves good sense so clearly, madam, as the belief that one has none," replied Ricky. Then he told her about his gift and offered to share it with her on one condition: that she should agree to marry him. "I will give you a whole year to grow used to the idea," he said.

The princess had so little wit, and longed for it so desperately, that she imagined the year would never come to an end, and so she gave her word. The very instant she did so, she felt a change pass through her. She found that she could say whatever she wished with ease and grace. Her talk with Ricky grew so lively and sparkling that he began to wonder whether he had given her more wit than he had kept for himself.

When she returned to the palace, the court hardly knew what to make of it. The young woman who had once babbled nonsense now spoke with good judgment and a delicate wit, and it was her sister who now found herself overlooked. The king began to consult his elder daughter on matters of state and sometimes even held his council in her apartments. Young princes from every neighboring kingdom came to court her, but she found none of them clever enough to hold her interest. At last there came one who was powerful, rich, witty and handsome, and she felt a real inclination toward him. Her father told her that the choice of a husband was entirely her own, and, being sensible, she asked for time. To reflect in peace, she went walking, and chance led her into the very wood where she had met Ricky a year before.

As she wandered deep in thought, she heard a muffled noise beneath her feet, like a crowd of people hurrying to and fro. Listening more carefully, she made out separate voices: "Fetch me that pot," called one; "Hand me that kettle," called another; "More wood on the fire," cried a third. At that very moment the ground opened before her, and she found herself looking down into an enormous kitchen crowded with cooks, all busy preparing a magnificent feast. Astonished, the princess asked them for whom all this splendor was intended. "For Prince Ricky of the Tuft, madam," answered the head cook. "His wedding is tomorrow." The princess was more astonished than ever, until suddenly she remembered that it was exactly one year since she had promised to marry him. She had forgotten it for a simple reason: she had made it while she was still foolish, and her new mind had lost all memory of her old follies.

She had not gone thirty steps further when Ricky came toward her, gallant and splendid, dressed like a prince on the eve of his wedding. "You see, madam, I keep my word to the letter," he said, "and I have no doubt that you have come here to keep yours and make me the happiest of men." "I will be frank with you," replied the princess. "I have not yet made up my mind, and I fear I shall never make it up the way you would like. Were you a dull and stubborn man, I should be in an awkward position, since you would simply remind me that a princess must keep her word. But you are the cleverest man in the world, and I am sure you will listen to reason. When I was a fool, I still could not bring myself to marry you. Now that your gift has made me far harder to please, how can you ask me to decide today what I could not decide then? If you truly wished to marry me, you were wrong to take away my foolishness and let me see more clearly than before."

"If a dull man would be right to hold you to your promise," answered Ricky, "why should I not have the same right, when my whole happiness depends on it? But tell me honestly: apart from my plain looks, is there anything in me that displeases you? Are you unhappy with my family, my mind, my temper or my manners?" "Not in the least," said the princess. "I admire every one of those things in you." "In that case I shall be happy," said Ricky, "for it is in your power to make me the most pleasing of men. The same fairy who stood beside my cradle also stood beside yours. She gave you the power to make beautiful the one you love, if only you choose to use it." "If that is so," said the princess, "then with all my heart I wish you to become the most handsome prince in the world. I give you the gift as fully as it is mine to give."

No sooner had she spoken than Ricky appeared before her eyes as the handsomest, most graceful man she had ever seen. There are those who insist the fairy's gift had nothing to do with it; for them, love alone worked the transformation. The princess, they say, had reflected on her suitor's patience, his discretion and all the fine qualities of his heart and mind. In the end, the face she had once thought plain simply ceased to matter, and she saw in it only the man himself. Whatever the truth may be, she promised there and then to marry him, provided that her father gave his consent. The king, who knew his daughter's high regard for Ricky and the prince's wisdom, welcomed him gladly as a son-in-law. The wedding took place the very next morning, just as Ricky had foreseen, and according to the orders he had given long before.
