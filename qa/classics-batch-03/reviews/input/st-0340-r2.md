# Persuasion

Summary: Years after a friend talks her out of marriage, Anne Elliot meets her lost love again.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): navy, letter, captain

## Level A1

Anne Elliot lived in a big old house. Her father was Sir Walter. He loved money and nice things. He did not listen to his daughter Anne.

Eight years ago, Anne loved a young man. His name was Frederick Wentworth. He was in the navy, with no money. Lady Russell was like a mother to Anne. Lady Russell said, "Do not marry him. He has no money and no home." So Anne said no to Wentworth. He went away to sea. Anne still loved him.

Now Sir Walter had very little money. Other people paid to live in his house. Then Wentworth came back. He was a captain now, with money. Anne met him again. But Wentworth did not talk much to her. He spent his time with Louisa Musgrove.

The young people visited Lyme by the sea. They walked on a high wall. Louisa ran up some steps. "Catch me!" she said to Wentworth. She jumped too soon, and he missed her. Louisa fell down and did not move.

The others did not know what to do. But Anne did. "Get a doctor!" said Anne. She told the others how to help. Wentworth saw her strong and clear mind.

Louisa stayed in bed for many weeks. Then she was well again. Some time later, she married another navy captain.

Anne went to her family in Bath. Wentworth came to Bath too. Anne talked with a friend of Wentworth's. Wentworth sat near them. Anne said, "Women love for a long time. We love, even with no hope." Wentworth heard her words. He wrote Anne a letter. He left it near her and went out.

Anne read the letter. Wentworth wrote, "I still love you. I love only you. Please give me a word of hope." Anne's heart was full. Soon Wentworth found Anne outside. They walked and talked. This time, Anne said yes. Lady Russell liked Wentworth now. Anne married him, and she was very happy.

## Level A2

Sir Walter lived in a large old house in the country. He loved his good looks, his family name and his money. He had three daughters, and Anne Elliot was the second. Anne was kind, but her father almost never listened to her.

Eight years before, Anne fell in love with Frederick Wentworth. He was a young navy officer with no money. The two young people wanted to marry. But Lady Russell, an old friend of the family, was against it. She was like a mother to Anne. Lady Russell told her, "Do not marry him. He has no money, and life at sea is hard." Anne listened to her friend, so she said no to Wentworth. He went away to sea, and Anne never forgot him.

Eight years later, Sir Walter had very little money. So a navy family paid to live in his house. Sir Walter moved to Bath. The wife in this family was Wentworth's sister. Anne went to stay with her younger sister in the country. Soon Wentworth came there too. He was a captain now, and he had a lot of money. But he was cold to Anne. He spent his time with Louisa Musgrove, a young and happy girl.

The young people took a short trip to Lyme by the sea. They walked along a high wall by the water. Louisa liked to jump down the steps to Wentworth. He caught her once, and she laughed. "Again!" she said, and she ran up the steps. "No, it is too high," said Wentworth. But Louisa jumped too soon, and he missed her. She fell to the ground and did not move.

The others did not know what to do. Wentworth held Louisa in his arms. "What can we do?" he said. Only Anne thought clearly. She sent a young navy officer to get a doctor. Then she told the others what to do. Wentworth watched Anne, and he saw her strong mind.

Louisa stayed in bed for many weeks, but then she got well. During that time, she fell in love with another navy captain. In the end, she married him.

Anne went to her family in Bath. Soon Wentworth came to Bath too. Then Anne visited some friends at a hotel. A friend of Wentworth's, another navy captain, was there too. Wentworth sat at a table near them and wrote. "Men love for a longer time than women," said the friend. Anne said, "No, women love longer, even with no hope." Wentworth heard every word. Then he put a letter in front of Anne and went out.

Wentworth wrote, "I must speak to you now. My heart is still yours, and I love only you. Please give me one word or one look." Anne's heart was full. She went out into the street, and Wentworth soon found her there. They walked together and talked about the past. This time, Anne said yes. Lady Russell was happy for them. Even Sir Walter agreed, because the captain now had money. Anne married Wentworth, and at last she was truly happy.

## Level B1

Sir Walter was a proud man who lived in a large old house in the country. He cared about three things: his good looks, his family name and his money. He had three daughters, and Anne Elliot was the second. Anne was clever, gentle and kind, but her father hardly noticed her.

Eight years earlier, when Anne was nineteen, she fell in love with Frederick Wentworth. He was a young officer in the navy, bright, brave and full of hope. He had no money, but he loved Anne deeply. They planned to marry. Lady Russell, an old friend of the family, was against the match. Anne's mother was dead, and Lady Russell was like a second mother to her. Lady Russell said, "He has nothing but his hopes, my dear. Life at sea is dangerous, and you are too young to wait for him." Anne trusted her old friend, so she ended the engagement. Wentworth was hurt, and he soon left for the sea. Anne never stopped loving him, but she never spoke of him either.

Eight years later, Sir Walter was in trouble. He spent far more money than he had. To save money, he rented his fine house to a navy admiral and moved to Bath. The admiral's wife was Wentworth's sister. Anne went to stay with her younger sister in a village nearby. Soon Wentworth arrived to visit his sister, and he often came to the village. He was now a captain, and he was rich from his success in the war. When he met Anne again, he was polite but cold. Instead, he spent his days with Louisa Musgrove, a lively young woman from the village. Everyone expected him to marry her, and Anne quietly watched.

The young people took a short trip to Lyme, a small town by the sea. They walked together along a high stone wall that ran out into the water. There were steep steps on the wall, and Louisa loved to jump down them. Wentworth caught her once, and she laughed with joy. "Again!" she cried, and she ran back up the steps. Wentworth warned her that it was too high, but she did not listen. She jumped a moment too soon, and his hands missed her. Louisa fell to the ground and lay completely still, with her eyes closed.

For a moment, everyone stood frozen with fear. Louisa's sister fainted, and Anne's younger sister began to cry wildly. Wentworth lifted Louisa in his arms, white and helpless. "What can we do?" he cried. Only Anne stayed calm. She rubbed Louisa's hands and sent a young navy captain to find a doctor. She told each person what to do, and they all followed her orders. Wentworth watched her with new respect.

Louisa was very ill for some weeks, but slowly she got better. As she got better, she fell in love with the captain who found the doctor. In the end, the two of them married. Wentworth was free again, and he began to think about Anne more and more.

Anne joined her father and her older sister in Bath. Her charming cousin paid her a lot of attention, but Anne did not trust him. Soon Wentworth came to Bath too. He saw Anne with her cousin, and he became jealous. They met at a concert, but they could only speak for a few minutes.

A few days later, Anne visited friends at a hotel. Wentworth was there too, writing a letter at a table near the window. Anne began to talk with Wentworth's friend, another navy captain. They talked about men and women, and about which of them loves longer. "Men love longer and more truly," said the friend. Anne shook her head. "No," she said, "we women love longer, even when there is no hope left." Wentworth's pen stopped, and he listened to every word.

Soon Wentworth left the room with his friend. A moment later he came back alone. He took a letter from under some papers and put it in front of Anne. Then he left again without a word. Wentworth wrote, "I heard your words, and I must speak. My heart belongs only to you, just as it did eight years ago. Give me one word or one look, and I will understand."

Anne read the letter again and again, and her heart was full. She hurried out into the street, and Wentworth soon caught up with her. They walked slowly together, and at last they talked openly about the past. This time, Anne said yes. Lady Russell admitted her mistake and welcomed Wentworth warmly. Even Sir Walter agreed, because the captain was now rich and successful. Anne married the man she loved, and she was truly happy.

## Level B2

Sir Walter was a vain man who lived in a large country house and admired himself in every mirror he passed. What he valued most were his handsome face, his ancient family name and the money he spent so freely. Of his three daughters, he loved the eldest best, because she was beautiful and proud like him. Anne Elliot, the second, was clever, gentle and thoughtful, yet at home her opinion counted for almost nothing.

Eight years earlier, at nineteen, Anne had fallen deeply in love with Frederick Wentworth. He was a young officer in the navy, confident and warm, and he was sure that his fortune would come. He had no money and no important relations, but he loved Anne with all his heart, and they became engaged. Lady Russell, an old friend of the family, was deeply worried by the match. Since the death of Anne's mother, she had been like a second mother to the girl, and Anne trusted her completely. She urged Anne to think carefully about the future. "He has nothing but his confidence, my dear. Life at sea is uncertain, and you deserve a safer future than this." After many painful days, Anne was persuaded to break the engagement. She believed she was acting for his good as much as her own. Wentworth was hurt and angry, and he left for the sea soon afterward. Anne never stopped loving him, but she never spoke of him to anyone.

Eight years later, Sir Walter discovered that he had spent far more than he could afford. To save money, he rented his fine house to a navy admiral and moved to Bath with his eldest daughter. By chance, the admiral's wife was Wentworth's sister. Anne was sent to stay with her younger sister, who was married and lived in a village nearby. Before long, Wentworth arrived to visit his sister, and he often came to the village. He was now a captain, and he had become rich through his success in the war. When he met Anne again, he was polite but distant, and his coldness hurt her deeply. Later she heard that he had found her so changed he hardly knew her. Instead of talking to Anne, he spent his days with Louisa Musgrove, a lively young woman from the village. Soon everyone expected him to marry Louisa, and Anne watched quietly from the side.

One day, the young people made a short trip to Lyme, a small town by the sea. They walked along a high stone wall that stretched out into the water, enjoying the fresh wind. Some steep steps led down from the upper part of the wall, and Louisa loved to jump down them into Wentworth's hands. He caught her once, and she laughed with delight. "Again!" she cried, running back up the steps. Wentworth warned her that it was too high, but she was determined to have her way. She jumped a moment too soon, before he was ready, and his hands missed her. Louisa fell to the ground and lay completely still, her eyes closed and her face pale.

For a terrible moment, nobody could move. Louisa's sister fainted, and Anne's younger sister burst into tears. Wentworth lifted Louisa in his arms, pale and helpless, and cried out for someone to help him. Anne was the only one who did not panic. She rubbed Louisa's hands, gave clear instructions, and sent a young navy captain to fetch a doctor. One by one, the others did exactly as she said. Wentworth watched her with a look of new respect. Afterward, Anne heard him say that no one was as capable as she was.

Louisa was taken to a house nearby, and the doctor arrived quickly. She was very ill for some weeks, but in time she recovered. While she was getting well, she grew close to the young captain who had fetched the doctor. To everyone's surprise, the two of them became engaged and later married. Wentworth was free again, and he realized that his heart had never really left Anne.

Anne joined her father and her elder sister in Bath, a busy town full of parties, concerts and fine people. Before long, her cousin, a smooth and charming man, began to pay her a great deal of attention. Many people said that he wished to marry her, but Anne did not fully trust him. Soon Wentworth came to Bath as well. When he saw Anne with her cousin, he became jealous and unsure of himself. They met at a concert, but they could only speak for a few minutes before others interrupted them.

A few days later, Anne visited friends at a hotel where they were staying. Wentworth was there too, writing a letter at a table near the window. Anne fell into conversation with Wentworth's friend, another navy captain. They began to argue, kindly, about whether men or women love longer. "Men love longer and more truly," said the friend, "and they feel things more strongly." Anne shook her head. "No," she said, "the only advantage we women have is that we keep loving, even after hope has gone." At the table, Wentworth's pen fell silent, and he listened to every word.

Shortly afterward, Wentworth left the room with his friend. A minute later he returned alone, saying that he had forgotten his gloves. He drew a letter from under some scattered papers and placed it in front of Anne. Then he left again without a word. The letter was addressed to her. Wentworth wrote, "I heard every word you said, and I cannot stay silent any longer. My heart has belonged only to you since we first met, eight years ago. I came to Bath for you alone. Give me one word or one look, and I will know whether to hope."

Anne read the letter again and again, overwhelmed with happiness. She hurried out into the street, and Wentworth soon caught up with her. They walked slowly together, and at last they spoke openly about the past. He told her about his anger and his jealousy, and she told him about her years of silent regret. This time, Anne said yes without any doubt. Lady Russell admitted that she had been wrong, and she welcomed Wentworth warmly. Even Sir Walter agreed to the match, because the captain was now rich and respected. Anne married the man she had always loved, and she was truly happy.

## Level C1

Sir Walter was a man of boundless vanity, who lived in a handsome house in the country and found his own reflection the most agreeable company in it. He valued three things above all others: his fine looks, his ancient family name and the comfortable life that his money allowed him, though the money was fast running out. Of his three daughters, he favored the eldest, who shared both his beauty and his pride. Anne Elliot, the second, was clever, gentle and perceptive, yet in her father's house her voice carried no weight whatsoever.

Eight years earlier, when she was only nineteen, Anne had fallen in love with Frederick Wentworth, a young officer in the navy. He was quick, warm and supremely confident, convinced that the sea would one day make his fortune, and for a few happy months they were engaged. Yet he had neither money nor connections, and to Lady Russell, the old friend who had guided Anne since her mother's death, the match looked like folly. Lady Russell loved Anne as a daughter, and Anne, in turn, trusted her judgment more than her own. She reasoned with the girl patiently, never raising her voice. "He has nothing to offer you but his confidence, my dear. A life at sea is uncertain from one year to the next, and you are far too young to tie yourself to a man with no future yet." Worn down by these gentle arguments, Anne was persuaded at last to break the engagement, telling herself that she was acting as much for his sake as for her own. Wentworth, wounded and resentful, could not forgive what he saw as weakness, and within weeks he had returned to the sea. In the years that followed, Anne never stopped loving him, though she never once spoke his name.

Eight years later, Sir Walter could no longer ignore the bills that had been piling up on his desk. Persuaded at last to economize, he rented his house to a navy admiral and retreated to Bath with his eldest daughter, where life was cheaper and the company more flattering. By a curious twist of fate, the admiral's wife turned out to be Wentworth's sister. Anne, as usual, was sent where she was useful, to stay with her younger sister, who had married into a cheerful family in a village nearby. It was not long before Wentworth came to visit his sister, and soon he was a regular guest in the village. He was a captain now, and the war had made him rich, so that the very fortune Lady Russell had doubted was firmly in his hands. Toward Anne, he was perfectly polite and perfectly distant, which wounded her more than open anger ever could. She learned, through her sister, that he had found her so altered he would scarcely have known her. Instead he devoted his attention to Louisa Musgrove, a lively, warm-hearted young woman from the big house in the village, who admired him openly. Before long the whole neighborhood was predicting a wedding, and Anne watched from the edge of the room, saying nothing.

The young people then made a short excursion to Lyme, a small town on the coast, where the sea air and the grand views delighted them all. They strolled along a high stone wall that curved out into the water, and at one point a flight of steep steps led down from its upper level. Louisa had taken a fancy to jumping down these steps into Wentworth's hands, and on the first occasion he caught her easily, to her great delight. Flushed with success, she ran back up and insisted on doing it again. Wentworth warned her that the drop was too great, but Louisa, who prided herself on her firmness of mind, would not be talked out of it. She sprang a moment too soon, before he was ready, and his hands closed on empty air. Louisa fell to the ground below. She lay there utterly still, eyes shut, face drained of color.

For a long and dreadful moment, no one could do anything at all. Louisa's sister fainted, Anne's younger sister broke into hysterical cries, and Wentworth, lifting Louisa in his arms, stood white and helpless, calling out for someone to help him. It was Anne alone who kept her composure. She knelt beside Louisa, rubbed her hands, and, in a low and steady voice, sent a young navy captain running for a doctor. One after another she gave her quiet instructions, and one after another the others obeyed, grateful that someone knew what to do. Wentworth watched her throughout, and something in his expression changed. Afterward, Anne overheard him say that there was no one so capable as she was, and the words stayed with her for many days.

Louisa was carried to a house nearby, where the doctor arrived without delay. Her recovery was slow, and for some weeks she remained very ill, but gradually her strength returned. During those quiet weeks, she grew close to the same young captain who had run for the doctor, a gentle, bookish man still mourning an earlier loss. To the astonishment of everyone who knew them, the two became engaged, and in due course they married. Wentworth found himself unexpectedly free, and he was forced to admit that his heart had never truly left Anne. What he had called her weakness, he now began to see as something closer to duty, however mistaken.

Anne, meanwhile, had joined her father and her elder sister in Bath, a busy and elegant town of concerts, card parties and endless visits. Within weeks, her cousin, a polished and charming man, began to pay her particular attention, and many people assumed he meant to marry her. Anne found his manners pleasant but could never quite trust them, for there was something too smooth in his perfect agreement with everyone. When Wentworth arrived in Bath and saw the cousin at her side, he became restless and unsure of himself. They met at a concert and exchanged a few words full of hidden meaning, but others interrupted them before either could say more.

A few days later, Anne called on friends at the hotel where they were staying. Wentworth was there as well, seated at a table by the window and writing a letter on behalf of his friend, another navy captain. Anne and this friend drifted into a good-natured argument about whether men or women love longer, and both defended their side with feeling. "Men love longer and more truly," he insisted, "and they feel things more deeply, as all the old books and songs will tell you." Anne smiled but would not give way. "Those books were written by men," she replied, "and if we women can boast of anything, it is that we go on loving when there is nothing left to hope for." At the table behind them, Wentworth's pen had fallen silent, and he was listening to every word she spoke.

Shortly afterward, Wentworth left the room with his friend, only to return a minute later alone, murmuring something about his gloves. Without looking at anyone else, he drew a letter from beneath some scattered papers, laid it before Anne, and was gone again before she could speak. The letter was addressed to her, in a hand she knew at once. Wentworth wrote, "Every word you spoke reached me, and I must answer you in the only way I can. My heart has belonged to you, and to no one else, from the day we first met, eight years ago. You say that men forget, but I have never forgotten, and it was for you alone that I came to Bath. One word from you, or even one look, will tell me whether I may hope."

Anne read it twice, then a third time, scarcely able to breathe for happiness. She hurried out into the street, and before she had gone far, Wentworth caught up with her. They walked slowly together, choosing the quietest paths, and at last they spoke openly about everything that had passed between them. He confessed his anger and his jealousy, and she told him of her long years of silent regret, and neither of them had ever been happier. This time Anne accepted him without the slightest doubt. Lady Russell, to her credit, admitted that she had misjudged him, and she set about learning to like him with real goodwill. Even Sir Walter gave his consent readily enough, since a rich and respected captain was no longer beneath the notice of an Elliot. So Anne married the man she had never stopped loving, and found in him the happiness she had once been persuaded to give up.
