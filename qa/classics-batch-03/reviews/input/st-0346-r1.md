# What Katy Did

Summary: A wild and careless girl wants to be good, but one forbidden swing changes everything.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): swing, family, patience

## Level A1

Katy Carr was twelve years old. She was the oldest of six children. Clover, Elsie and Johnnie were her sisters. Dorry and Phil were her brothers. Their mother was dead. So Aunt Izzie took care of the house. Papa was a doctor and worked all day.

Katy wanted to be good and kind. But things often went wrong for her. She ran and played and broke things. She did not always remember the rules.

A new swing hung in the shed. "Do not use it today," said Aunt Izzie. But Aunt Izzie did not say why. A part of the swing was broken. That afternoon Katy was angry with Aunt Izzie. She went to the swing alone. She went higher and higher. Then the swing broke, and Katy fell. Katy could not get up.

Papa looked at her back. "You must stay in bed," said Papa. Weeks and months went by. Katy still could not walk. Katy kept her room dark. She cried and was cross with everyone.

Then Cousin Helen came for a visit. Cousin Helen could not walk either. But she always had a happy face. "Your room is a school," said Cousin Helen. "Here you can learn patience. You can learn to keep your room nice. And you can learn to hope."

Katy tried, but it was hard. She let the light into her room. She put her things in order. Soon the children came to her every day. She read to them and helped them.

Then Aunt Izzie got sick and died. The whole family was very sad. Now Katy took care of the house. Everyone came to her room with questions. She was the heart of the home.

Almost four years went by. Slowly Katy began to stand again. One day Cousin Helen came back. Katy walked to the door to meet her. "Katy, you can walk!" said Cousin Helen. "Yes," said Katy. "I had a good teacher."

## Level A2

Katy Carr was twelve years old. She was the oldest of six children in the Carr family. After Katy came Clover, Elsie, Dorry, Johnnie and Phil. Johnnie was a little girl, but Dorry and Phil were boys. Their mother died when Phil was a baby. Papa was a doctor, and he was busy from morning to night. So Aunt Izzie, his sister, took care of the house.

Katy was tall, and her hair was always in her eyes. She dreamed of great things. Every morning she said to herself, "Today I want to be good." But by night she always had a long list of mistakes. She broke things, lost things and forgot the rules. Aunt Izzie often spoke to her in a hard voice.

One summer the children found a new swing in the shed. At breakfast Aunt Izzie said, "Do not use the swing today." "Why not?" asked Katy. "Because I say so," said Aunt Izzie. A part of the swing was broken. Aunt Izzie knew this, but she did not explain. That afternoon Katy was cross with Aunt Izzie. She went to the shed alone and got on the swing. She went higher and higher, almost up to the top. Suddenly the swing broke, and Katy fell to the floor. She could not get up.

Papa carried her to her bed. He looked at her back for a long time. "You must stay in bed, Katy," said Papa. "Your back needs a long rest. Perhaps you can walk again one day, but not soon." Weeks went by, and then months. Katy kept her room dark, and her things were everywhere. She cried a lot and spoke unkindly to everyone.

Then Cousin Helen came to stay for a few days. Cousin Helen could not walk either. She spent her days on a long chair. But her face was always bright and kind. "Katy, think of your room as a school," said Cousin Helen. "There are many lessons in this school. The first lesson is patience. Then you learn to be cheerful and keep things nice. The last lesson is hope." Katy cried, but she listened.

After Cousin Helen went home, Katy tried hard. She opened her window and put flowers on the table. She kept her room clean and pretty. Soon the children came to her room every day. She told them stories and helped them with their school work. Little by little, Katy's room became the center of the house.

Then Aunt Izzie got sick and died. The whole family was very sad. Now Katy took care of the house from her bed. The cook came to her with questions every morning. Katy planned the food and watched over the younger children. Papa called Katy the heart of the home.

Almost four years went by. One day Katy stood up by herself. Every day she took a few more steps. Then Cousin Helen came again. Katy walked to the door all by herself to meet her. "Katy, you can walk!" cried Cousin Helen. "Yes," said Katy. "I had a very good teacher."

## Level B1

Katy Carr was twelve years old and the oldest of six children. After her came Clover, Elsie, Dorry, Johnnie and Phil. Johnnie had a boy's name, but she was a little girl. Their mother died when Phil was a baby. Papa was a doctor who worked from early morning until late at night. So his sister, Aunt Izzie, looked after the house and the children. Aunt Izzie was neat and strict, and she did not understand children very well.

Katy was tall and thin, with long arms and legs, and always in a hurry. Her head was full of plans and stories. She dreamed of being beautiful and good, and of doing something great one day. Every morning she decided to be gentle and patient with everyone. By evening, though, there was always a long list of mistakes. She tore her dresses, lost her things and forgot what Aunt Izzie told her. "Katy, please try to be more careful!" Aunt Izzie said again and again.

One summer the children were delighted with a new swing in the woodshed. On the third morning, Aunt Izzie told them not to use the swing that day. She did not give a reason. In fact, one of the iron hooks which held the rope had a crack in it. A man was coming to fix it the next day. That afternoon Katy was cross with Aunt Izzie. She thought the order was just another of Aunt Izzie's silly rules. She went into the woodshed alone and began to swing. Higher and higher she flew, until her feet almost touched the roof. Then the hook broke, and Katy fell to the floor. She lay still, and she could not get up.

Papa carried her upstairs and looked at her back for a long time. His face was serious. "You must lie in bed and keep very still," said Papa. "You will get better, but it will take a long time." At first Katy thought a long time meant a week or two. But the weeks became months, and still she could not stand. She kept the curtains closed and lay in the dark for hours. She was cross with everyone, and she often cried. Soon the other children stopped coming to see her.

Then Cousin Helen came to visit. For many years Cousin Helen could not walk, and she spent her days on a sofa. Still, she was the most cheerful person Katy knew. She sat by Katy's bed and listened to all her sad words. Then Cousin Helen began to talk about a school. "You are in a new school now, Katy," she said. "It is a hard school, but its lessons are good ones. The first lesson is patience. Then there is the lesson of being cheerful. Next you learn to keep your room neat and pretty. The last lesson is hope, and you must never forget it." Katy did not understand everything, but she promised to try.

After Cousin Helen went home, Katy opened the curtains and let the sun in. She asked for flowers and kept her table tidy. She learned to smile at the people who came into her room. Slowly the children started to come back. Clover brought her sewing, and Elsie came for help with her lessons. The boys came for stories. Before long, Katy's room was the place where everyone wanted to be. Papa said that she was becoming the heart of the house.

Then Aunt Izzie got sick and died. Everyone was very sad, and Katy was sorry for all her angry words. Now there was nobody to run the house except Katy. Every morning the cook came upstairs, and they planned the meals together. Katy kept the accounts and made sure the children went to school on time. Clover and Elsie helped her, and the house ran well.

Almost four years passed. One day Katy found that she could stand by herself for a moment. Every day after that she took a few more steps, holding on to the furniture. Only Papa knew, because Katy wanted to surprise someone. Then Cousin Helen came to visit again. Katy walked to the door all by herself to meet her. "Katy, you can walk!" cried Cousin Helen, and she held out her arms. Katy laughed and said, "I had a very good teacher."

## Level B2

Katy Carr was twelve years old, the eldest of the six Carr children, and easily the tallest girl in her class. After her came Clover, Elsie, Dorry, Johnnie and little Phil. Johnnie, in spite of her name, was a small girl with a round face and a loud laugh. Their mother died when Phil was still a baby, and the children hardly remembered her. Papa was the town doctor, so he left the house early and often came home long after dark. His sister, Aunt Izzie, had come to keep house for him, and she ran it with great energy. She was honest and hardworking, but she had never been a noisy child herself. She could not understand why anyone would want to climb onto a roof.

Katy, unfortunately, was exactly that sort of child. Her arms and legs seemed too long for her, her hair was always coming loose, and she was forever in a hurry. Her head was crowded with stories, and in her daydreams she was beautiful, gentle and admired by everybody. Each morning she made a fresh resolution to be patient, to speak softly and to set a good example to the others. Each evening, however, she had to count up a long list of disasters. She tore her dresses on fences, left her books out in the rain and forgot almost every message she was given. "Katy, will you never learn to be careful?" Aunt Izzie would sigh, and Katy would promise to do better tomorrow.

One summer a new swing was put up in the woodshed, and for two days the children could talk of nothing else. On the third morning, at breakfast, Aunt Izzie announced that nobody was to use the swing that day. She gave no reason, because she believed that children should obey without asking questions. The truth was simple: one of the iron hooks had cracked. A man was coming to mend it the next morning. Had Aunt Izzie explained this, the whole story might have been different. That afternoon Katy was cross with Aunt Izzie, and the order seemed to her just one more of her aunt's pointless rules. She slipped into the woodshed alone, sat down on the swing and pushed off. Higher and higher she went, until her toes nearly brushed the roof and the whole shed seemed to rush past her. Then, at the very top of a swing, the cracked hook gave way, and Katy fell to the floor. When she tried to get up, she found that she could not.

Papa carried her upstairs and examined her back for a long time without saying a word. Then he sat down beside the bed. "Your back has been hurt, Katy," said Papa quietly. "You must lie still and be very patient. You will get well, but it will take time, and nobody can say exactly how long." Katy imagined a week or two at most. Instead the weeks turned into months, and still she could not stand. She had the curtains drawn, so that the room stayed dim all day, and she refused to let anybody tidy it. She cried for hours, grumbled at everyone and wished the whole world would go away. Little by little, the other children stopped coming in, and the house grew quiet around her.

Then Cousin Helen came for a short visit. Cousin Helen had been unable to walk for many years and spent her days on a sofa. Yet she was the brightest, most cheerful person Katy had ever met. Her dresses were pretty, her room at home was always full of flowers, and she never complained. She sat by Katy's bed, listened to every bitter word and did not scold her once. When Katy had finished, Cousin Helen began to talk about a school. "You have been sent to a new school, Katy," she said. "It is a hard school, and the lessons take a long time, but they are worth learning. The first lesson is patience. Next you study cheerfulness, of keeping your room neat and pleasant, and of making the best of things. The last lesson is hope, and that is the one you must hold on to most tightly." Katy did not understand all of it, but she promised to try.

After Cousin Helen went home, Katy asked for the curtains to be opened and the window to be pushed up. She wanted flowers on her table, a clean cloth beside the bed and her books arranged in a neat row. She practiced smiling when people came in, even on days when she did not feel like it. Slowly the children began to return. Clover brought her sewing, Elsie came for help with her homework, and the boys came to hear stories. Before long, Katy's room had become the place where everyone wanted to be. Papa said she was turning into the heart of the house.

Then Aunt Izzie got sick and died. The family was deeply sad, and Katy was ashamed of every unkind word she had ever said to her aunt. With Aunt Izzie gone, there was nobody to run the house except Katy herself. Every morning the cook climbed the stairs, and the two of them planned the day's meals together. Katy kept the household accounts, settled the children's quarrels and made sure everybody left for school on time. Clover and Elsie became her willing helpers, and the house ran more smoothly than anyone had expected.

Almost four years passed in this way. One afternoon Katy discovered that she could stand by herself for a moment, holding on to the edge of the bed. From then on she practiced every day, taking a few more steps each time and gripping the furniture as she went. She told nobody but Papa, because she had a surprise in mind. When Cousin Helen arrived for her second visit, Katy walked to the door all by herself to meet her. "Katy, you can walk!" cried Cousin Helen, holding out her arms. Katy laughed and said that she had simply had a very good teacher.

## Level C1

Katy Carr was twelve years old, the eldest of six motherless children, and so tall for her age that she was perpetually knocking her elbows against door frames. Below her, in a descending staircase of heads, came Clover, Elsie, Dorry, Johnnie and little Phil, the last of them barely more than a toddler. Johnnie, despite a name that misled every visitor, was a sturdy small girl with a laugh that could be heard from the garden gate. Their mother had died when Phil was a baby, and the younger ones kept only a faint, soft memory of her. Papa, the town doctor, was a kind and clever man, but sick people did not keep office hours, and he was seldom at home before dark. The running of the household therefore fell to his sister, Aunt Izzie, who had taken it on with more energy than imagination. She was honest, tireless and fond of the children in her own way. She simply could not grasp why any child would wish to climb onto a roof, wade through a ditch or invent a game that ended in torn stockings.

Katy, unhappily, was precisely the kind of child who did all three before lunch. Her limbs seemed to belong to somebody larger, her hair escaped from every ribbon, and she moved through life at a gallop. Inside her head, however, an entirely different Katy existed: graceful, beautiful and so wonderfully good that the whole town spoke of her with admiration. Every morning the real Katy resolved to become this imaginary one, to speak gently, to be patient with the little ones and to set an example worth following. Every evening she was obliged to count the damage instead: a dress caught on a fence, a library book abandoned in the rain, an errand forgotten halfway down the street. "Katy, will you never learn to be careful?" Aunt Izzie would ask, with the weary air of someone who already knew the answer. And Katy, flushed and sorry, would promise that tomorrow would be different.

One summer a new swing was hung from the beams of the woodshed, and for two whole days the children talked of little else. On the third morning, at breakfast, Aunt Izzie announced that nobody was to use the swing that day, and she offered no reason whatever. In her view, an order that needed explaining was an order already half disobeyed. The reason, as it happened, was perfectly sensible: one of the iron hooks holding the rope had cracked, and a man was coming to mend it the following morning. One sentence of explanation would have changed everything, but that sentence was never spoken. By the afternoon Katy was cross with Aunt Izzie. The forbidden swing began to look like nothing more than another of her aunt's arbitrary rules, made only to spoil a perfectly good summer. So she slipped into the dim woodshed alone, settled herself on the seat and pushed off from the floor. Up she went, and back, and up again, each time a little higher, until her toes almost brushed the roof and the open doorway flashed past like a picture in a book. At the very top of one long sweep, the cracked hook gave way, and Katy fell to the floor of the woodshed. When she tried to rise, her body simply would not obey her.

Papa carried her upstairs, examined her back for a long while in silence, and then drew a chair close to the bed. "Your back has been injured, Katy," said Papa, very gently. "You must lie still, and you must be patient. You will get better, I believe, but it will be slow, and I cannot promise you how slow." Katy, who had never in her life waited patiently for anything, assumed he meant a week, or perhaps a fortnight. The weeks, however, stretched into months, and still her legs would not carry her. She had the curtains drawn so that the room lay in a permanent gray twilight, and she would allow no one to tidy it. Medicine bottles crowded the table, books slid unread to the floor, and Katy lay amid the clutter, weeping for hours and snapping at anyone who came near. One by one the children found reasons to stay away, and the life of the house seemed to flow past her door without her.

It was at this low point that Cousin Helen came to stay for a few days. Cousin Helen had not walked for many years and spent her days stretched out on a sofa, yet Katy had never met anyone so serene or so full of light. Her dresses were pretty, her hair was always beautifully done, her room at home was famous among the cousins for its flowers, and nobody had ever heard her complain. She sat beside Katy's bed and listened to the whole bitter story without once interrupting or scolding. Only when Katy had cried herself quiet did Cousin Helen begin to speak, and what she spoke of, surprisingly, was a school. "You have been sent to a new school, dear," she said. "It is a hard one, and its lessons take years rather than weeks, but nothing you could learn anywhere else is half so valuable. The first lesson is patience. Then come cheerfulness, and keeping your room neat and pleasant for the people who visit it, and the art of making the best of things. The last lesson is hope, and that one you must never, ever let go of." Katy understood perhaps half of this, but the half she understood was enough to make her promise to try.

When Cousin Helen had gone home, the first thing Katy did was to ask for the curtains to be pulled back and the window raised. Sunlight fell across the bed for the first time in months, and the room, cleared of its clutter, looked suddenly larger. She asked for flowers on the table and kept her books in a tidy row. She made herself smile when people came in, even on mornings when smiling was the last thing she felt like doing. It was astonishing how quickly the change was noticed. Clover drifted in with her sewing, Elsie arrived with arithmetic problems, and the boys came clamoring for stories, which Katy invented by the dozen. Before the year was out, her room had become the place where everyone gathered, and Papa remarked one evening that Katy was turning into the heart of the house.

Then Aunt Izzie got sick and died. The grief in the house was real and deep, and Katy, lying awake, remembered with a pang every impatient word she had ever flung at her aunt. With Aunt Izzie gone, the question of who was to manage the household answered itself: there was no one but Katy. So each morning the cook climbed the stairs with her list, and the two of them sat planning the day's meals together. From her pillows Katy kept the household accounts and settled quarrels over borrowed ribbons and broken kites. She saw to it that the younger ones set off for school clean and on time. Clover and Elsie became her lieutenants, carrying out her plans with growing pride, and the house ran more smoothly than it had in Aunt Izzie's busiest days.

Almost four years went by in this fashion. Then, one quiet afternoon, Katy discovered that she could stand on her own for a moment, gripping the edge of the bed. After that she practiced every day, a step or two more each time, steadying herself on chairs and the corner of the dresser. She confided in no one but Papa, for she had a surprise in mind and was determined to keep it. When Cousin Helen arrived for her second visit, it was Katy who walked to the door, slowly but entirely by herself, to meet her. "Katy, you can walk!" cried Cousin Helen, holding out both arms. Katy only laughed, and said that she had been lucky enough to have a very good teacher.
