# Nils and the Wild Geese

Summary: A lazy farm boy is made tiny by an elf and flies across Sweden on a white goose.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): goose, geese, elf, fox

## Level A1

Nils lived on a farm in Sweden. He was not a good child. He did not like school or work. He was often bad to the animals.

One morning, his mother and father went out. Nils stayed at home. Then he saw a little elf. Nils laughed at the elf. The elf did not like this. In a moment, Nils became very small. Now he was as small as a hand. But he could understand the animals now.

Wild geese flew over the farm. "Come with us!" they called. Martin, a big white goose, wanted to go. He went up into the air after them. Nils ran and held on to Martin.

An old goose led the wild geese. Her name was Akka, from Kebnekaise. "We do not want a child," said Akka. "He is my friend," said Martin. So Nils stayed with the geese.

That night, the geese slept by the water. Smirre the fox came close. He took a young goose and ran. Nils ran after him. He took the fox by the tail. Smirre let the goose go. "Thank you, Nils," said Akka. After this, all the geese liked Nils.

Nils went with the geese across Sweden. He saw the whole country from Martin's back. They went far, to Kebnekaise. On the way, Nils helped many animals. He was kind to them now. Smirre followed the geese again and again. But each time, Nils helped his friends.

At last, they came back to Skane. Martin took Nils home to the farm. There, his father found Martin and took him. "I can sell this goose," said the father. Nils ran into the house. "Stop! Martin is my friend!" said Nils. At that moment, Nils became big again. He was a good boy now. His mother and father held him close. The wild geese flew away. But Martin stayed at home with Nils.

## Level A2

Nils Holgersson was a boy of fourteen. He lived with his parents on a small farm in Sweden. Nils did not like school or work. He was also often bad to the animals.

One Sunday, his parents went to church. "Stay at home and read your book," said his father. Nils sat at the table, but he did not read. Soon he saw something in his mother's old box. A little elf sat there and looked at her things. Nils took a net and caught the elf. "Let me go, and I can give you money," said the elf. But Nils only laughed at him. The elf did not like this at all. In a moment, Nils became very small. Now he was no bigger than a man's hand.

Nils ran outside, but the animals laughed at him. "Look at Nils now!" they said. He could understand every animal now. Nobody felt sorry for him, because he was never kind to them.

Then a group of wild geese flew over the house. "Come with us!" they called. Martin, a young white goose, wanted to go with them. He ran across the yard and went up into the air. Nils ran after him and held on to Martin.

The leader of the wild geese was Akka from Kebnekaise. She was very old and knew a lot. That evening, the geese came down by the water. "We do not want people with us," said Akka. "He is my friend, and he stays with me," said Martin.

That night, Smirre the fox came out of the trees. He caught a young goose and ran. Nils got up and ran after him. He took the fox by the tail and held on. Smirre let the goose go and turned to Nils. Nils ran to a tree and went up into it. The fox waited under the tree all night. In the morning, Akka flew low over the fox again and again. Smirre ran after her, and Nils got away. From then on, the geese were happy to have Nils.

For many months, Nils travelled with the geese across Sweden. They flew very far, all the way to Kebnekaise. On the way, he helped many animals in trouble. In time, he became a kind boy. Smirre followed the geese for a long time. But each time, Nils found a way to stop him.

One day, Nils learned some news from the elf. He could be big again. But first, he had to take Martin home safe. Nils was happy, but he also loved his life with the geese.

At last, the geese came back to Skane. Nils and Martin went down to the old farm. There, his father caught Martin and took him into the house. "This fine goose can go to the market," said his father. Nils ran into the house to help his friend. "Do not take Martin!" he called. At that moment, Nils became a big boy again. His parents ran to him and held him close. Then the wild geese flew away. But Martin stayed at home with Nils.

## Level B1

Nils Holgersson was fourteen and lived on a small farm in Skane. Skane is in the far south of Sweden, where the land is flat and green. Nils was a lazy boy who did not like school or work. He ate a lot, slept a lot, and was often cruel to the animals. The cows, the hens and even the cat were afraid of him.

One Sunday morning, his father and mother got ready for church. Before they left, his father put a thick book on the table. "Read this while we are away," he said. Nils sat down with the book, but soon he was almost asleep. When he looked up, he noticed something strange. His mother's old chest was open, and a tiny elf was sitting on its edge. The elf was looking at the old treasures inside the chest.

Nils quickly took a net from the wall and caught the elf in it. "Let me go," said the elf. "I will give you a gold coin." Nils agreed and let him out, but then he wanted more. He tried to catch the elf a second time. At that moment, the whole room seemed to grow around him. The chairs and the table now looked as big as houses. Nils was suddenly no bigger than a man's hand.

He ran out into the yard to look for the elf. The sparrows, the hens and the cat all laughed at him. "Look how small the bad boy is now!" they cried. To his surprise, Nils could understand every word. Nobody was sorry for him, because he was never kind to any of them.

Just then, a long line of wild geese flew low over the farm. "Come with us to the mountains in the north!" they called. Martin, a young white goose on the farm, stretched his wings. "I am going with them," he said. Nils knew how much his mother valued this goose. He ran and threw his arms around Martin's neck. But Martin was already rising, and soon the farm lay far below them.

The leader of the wild geese was Akka from Kebnekaise, a very old and wise goose. That evening, the flock came down to rest on the ice of a big lake. Martin was very tired, and Nils brought him water and food. Akka looked at the tiny boy with cold eyes. "We do not want humans in our flock," she said. "He will stay with me," answered Martin.

In the middle of the night, Smirre the fox crept across the ice. He caught a young goose in his teeth and ran toward the forest. Nils jumped up and ran after him without thinking. He took hold of the fox's tail and held on with all his strength. Smirre let the goose go and turned to catch Nils. But the boy was quicker, and he climbed up a tall tree. The fox sat under the tree and waited all night. At dawn, Akka flew down low again and again, just out of Smirre's reach. The fox ran after her, and Nils was able to escape. After that night, Akka let Nils stay with the flock.

The geese travelled north over the whole country, and Nils travelled with them. He saw dark forests, deep lakes, busy towns and old castles. Akka taught him about the land and its animals, and he listened carefully. He helped many small creatures on the way. Slowly, he began to care about others more than about himself. Smirre followed the flock for a long time, but Nils always stopped him. At last, they reached the high mountains near Kebnekaise.

One day, Akka spoke with the elf about Nils. The elf said Nils would become a normal boy again. But first he had to bring Martin home safe. Nils was glad, but he was also sad, because he loved his life with the geese.

When the geese flew south again, they passed over Skane. Martin and Nils went down to the old farm. Nils's father caught the fine white goose and carried him into the kitchen. "We will sell this goose at the market tomorrow," he told his wife. Nils ran into the house to help his friend. "You cannot take Martin!" he shouted. In that moment, he became a boy of normal size again. His mother and father held him and cried for joy. Then Nils went outside to say goodbye to Akka and the wild geese. They flew once over the farm and then went away to the south. Martin stayed at home with Nils, who was now kind to every animal.

## Level B2

Nils Holgersson was fourteen and lived with his parents on a small farm in Skane, in the far south of Sweden. He was a tall, thin boy with fair hair, and he was not much use to anyone. At school, he was lazy; at home, he avoided every job, and his greatest pleasure was teasing the animals. The cows turned their heads away when he passed, and the hens ran for cover whenever they heard his voice.

One fine Sunday in March, his parents set off for church. Before leaving, his father placed a heavy book of sermons on the table and told Nils to read a chapter. "I will ask you about it when we come back," he warned. Nils sat down unhappily, turned a few pages, and soon began to nod. When he opened his eyes again, he noticed in the mirror that the lid of his mother's old chest was raised. On the edge of the chest sat an elf, no taller than a hand, gazing at the old silver and lace inside.

Without a second thought, Nils grabbed a butterfly net from the wall and swept the little creature into it. "Let me go," begged the elf. "I will pay you a gold coin, as large as the silver buttons on your father's coat." Nils accepted the offer and opened the net, but a moment later he regretted the deal. He reached out to catch the elf once more, hoping to get something better. Suddenly the room seemed to stretch in every direction. The table rose above him like a roof, and the chairs became as tall as trees. Nils had been made no bigger than a man's hand.

He ran out into the yard, desperate to find the elf and ask for forgiveness. Instead, the sparrows, the hens and the old cat gathered round and laughed at him. "Look at the boy now!" they called. "He is not so frightening any more!" To his astonishment, Nils understood every word, because the elf's magic had given him the language of animals. Not one of them offered him help, since he had never shown kindness to any of them.

At that moment, a long line of wild geese came flying low over the farm, calling to the tame geese below. "Come with us to the mountains of the north!" they cried. Martin, a handsome young white gander, could not resist the call. He spread his wings and began to run across the yard. Nils knew how much his mother valued Martin, so he flung his arms round the goose's neck to hold him back. But it was too late, and a moment later the boy and the gander were high in the air above Skane.

The flock was led by Akka from Kebnekaise, a gray old goose who was respected by every bird in the north. That evening they landed on the ice of a wide lake, and Martin, not used to flying, nearly fell from exhaustion. Nils found him water and a few roots to eat, and slowly the gander recovered. Akka, however, looked at the boy with deep suspicion. "Humans have hunted us for hundreds of years," she said. "We do not want one of them in our flock." "Then I will leave with him," answered Martin, and Akka let the matter rest for the night.

Some hours later, Smirre the fox crept silently across the ice. He seized a young goose and raced for the forest with his prize. Nils woke at the sound of beating wings and ran after the fox without stopping to think. He caught hold of Smirre's tail and dug in his heels with all his strength. Surprised, the fox dropped the goose, which flew back to safety. Smirre turned to deal with Nils, but the boy darted to a beech tree and scrambled up into its branches. The fox settled down beneath the tree to wait, sure that the tiny boy would have to come down sooner or later. When the sun rose, the geese came one by one, flying just above the ground and just out of the fox's reach. Smirre chased them across the fields until he was too tired to run, and Nils climbed down and escaped. From that day on, Akka welcomed him into the flock.

For many months, Nils travelled with the geese from one end of Sweden to the other. He saw the dark forests of the middle country, mines deep in the hills, busy cities and quiet islands. Akka taught him the names of the rivers and the habits of the animals, and he became a careful listener. Along the way, he helped many creatures, among them a mother squirrel whose young had been carried off. Smirre followed the flock for many weeks, seeking revenge, but each time Nils managed to spoil his plans. Little by little, the selfish boy from Skane grew brave, patient and kind. At last the geese reached the high mountains around Kebnekaise, where Akka had her home.

During the journey, Akka learned the elf's condition from another bird. Nils would become a normal boy again only if he brought Martin home safe and sound. The news made him both glad and sad, because he had come to love the free life of the flock.

When the geese at last turned south again, their road passed over Skane. Martin wanted to see the old farm, so Nils and the gander went down into the yard. Nils's father caught Martin at once and carried him into the kitchen. "We will take this fine goose to the market," he told his wife. Nils ran into the house, forgetting all fear. "You must not take Martin!" he shouted. As soon as he spoke, he was a full-sized boy again, standing in the middle of the kitchen. His mother and father stared at him, then held him tight and wept for joy. Martin was set free, and the boy went outside to say goodbye to Akka. The wild geese rose into the gray sky and disappeared towards the south. Martin stayed on the farm with Nils, who never again treated an animal badly.

## Level C1

On a small farm in Skane, at the southern tip of Sweden where the fields lie flat beneath an enormous sky, there lived a boy called Nils Holgersson. He was fourteen, long-limbed and fair, and so thoroughly idle that his parents had almost given up hope of him. School bored him, chores offended him, and the only thing that roused him to any real effort was the chance to torment an animal. The cows lowered their heads when his shadow fell across the stall, and the hens scattered at the mere sound of his boots on the cobbles.

One bright Sunday in March, his father and mother put on their best clothes and set off for church, leaving Nils behind with strict instructions. His father opened a thick book of sermons on the table, chose a chapter, and announced that there would be questions about it at dinner. Nils settled himself in the chair with the air of a prisoner, read half a page, and drifted into a doze. When he woke, the mirror on the wall showed him something that made him sit up at once: the lid of his mother's heavy old chest stood open. Perched on its edge, no taller than a hand, sat an elf in old-fashioned clothes, gazing with quiet pleasure at the lace and silver his mother kept inside.

Nils did not stop to wonder. He snatched the butterfly net from its nail by the window and, with one quick sweep, had the little creature trapped and dangling. "Release me," the elf said, with more dignity than fear, "and I will give you a gold coin as large as the silver buttons on your father's Sunday coat." It seemed a fair bargain, so Nils let him climb out; yet no sooner was the elf free than the boy began to feel he had sold himself too cheap. He lunged once more, meaning to catch him again and drive a harder bargain. What happened next he could never afterwards describe, except that the room suddenly seemed to rush away from him in every direction. The table soared overhead like the roof of a barn, the chair legs rose about him like the trunks of pines, and the familiar kitchen became a vast and echoing hall. Nils Holgersson had shrunk until he was no bigger than a man's hand.

He fled into the yard, frantic to find the elf and beg to be made himself again. Instead he found an audience. The sparrows on the fence, the hens by the well and the old cat on the step all crowded round, and to his dismay he could follow every word they said. "See how the great Nils has come down in the world!" crowed the cockerel. The cat, whose tail Nils had pulled more times than either of them could count, merely yawned and told him that he deserved whatever came to him. Not one creature on the farm lifted a feather or a paw to help him, and for the first time Nils knew what it meant to be small and friendless.

It was at this unhappy moment that a long line of wild geese came sweeping low over the farm, honking down to the tame geese in the yard. "Come with us! Come north to the mountains!" they called, half in invitation and half in mockery. Most of the farm geese merely flapped and grumbled, but Martin, a fine young white gander, felt something wake inside him that he had never felt before. Spreading his wings, he began to run across the yard, determined to prove that a farm bird could fly as well as any wild one. Nils knew that his mother set great store by Martin, and without a second thought he threw both arms round the gander's neck to hold him back. It made no difference; the next instant the ground fell away, and the boy found himself clinging to Martin's back high above the patchwork fields of Skane.

The flock was led by Akka from Kebnekaise, a gray, ancient goose whose wisdom was respected from one end of the country to the other. By evening the wild geese had come down on the ice of a broad lake, and Martin, who had never flown so far, sank down almost too weary to lift his head. Nils, forgetting his own troubles, hunted along the shore until he found water and a few soft roots, and carried them back to his exhausted friend. Akka watched all this, but when she turned to the boy her eyes were cold. "Your kind have hunted ours for longer than any goose can remember," she said. "A human, however small, has no place in this flock." Martin answered that where the boy went, he went too, and Akka, saying nothing more, allowed the question to wait until morning.

But the night had other plans. Some hours later Smirre the fox, who had long dreamed of a goose dinner, came gliding across the frozen lake without a sound. In a single bound he seized a young goose and made off toward the dark line of the forest. Nils, roused by the sudden beating of wings, raced after him before he had time to be afraid. He caught the fox's tail in both hands, braced his feet against the frozen ground and held on as if his life depended on it. Astonished at being dragged back by something so tiny, Smirre opened his jaws, and the young goose escaped and flew back to the others. The furious fox spun round to deal with the boy, but Nils was already scrambling up the smooth gray trunk of a beech tree. All night Smirre circled beneath it, certain that hunger or cold would sooner or later bring his little enemy down. At first light, however, the wild geese arrived, one after another, skimming just above the ground and always just beyond the reach of the fox's teeth. Unable to resist, Smirre chased each of them in turn until he was worn out, and in the meantime Nils slipped down from the tree and ran to safety. That morning Akka said no more about sending him away.

For many months afterwards, Nils travelled with the flock from the southern plains to the far north of the country. From Martin's back he looked down on dark forests that rolled on for days, on mines dug deep into the hills, on busy harbor towns and quiet islands. Akka taught him the names of lakes and mountains and the habits of every creature they met, and Nils, who had never listened to a teacher, could not hear enough. He came to the aid of many animals on the journey, from a mother squirrel whose young had been carried off to whole families of birds in danger. Smirre, burning for revenge, trailed the flock for weeks on end, yet every time he set a trap, the small boy on the white gander somehow found a way to spoil it. Little by little, almost without noticing, the lazy and cruel boy of Skane was turning into someone patient, brave and kind. At last the geese reached the great mountains round Kebnekaise, where Akka had her home among the high rocks.

Somewhere on that long road, Akka discovered the elf's condition and passed it on to Nils. He would be restored to his proper size, the elf had declared, only if he brought the white gander home again safe and sound. The news ought to have filled him with simple joy, but it did not, for he had grown to love the open sky and the honest friendship of the flock.

When at last the geese turned south once more, their road carried them over Skane, and Martin begged to look at the old farm one last time. They had barely landed in the yard when Nils's father caught sight of the splendid white gander and carried him triumphantly into the kitchen. "Fine birds like this fetch a good price," he told his wife, "and tomorrow he goes to the market." Nils did not pause to think about what his parents would say when they saw him so small. He rushed into the house and planted himself before his father. "You shall not take Martin!" he cried, with all the voice he had. And in the very moment he spoke, the spell broke, and a full-sized boy stood in the middle of the kitchen floor. His mother let out a cry, his father dropped the gander, and then both of them were holding him, weeping and laughing in the same breath. Martin was set free at once and stayed on the farm, where nobody ever spoke again of taking him to market. Later that day Nils walked down to the shore, where the wild geese waited, and stood for a long while with Akka before they rose into the gray sky. He watched the long line until it vanished towards the south, and from that day on no animal on the Holgersson farm ever had reason to fear him.
