# Persephone and the Pomegranate

Summary: A mother searches the whole world for her missing daughter while nothing on the earth can grow.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): pomegranate, seeds, seasons

## Level A1

Ceres had one child, a girl. Her name was Proserpina. Ceres made food grow for the world. One morning, Ceres went far away for work. "Do not go far," she told Proserpina.

Proserpina played by the sea. She picked many flowers there. Then she saw a big plant with flowers. "These are for my mother," said Proserpina. She pulled it up. Then the ground opened.

Four black horses came out. A man sat in a chariot behind them. His name was Pluto. His house was under the ground. "I want a friend," said Pluto. He took her away in his chariot. "Mother!" called Proserpina, but Ceres was far away.

The sun never came to Pluto's house. Pluto was kind to Proserpina. But she only wanted her mother. She did not eat his food.

Ceres came home, but Proserpina was not there. Ceres looked for her in every place. She walked for many days and nights. At last, she met Phoebus. Phoebus lived high above the world. He saw everything from there. Phoebus said, "Pluto took her away. She is in his house now."

"Nothing can grow without my child," said Ceres. So no food grew in the world. The people had nothing to eat. So the gods sent Quicksilver to Pluto. His job was to bring Proserpina home.

Proserpina did not eat for a long time. She wanted food very much. Pluto's man brought her a pomegranate. Nothing grew now, so it was very small. Proserpina ate six of its seeds. Just then, Quicksilver came in.

Quicksilver said to Pluto, "Proserpina must go home. The people have no food." Pluto said, "She can go. But she ate six of my seeds. So she stays here six months each year."

Quicksilver took Proserpina home. Ceres held her close. Food grew again. Now Proserpina goes to Pluto every year. Then nothing grows. After six months, she comes home again. Then the flowers come back. So now we have the seasons.

## Level A2

Long ago, Ceres made the plants grow all over the world. She had one daughter, Proserpina, and she loved her very much. Ceres always had a lot of work, because the world needed her. One morning, she had to go on a long trip. Ceres told Proserpina, "Play by the sea with your friends. But do not go far from them."

Proserpina picked flowers by the sea all morning. Then she walked on and saw a large plant. Its flowers were the most beautiful in the world. "These are for my mother," she thought. She held the plant with both hands and pulled hard. It came out of the ground, and a deep hole opened.

Four black horses came out of the hole with a golden chariot. A man sat in it, and he spoke to Proserpina. "I am Pluto, and I live under the ground. I am alone there, and I want a friend." Then Pluto took her away in his chariot. "Mother! Mother!" Proserpina called, but Ceres was too far away.

Pluto's palace was very large and full of fine things. But there was no light there at all. Pluto was kind to Proserpina and wanted her to be happy. But she only thought about her mother. She did not eat any of his food at all.

When Ceres came home, she could not find her daughter. She looked for her day and night, all over the world. Nobody knew anything about the girl. At last, Ceres met a quiet woman, Hecate. "I heard a girl call, but I saw nothing," said Hecate. So the two women went together to Phoebus. Phoebus lived high above the world and saw everything. Phoebus told Ceres, "Pluto took her away in his chariot. She is in his palace under the ground."

Ceres felt very bad. "Nothing can grow until my daughter comes back," she said. So nothing grew, and the people had no food. So the gods sent Quicksilver down to Pluto. His job was to bring Proserpina home.

Under the ground, Proserpina did not eat for a long time. One day, one of Pluto's men brought her a pomegranate. It was small and dry, because nothing grew in the world now. Proserpina was very hungry, so she ate six of its seeds. At that moment, Quicksilver came into the room.

Quicksilver told Pluto, "Ceres wants her daughter back. The people in the world have no food." Pluto looked at Proserpina for a long time. Then Pluto said to her, "You can go home. But you ate six seeds from my pomegranate. So you must stay with me for six months every year." Proserpina felt a little sorry for Pluto, because he was so alone.

Quicksilver took her home, and Ceres held her in her arms. At once, the plants grew again. Now, every year, Proserpina lives with Pluto for six months. In that time, Ceres is sad, and nothing grows. When Proserpina comes back, Ceres is happy, and the flowers return. This is how the seasons began.

## Level B1

Long ago, Mother Ceres looked after the plants of the whole earth. She made the corn grow in the fields and the fruit grow on the trees. She had one daughter, Proserpina, and the girl was the joy of her life. Ceres was always busy, and one morning she had to travel to a land far away. Before she left, she took Proserpina to the seashore to play with the sea nymphs. "Stay with your friends," Ceres said, "and do not wander off alone."

The sea nymphs were kind to Proserpina, and they made her a necklace of shiny shells. "I will bring you flowers in return," Proserpina promised them. "I will make each of you a crown of flowers." She ran up the beach alone and began to pick flowers. She picked one after another until her arms were full.

Then she saw a large bush with the most wonderful flowers in the world. "These are the best of all," she thought. She pulled at the bush with both hands. Slowly the roots came up, and the ground opened wide. Out of the deep hole came a golden chariot with four black horses. A man with a crown on his head was sitting in the chariot. It was Pluto, the king of the world under the ground. "Do not be afraid," he said to Proserpina. "I only want a friend in my palace." Then he took her away in his chariot, and the ground closed behind them. "Mother! Mother!" Proserpina called, but Ceres was too far away to hear her.

Pluto's palace was large and rich, with gold and jewels in every room. But no sunlight ever reached it, and no flowers grew there. Pluto was not cruel. He was lonely, and he wanted Proserpina to bring some light into his home. He showed her all his treasures, but she only wanted to go back to her mother. She refused to eat any of the food on his table.

When Ceres came back, she ran to the seashore to look for her daughter. "She went to pick flowers," the nymphs said, "and she did not come back." Ceres lit a torch and began to search. For days and nights she walked over mountains and through forests. Her torch never went out. She asked everyone she met, but nobody could tell her anything.

At last, she came to the cave of Hecate, a quiet woman who lived alone. "I heard a girl's cry," Hecate said, "but I saw nothing." Then Hecate went with Ceres to visit Phoebus, the god of the sun. Phoebus was sitting in the bright sunshine and playing music. From the sky he could see everything on the earth. "Pluto took your daughter away in his chariot," Phoebus told Ceres. "She is living in his palace under the ground."

Ceres was full of sorrow and anger. "Nothing will grow on the earth until my daughter comes back to me," she said. So the corn stopped growing, the leaves fell from the trees, and the fields turned brown. People had no food, and they cried out for help. Finally, the king of the gods sent Quicksilver, his fastest messenger, to bring Proserpina home.

Down in the palace, Proserpina still ate nothing, and she grew very hungry. One day, Pluto's servant brought her a pomegranate on a golden plate. It was the only fruit he could find. It was small and dry, because nothing grew on the earth any more. Proserpina only wanted to smell it, but she was so hungry that she bit into it. She ate six of its seeds. At that very moment, Quicksilver came into the room.

"Ceres will not let anything grow until her daughter returns," Quicksilver told Pluto. "The people of the earth are hungry, and you must let Proserpina go." Pluto was quiet for a long time. Then he turned to Proserpina and said, "You may go. But you ate six seeds of my pomegranate. So you must spend six months of every year here with me." Proserpina felt a little sorry for Pluto, because he was kind to her and very lonely.

Quicksilver took her back to the earth, and Ceres held her daughter tightly in her arms. At once the grass turned green, and flowers opened everywhere. Now Proserpina spends six months of each year in Pluto's palace. While she is away, Ceres is sad, and the earth is cold and bare. When she comes home, the flowers bloom again. That is how the seasons began.

## Level B2

Long ago, when the world was still young, Mother Ceres looked after every growing thing on the earth. It was her work that made the corn rise tall in the fields and the apples turn red on the trees. She had one daughter, Proserpina, a cheerful girl who was the joy of her mother's life. Ceres had so much to do that she was hardly ever at home. One morning she had to travel to a distant land where the crops needed her. Before she set out, she brought Proserpina to the seashore to spend the day with the sea nymphs. "Stay with your friends," Ceres told her, "and do not wander off by yourself."

The sea nymphs loved Proserpina, and they made her a necklace of bright shells from the bottom of the sea. "You have given me something lovely," said Proserpina, "so I will give you something in return. I will gather flowers and make a crown for each of you." The nymphs reminded her of her mother's words, but she laughed and ran up the beach alone. She picked flower after flower until she could hardly carry them all.

Then, a little farther on, she came upon a large bush covered with the most splendid flowers she had ever seen. "These are the finest of all," she thought, and she took hold of the bush with both hands. She pulled and pulled, and at last the roots gave way. At that moment the ground opened, and out of the deep hole rose a golden chariot drawn by four black horses. In the chariot sat a man in rich robes, with a crown on his head. He was Pluto, the king of the great kingdom under the ground. "Do not be afraid," he said to Proserpina in a gentle voice. "My palace is very lonely, and I want a friend to make it cheerful." Then he took her away in his chariot, and the earth closed behind them as if nothing had happened. "Mother! Mother!" cried Proserpina, but Ceres was much too far away to hear her.

Pluto's palace was magnificent, with walls of gold and rooms full of jewels. Yet no sunlight had ever reached it, and not a single flower grew anywhere inside. Pluto was not a cruel king; he was simply lonely, and he hoped Proserpina would brighten his gloomy home. He showed her all his treasures and offered her the finest dishes from his table. Proserpina, however, wanted nothing except to go back to her mother, and she refused to eat even a mouthful.

When Ceres returned and found that her daughter was gone, she hurried to the seashore. "She went to pick flowers," the nymphs told her, "and she never came back." Ceres lit a torch from the fire of a burning mountain and set out to search the world. Day and night she walked over hills and through forests, and her torch never went out. She asked everyone she met about her daughter, but nobody had seen the girl.

At last, she came to the cave of Hecate, a quiet, sad woman who seldom went out into the daylight. "I heard a girl crying for her mother," Hecate said, "but I saw nothing at all." Hecate offered to go with Ceres, and together they went to Phoebus, the god of the sun. They found him sitting in the brightest sunshine, playing sweet music. Because he looked down on the earth all day long, very little was hidden from him. "Pluto took your daughter away in his chariot," Phoebus told Ceres. "She is now in his palace far beneath the ground."

Ceres was filled with sorrow, and her sorrow soon turned to anger. "Not a single seed shall grow on the earth until my daughter is given back to me," she declared. From that day the corn stopped growing, the leaves fell from the trees, and the green fields turned brown and bare. People had nothing to eat, and their cries for help rose up to the sky. At last the king of the gods sent Quicksilver, his swiftest messenger, down to Pluto's kingdom to bring Proserpina home.

Meanwhile, deep in the palace, Proserpina still ate nothing, and she was growing terribly hungry. One day Pluto's servant brought her a pomegranate on a golden plate. It was the only fruit he had been able to find. It was small and dry, because the earth had stopped bearing fruit. Proserpina told herself that she would only smell it, but her hunger was too strong, and she bit into it. She had eaten just six of its seeds when the door opened and Quicksilver walked in.

"Ceres will not let a single seed sprout until her daughter returns," Quicksilver told Pluto. "The people of the earth are starving, so you must let Proserpina go." Pluto said nothing for a long while. Then he turned to Proserpina and said, "You may go home. But you ate six seeds of my pomegranate. For that reason you must spend six months of every year here with me." To her own surprise, Proserpina felt sorry for him, because he had been kind to her and was so very lonely.

Quicksilver led her back to the world above, and Ceres held her as if she would never let her go. At once the grass turned green again, and flowers opened wherever Proserpina stepped. Ever since then, Proserpina has spent six months of each year in Pluto's palace. While she is away, Ceres grieves, and the earth grows cold and bare. When she comes home again, her mother's joy makes the flowers bloom once more. That is how the seasons came to be.

## Level C1

In the days when the world was still young, Mother Ceres had charge of every growing thing upon the earth, from the smallest blade of grass to the tallest tree. It was by her care that the corn rose golden in the fields and the apples blushed red in the orchards, and nobody could remember a time when she had rested. Her one great comfort was her daughter, Proserpina, a merry girl whose laughter seemed to make the very sunshine brighter. Busy as she always was, Ceres had to travel one morning to a distant country where the harvest was in trouble, and she could not take the girl with her. So before setting out, she left Proserpina on the seashore in the company of the sea nymphs, who loved nothing better than a visitor. "Stay close to your friends," Ceres told her, "and on no account wander off by yourself."

The sea nymphs made a great fuss of Proserpina, and they dived to the bottom of the sea to bring up shells, which they strung into a shining necklace for her. "You have given me such a lovely present," said Proserpina, "that I must give you something in return. I will gather the prettiest flowers I can find and weave a crown for each of you." The nymphs reminded her, a little anxiously, of what her mother had said, but she only laughed and ran off up the beach. Before long she had picked so many flowers that her arms could scarcely hold them, and still she wandered on, always certain that finer ones were waiting a little farther along.

Then, just a few steps farther on, she noticed a large bush whose branches were heavy with the most splendid flowers she had ever seen. "These are the finest of all," she said to herself, "and they shall go in the very middle of every crown." She took hold of the bush with both hands and tugged at it with all her strength, until at last its roots began to give way. At that instant the ground split open, and out of the deep hole rose a golden chariot drawn by four black horses. Seated in it was a man in rich, dark robes, with a crown of jewels upon his head; this was Pluto, the king of the vast kingdom that lies beneath the ground. "You have no reason to be afraid," he said, and his voice was gentler than she expected. "My palace is a lonely place, and I have long wished for a cheerful companion to share it with me." Then he took her away in his chariot, and the earth closed over them so neatly that nobody could have said where it had opened. "Mother! Mother!" Proserpina called as the light vanished behind her, but Ceres was much too far away to hear.

Pluto's palace was magnificent beyond anything Proserpina had imagined, with walls of polished gold and halls in which jewels glittered like stars. Yet no ray of sunlight had ever touched those walls, and not a single flower had ever bloomed in those vast, silent halls. Pluto was not a cruel king, only a lonely one, and he had hoped that a girl as bright as Proserpina might chase some of the gloom from his home. He led her through room after room of treasure and had the finest dishes in his kingdom laid before her. He had very little idea of how to please a girl, and every gift he offered only reminded her of what she had lost. Proserpina, however, wanted nothing in the world except her mother, and she made up her mind that she would not taste so much as a crumb of Pluto's food.

When Ceres came home and found the house empty, she hurried straight to the seashore, where the nymphs met her with anxious faces. "She went to pick flowers," they told her, "and she never came back to us." Ceres lit a torch from the fire of a burning mountain and set out to search the whole wide world. Through long days and longer nights she wandered over hills and through forests, and in all that time her torch never once went out. She peered into every valley and called her daughter's name at every crossroads, until her voice was little more than a whisper. She questioned every traveler she met, but no one could tell her anything about a girl with an armful of flowers.

At length her search brought her to the cave of Hecate, a pale and quiet woman who seldom ventured out into the daylight. "I did hear a girl calling for her mother," Hecate said, "but whoever she was, I never saw her." Hecate offered to keep Ceres company, and the two of them went together to Phoebus, the god of the sun. They found him sitting in the brightest sunshine with his lyre, and he went on playing a sweet tune while they spoke to him. Because he looked down upon the earth all day long, very little could be hidden from his eyes. "Pluto took your daughter away in his chariot," Phoebus told Ceres at last. "She is now in his palace, far beneath the ground."

Ceres was overwhelmed with sorrow, and before long her sorrow had hardened into anger. "Not one seed shall sprout, nor one leaf unfold, until my daughter is given back to me," she declared. From that hour the corn stopped growing, the leaves dropped from the trees, and the green fields faded to brown, until the whole earth looked as bare as a winter road. People had nothing to eat, and their cries for help rose higher and higher, until they reached the ears of the king of the gods himself. He sent Quicksilver, the swiftest of his messengers, hurrying down to Pluto's kingdom with orders to bring Proserpina home.

Meanwhile, deep in the palace, Proserpina had still eaten nothing, and her hunger was becoming harder to bear with every hour. One day Pluto's servant came to her carrying a pomegranate on a golden plate. It was the only fruit he had been able to find anywhere, and it was small and shriveled, for the earth had long since stopped bearing fruit. Proserpina told herself that there could be no harm in merely smelling it, but her hunger proved stronger than her resolve, and she bit into it. She had swallowed just six of its seeds when the door flew open and Quicksilver stepped into the room.

"Ceres has sworn that no seed will sprout until her daughter returns," Quicksilver informed Pluto. "The people of the earth are starving, and so you must let Proserpina go." For a long while Pluto said nothing at all, and the great hall seemed even darker than before. Then he turned to Proserpina and said, "You may go home. Yet you have eaten six seeds of my pomegranate. For that, you must spend six months of every year here with me." To her own astonishment, Proserpina felt a pang of pity for him, since he had treated her kindly and was, after all, so very much alone.

Quicksilver led her back to the world above, and Ceres caught her daughter in her arms and held her as though she would never again let her go. At once the grass sprang up green, and flowers opened wherever Proserpina set her foot. Ever since then, Proserpina has spent six months of each year in Pluto's palace beneath the ground. While she is away, Ceres grieves, and the earth lies cold and bare under the gray sky. When her daughter returns, the mother's joy wakes every root and seed, and the flowers bloom once more. And so it was that the seasons came into the world.
