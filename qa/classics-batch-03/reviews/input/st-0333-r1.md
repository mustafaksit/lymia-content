# The Naval Treaty

Summary: A secret treaty disappears from a young clerk's desk, and Sherlock Holmes must find the thief.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): treaty, paper, bell

## Level A1

Percy Phelps had a job in London. He worked in a government office. The head of the office called him. He gave Percy an important treaty. "Write it out again tonight," he said. "Do not show it to anyone."

Percy worked late in his room. He wanted coffee, so he rang a bell. Nobody came. Percy went down to look. An old man sat by the front door. He was asleep in his chair. Then the bell sounded again! Somebody was in Percy's room. Percy ran back up, but he saw nobody. The treaty was not on the table.

Percy went home to Woking. There he became very ill. For many weeks, he stayed in bed. Annie Harrison looked after him every day. Percy loved her. Her brother Joseph Harrison lived there too.

Percy wrote to his old friend Watson. Watson came to Woking with Sherlock Holmes. Holmes looked at the bell, door and windows.

One night, Percy heard a sound. A man was at his window! The man saw Percy and ran away. "Percy, come to London with Watson," said Holmes. "Annie, stay in his room all day. Close the door at night."

That night, Holmes waited in the garden. A man opened the window and came in. It was Joseph Harrison! He pulled up a board in the floor. The treaty was under it. Holmes ran in and stopped him. Joseph ran away into the night. But Holmes had the treaty.

The next morning, Percy visited Holmes in London. Holmes put a plate in front of him. Percy looked under the cover. His treaty was there! "Joseph needed money," said Holmes. "He came to your office that night. Nobody was there, so he rang the bell. He saw the treaty and took it. He took it home to Woking. He hid it in his own room. But then you were ill in that room. Annie was always with you. So Joseph could not get the paper back."

## Level A2

Percy Phelps had a good job in a government office in London. His mother's brother was an important man in the government. One day, this man gave Percy a very important paper. It was a secret treaty between two countries. "Write it all out again tonight," he told Percy. "Nobody else must see it."

Percy stayed in his room after everyone went home. The treaty was long, and the work took many hours. Late in the evening, he wanted some coffee. So he rang the bell for the old man at the door. The old man's wife came up, and Percy asked for coffee. But the coffee did not come. Percy went down to find out why. The old man sat in his chair, fast asleep. Then, above them, the bell rang! Somebody was in Percy's room. Percy ran back up, but nobody was there. The treaty was gone from his table.

The police looked for the paper, but they did not find it. Percy went home to Woking, and there he became very ill. For nine weeks he lay in bed. Annie Harrison looked after him, and Percy planned to marry her. Her brother, Joseph Harrison, gave Percy his own room.

At last Percy wrote to his old school friend Watson. Watson brought Sherlock Holmes to Woking. Holmes looked at the bell, the door and the windows. He asked many questions, and then he went back to London.

A few nights later, Percy heard a small sound at his window. A man stood outside in the dark. Percy got up, and the man ran away across the garden. Holmes heard the story the next day. "Percy, come to London with Watson and me," he said. Then he spoke to Annie alone. "Please stay in Percy's room all day. At night, close the door and take the key with you."

But Holmes did not go to London. That night he waited in the garden near Percy's window. Very late, a man opened the window and went in. It was Joseph Harrison! He pulled up a board in the floor. Under it was the treaty. Holmes followed him in through the window. Joseph tried to run, but Holmes stopped him and took the paper. Then Joseph pulled free and ran away into the night. Holmes had the treaty in his hand.

The next morning, Percy and Watson ate with Holmes in London. Holmes gave Percy a plate with a cover on it. Percy took off the cover. There lay the treaty! Percy shouted with joy. Then Holmes explained everything. "Joseph lost a lot of money, and he needed more. That night he came to your office to visit you. Nobody was in your room, so he rang the bell. Then he saw the treaty on the table and took it. He put it under the floor of his own room at Woking. But the next day you came home very ill. You took that room, and Annie never left you. So Joseph could not get the paper back."

## Level B1

Percy Phelps was a young man with a bright future. He worked in a government office in London, and his uncle was a powerful minister there. One afternoon, his uncle called him into his private office. On the desk lay a long paper, a secret treaty between two great nations. "I need a copy of this, and I need it tonight," his uncle said. "Nobody else must see it, Percy. Lock it in your desk when you are not working."

Percy waited until the other clerks went home. Then he sat down alone and began to copy the treaty. It was slow, careful work, and by ten o'clock he felt very tired. He decided to ask for a cup of coffee. In the corner of his room there was a bell which rang downstairs. At the bottom of the stairs an old man kept watch over the front door. Percy rang, but the old man's wife came up instead. She took his order and went away.

Twenty minutes later, there was still no coffee. Percy walked down the stairs to find out what was happening. The old man was fast asleep in his chair. Next to him, a kettle was boiling on the fire. At that moment, the bell above them rang sharply. The old man woke with a jump. "That's your room, sir!" he said. "But you are standing here. Who is ringing it?"

Percy ran back up the stairs. His room was empty, and the treaty was no longer on the desk. Nobody went past the old man at the front door. So the thief probably used the side door and the back stairs. Outside, the street was dark and wet with rain. The police followed the old man's wife to her home, but they found nothing.

Percy went home to Woking feeling sick with worry. On the train he became seriously ill. For nine weeks he lay in bed with a high fever. Annie Harrison, the young woman he was going to marry, cared for him day and night. Her brother Joseph, who was staying at the house, gave up his own bedroom for Percy. When Percy finally felt strong enough, he wrote to his old school friend, Dr Watson.

Watson came to Woking with Sherlock Holmes. Holmes listened to the whole story without speaking. He looked carefully at the bell, the door and the windows. He also stopped for a moment to admire a rose by the window. "There are many clues here," he said at last. "Perhaps too many." He returned to London to ask more questions.

A few nights later, something strange happened. Percy woke up because he heard a small noise at his window. A man was trying to open it from outside. When Percy jumped out of bed, the man ran off across the garden. Holmes listened to this news with great interest. "Percy, you will come to London with Watson and me today," he said. Then he spoke quietly to Annie. "Please stay in Percy's room all day, Miss Harrison. When you go to bed tonight, lock the door and keep the key."

But Holmes never got on the train. That night he hid in the bushes in the garden and watched the window. At about two in the morning, someone opened it and climbed inside. It was Joseph Harrison. He pulled up a board in the floor and took out a roll of paper. Holmes jumped in through the window. Joseph tried to run, but Holmes stopped him and took the paper. In the end, Joseph pulled free and ran away into the night. But Holmes kept the treaty.

The next morning, Percy and Watson sat down to breakfast with Holmes in London. Holmes placed a covered dish in front of Percy. Percy lifted the cover, and there lay the treaty, safe and complete. He gave a cry of joy and danced around the room. Then Holmes explained everything. Joseph lost a lot of money in bad business deals, and he needed more. That evening he visited the office to travel home with Percy. He found the room empty, so he rang the bell. Then he saw the treaty on the desk and took it. He hid it under the floor of his bedroom, planning to sell it later. But Percy came home ill and took that very room. Annie never left him alone, so Joseph could not reach his hiding place. "Your illness saved the treaty," Holmes said with a smile.

## Level B2

Percy Phelps had done well at school, and his family connections had helped him even more. His uncle was a lord and one of the most powerful ministers in the government. Thanks to him, Percy held a respected post in a London government office. One afternoon in May, his uncle called him into his private room and closed the door. On the desk lay a long document, the original of a secret treaty between two great nations. "Other governments would pay a fortune to read this," his uncle said quietly. "I need an exact copy by tomorrow, and nobody else is to know about it."

Percy waited until the other clerks had gone home, and then he started work. The treaty was full of long, difficult clauses, and every word had to be exact. By ten o'clock he was so tired that he could hardly keep his eyes open. A cup of strong coffee seemed like the answer. His room had a bell that rang down in the hall, where an old porter guarded the main entrance. Percy pulled it, but it was the porter's wife who came up. She promised to bring coffee and went away.

Twenty minutes passed, and the coffee never arrived. Percy finally went down the stairs to see what had gone wrong. In the little room by the entrance, the old porter was sleeping in his chair. A kettle was boiling so hard on the fire that the lid was jumping. Percy reached out to wake the man, but at that moment the bell above them rang loudly. The porter sat up in shock. "That's your bell, sir," he said, staring at Percy. "But if you are down here, who is up there?"

Percy raced back up the stairs, but his room was empty. The treaty had disappeared from his desk, although every other paper was still in its place. The porter insisted that nobody had come in by the main door. The thief, therefore, must have entered through the side door and climbed the back stairs. It had been raining hard all evening, yet there were no wet footprints on the floor. The police followed the porter's wife to her home and searched it, but they found nothing.

Percy's nerves could not take the shock. On the train home to Woking he fell seriously ill. For nine weeks he lay in bed with a high fever. Annie Harrison, the young woman he was engaged to marry, nursed him with endless patience. Her brother, Joseph Harrison, had been staying with the family when the trouble began. He gave up his own comfortable bedroom on the ground floor so that Percy could rest there. As soon as Percy could hold a pen, he wrote to his old school friend, Dr Watson.

Watson brought Sherlock Holmes down to Woking on the next train. Holmes listened to Percy's story with his eyes half closed and asked several sharp questions. He looked closely at the bell, the side door and the windows of the sickroom. For a while he seemed more interested in a rose by the window than in the crime itself. "The trouble with this case is not a lack of clues," he said, "but too many of them." Then he went back to London to visit Percy's uncle and ask further questions.

Several nights later, the case took a sudden turn. At about two in the morning, Percy was woken by a faint scratching at his window. Someone outside was trying to force it open. When Percy jumped out of bed, the figure turned and fled across the dark garden. Holmes listened to this news with a strange gleam in his eye. "You will come back to London with Watson and me this afternoon," he told Percy. Then, while the others were busy, he took Annie aside and spoke to her in a low voice. "Please stay in Percy's room for the whole day, Miss Harrison. When you go to bed, lock the door from the outside and keep the key."

Holmes walked with them to the station, but at the last moment he did not get on the train. That night he hid among the bushes outside the sickroom and waited for many cold hours. At last, a figure crept across the lawn, opened the window and climbed inside. In the moonlight, Holmes saw that it was Joseph Harrison. Joseph knelt down, pulled up a loose board in the floor and took out a roll of paper. Holmes sprang in through the window. Joseph tried to run for the door, but Holmes stopped him and pulled the paper from his hand. A moment later, Joseph twisted free and ran off into the darkness. Holmes let him go, because the treaty was safe in his hands.

The next morning Percy and Watson joined Holmes for breakfast at his rooms in London. Holmes was in an excellent mood, and he asked Percy to uncover the dish in front of him. Percy lifted the cover and found the lost treaty lying on the plate. With a cry of joy, he danced wildly around the room before falling into a chair. Then Holmes explained the whole affair. Joseph had lost a great deal of money in risky business deals and was desperate for more. On the night of the theft, he had called at the office. He hoped that he and Percy could travel home together. Finding the room empty, he rang the bell for the porter. Then he noticed the treaty on the desk. In a moment of greed, he put it in his pocket and slipped out by the side door. He hid it beneath his bedroom floor, intending to sell it as soon as possible. However, Percy came home ill the next day and was given that very room. Since Annie never left the sick man alone, Joseph could not reach his hiding place for weeks. "If you had stayed healthy, the treaty would be gone by now," Holmes said with a smile.

## Level C1

Percy Phelps had left school with every advantage a clever young man could wish for, not least an uncle who sat among the most powerful ministers in the land. Through that uncle's influence he had secured a comfortable post in a London government office, where his quiet diligence soon earned him a measure of trust. It was this trust that brought him, one afternoon in May, into his uncle's private room, where the door was shut firmly behind him. On the desk lay a long document in a careful hand: a secret treaty between two great nations, whose terms many foreign governments would have paid handsomely to learn. "I want a copy made tonight, in your own office," his uncle said, lowering his voice. "Lock the original away whenever you leave the desk, and speak of it to no one."

Percy waited until the last of the clerks had put on their hats and gone, and then he settled down to his task. The clauses were long and dense, and by ten o'clock his eyes ached while his attention wandered. What he needed, he decided, was a cup of strong coffee. A bell in the corner of his room rang down in the hall, where an elderly porter kept watch over the main entrance, and Percy pulled it. To his mild surprise, it was not the porter who answered but the porter's wife, a large, rather rough woman who took his order and lumbered off down the stairs.

Twenty minutes went by, and then a few more, without any sign of the coffee. At last, irritated and half asleep himself, Percy went down the main staircase to discover the cause of the delay. He found the old porter in his little room, slumped in his chair and snoring gently, while a kettle rattled its lid furiously on the fire. Percy was reaching out to shake him awake when the bell on the wall above them began to ring. The porter woke with a start and looked, first at the bell and then at Percy, with growing bewilderment. "But that's your room, sir," he stammered, "and you are standing here beside me."

Percy took the stairs two at a time, yet the room he burst into was perfectly empty. Every paper on the desk lay exactly as he had left it, except for one; the treaty was gone. The porter was certain that no one had passed the main door, so the thief could only have come in by the side entrance and the narrow back stairs. It had been raining heavily all evening, but, curiously, there was not a single wet footprint on the floor. Suspicion fell at once on the porter's wife, who had left the building in a hurry, and the police followed her home and searched every corner of her house. They found nothing, and Percy, who had walked out into the wet street feeling as though his whole career had collapsed, was left alone with his despair.

What happened next he hardly remembered. On the train back to Woking he broke down completely, and for nine weeks he lay in bed in the grip of a fever. Throughout those long weeks he was nursed by Annie Harrison, the gentle young woman to whom he was engaged, who scarcely left his side by day or by night. Her brother, Joseph Harrison, had been staying at the house when the disaster struck. He had generously given up his own bedroom on the ground floor so that the invalid might lie undisturbed. When Percy could at last hold a pen, he wrote to Watson, an old school friend whose famous companion was said to work miracles.

So it was that Watson and Sherlock Holmes stepped off the train at Woking one warm morning. Holmes listened to the whole sorry story with his eyes half closed, interrupting only to ask questions that seemed, to Percy, almost absurdly small. He wanted to know exactly where the bell hung, how the side door was fastened, and which windows of the sickroom looked out onto the lawn. At one point he drifted to the window and lifted a drooping rose with the tenderness of a man admiring a work of art, while his client watched in visible despair. "The difficulty here is not a lack of clues," he remarked at length, "but rather that we have far too many, and most of them point nowhere." That same afternoon he returned to London, where he called on Percy's uncle and pursued certain inquiries he chose not to explain.

A few nights later, the case took a turn that nobody could have foreseen. In the small hours, Percy, who was by then sleeping more lightly, woke to a soft scraping sound and saw that someone was working at the catch of his window. He cried out and sprang from the bed, and the shadowy figure outside turned at once and vanished across the garden. When Holmes heard of this, a curious light came into his eyes, and his manner, until then almost lazy, became brisk and decided. "You are coming up to London with Watson and me this afternoon," he told Percy, in a tone that admitted no argument. Then, drawing Annie aside, he made a request of her that she found puzzling but agreed to without question. "Stay in that room the whole day, Miss Harrison, and do not leave it for a moment. When you go to bed tonight, lock the door from the outside and take the key with you."

Holmes accompanied the party to the station and stood chatting by the carriage door. As the train began to move, however, he stepped back onto the platform and waved them off alone. He spent the evening walking the lanes, and when darkness fell he slipped into the garden and crouched among the bushes beneath the sickroom window. Hour after hour he waited, cold and motionless, while the lights of the house went out one by one. At about two o'clock a figure crossed the lawn, eased the window open, and climbed lightly into the room. In the pale moonlight Holmes saw, without much surprise, the face of Joseph Harrison. The young man knelt, pried up a short length of floorboard, and drew out a roll of paper from the hollow beneath. Holmes was through the window in an instant, and when Joseph made a dash for the door, he blocked his way and took the precious roll from his grasp. For a few tense seconds the two men faced each other in silence, Joseph pale and trembling and Holmes holding the paper firmly behind his back. Then Joseph slipped past him and fled into the night, leaving the treaty behind.

Percy and Watson arrived at Holmes's rooms the next morning to find the table laid for breakfast and their host in unusually high spirits. Holmes urged Percy, who looked pale and hopeless, to help himself from the covered dish that had been placed in front of him. Percy lifted the cover without interest and then froze, for there on the plate lay a roll of pale blue paper. With a cry of joy he seized it, pressed it to his chest, and danced about the room until he collapsed, breathless, into an armchair. Only when he had recovered did Holmes, with evident enjoyment, lay out the whole chain of reasoning.

Joseph, it turned out, had lost a great deal of money on reckless speculation and was desperate to cover his debts. On the night of the theft he had come to the office, meaning to travel home with Percy, and had entered quietly by the side door. Finding the room deserted, he rang the bell to summon someone, and in the same moment his eye fell on the treaty lying open on the desk. A man in his position could hardly have resisted such a temptation; he pocketed the document and was gone before the porter had even reached the stairs. His plan was to hide it under his bedroom floor and sell it to a foreign embassy once the fuss was over. What he could never have foreseen was that Percy would come home ill and be put to bed in that very room, guarded day and night by Annie's devoted care. "Your fever, my dear Phelps, kept the treaty safer than any lock," Holmes concluded, "and all your future brother-in-law could do was wait outside in the dark."
