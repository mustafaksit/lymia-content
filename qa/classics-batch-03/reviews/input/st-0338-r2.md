# The Stolen Bacillus

Summary: A scientist shows a curious visitor a tube of microbes, and the visitor has a dangerous plan.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): tube, cab, chase, microbe

## Level A1

The Bacteriologist lived and worked in London. A young man came to see him. There were many small tubes on the table. He showed the young man one tube. "Look," said the Bacteriologist. "There is a microbe in this tube. It is very bad for people. Put a little in the city water. Then many people die."

The young man looked at the tube. He had a bad plan in his head. He wanted to frighten the whole city.

Then Minnie called from the door. Minnie was the Bacteriologist's wife. The Bacteriologist went to her for a minute. In that minute, the young man left. Now one tube was not on the table! "He took my tube!" said the Bacteriologist.

The Bacteriologist ran out of the house. He did not stop for his coat. The young man was in a cab already. The Bacteriologist got into a second cab. "Follow that cab!" he said to the driver. And so the chase began.

Minnie saw her husband from the door. "He has no coat!" she said. She took his coat and ran out. Then she got into another cab. Now three cabs went across London.

The young man sat in the first cab. He held the tube in his hand. The cab went very fast. The tube fell from his hand and broke. A little was still in it. The young man drank it. "Now the microbe is in me," he said. "I can take it all over London." He got out near Waterloo Bridge. Then he walked away among the people.

The Bacteriologist got out there too. Minnie came soon after. The Bacteriologist began to laugh. "What is it?" asked Minnie. "He took a different tube," said the Bacteriologist. "It was not the bad one. It has a new microbe in it. This microbe makes monkeys blue. So soon the young man is blue too!" Minnie laughed, and she gave him the coat. "Now put it on," she said.

## Level A2

The Bacteriologist lived in London, and he worked at home. One afternoon, a young man came to see him. The young man had a white face and dark hair. He asked many questions about the Bacteriologist's work. There were many small tubes on the table. The Bacteriologist picked up one tube and held it up. "This tube holds a very bad microbe," he said. "Put a little in the city water, and people get ill. Soon thousands of people die."

The young man looked at the tube for a long time. He asked about it again and again. He had a secret plan. He wanted to frighten all of London. And now the tube was right in front of him.

Then Minnie called from the door. Minnie was the Bacteriologist's wife, and she wanted to talk to him. The Bacteriologist left the room for a minute. When he came back, the young man quickly said goodbye. He went out of the house in a great hurry. Then the Bacteriologist looked at his table. One of the tubes was not there! "He took the tube!" cried the Bacteriologist.

The Bacteriologist ran out into the street. He did not stop for his hat or his coat. He still had his house shoes on his feet. Down the street, the young man got into a cab. The Bacteriologist found a second cab and got in. "Follow that cab!" he told the driver. So the chase began.

Minnie watched her husband from the window. "He has no coat!" she said. "And it is so cold today!" She took his coat and ran outside. She found one more cab and followed the other two. Now three cabs raced across London, one behind the other.

In the first cab, the young man held the tube. He told the driver to go faster and faster. Suddenly the cab went over a big stone in the road. The tube fell, and it broke. Only a few drops stayed inside. The young man drank them. "Now the microbe lives in me," he said. "I can carry it to every part of London." He paid the driver and got out near Waterloo Bridge. Then he walked away among the people.

The Bacteriologist's cab stopped near Waterloo Bridge too. He saw the young man, but he did not run after him. A minute later, Minnie arrived in the third cab. She ran to her husband with his coat. To her surprise, he was not worried at all. He began to laugh.

"What makes you laugh?" asked Minnie. "The young man took the wrong tube," said the Bacteriologist. "The bad microbe is still at home. He took my new microbe. It does not hurt people, but it does a strange thing. It turns monkeys blue! So soon our young friend gets a blue face too." Minnie laughed, and she held out the coat. "Now put this on," she said. "It is very cold, and you must not get ill."

## Level B1

The Bacteriologist lived in a quiet house in London, where he had a small laboratory. One afternoon, a young visitor sat with him in the laboratory. The visitor was pale and thin, with untidy dark hair and nervous fingers. He brought a letter from a friend, and he asked endless questions. The Bacteriologist enjoyed describing his research, so he answered them all.

On the table there were many small glass tubes. The Bacteriologist picked one up and held it near the window. "In this tube there is a microbe," he said. "It is one of the most dangerous things in the world. One drop in the city water, and the sickness spreads through every street. Thousands of people die." The visitor gazed at the tube, and his eyes began to glitter. "So much danger in such a small thing," he said softly.

The Bacteriologist noticed the strange expression, but he did not understand it. The visitor had a secret plan, and he dreamed of frightening the whole city. Now the perfect tool was lying on the table in front of him.

At that moment, someone knocked at the door. It was Minnie, the Bacteriologist's wife. "Can I speak to you for a minute?" she asked. The Bacteriologist stepped out of the room and talked with her in the hall. When he came back, the visitor was already standing with his hat in his hand. He said goodbye in a hurry and almost ran out of the house. The Bacteriologist turned to his table, and his face went white. One of the tubes was missing. "He has stolen it!" he cried.

The Bacteriologist rushed out into the chilly street. He had no hat or coat, and he was still wearing his slippers. At the corner, the visitor was climbing into a cab. The Bacteriologist stopped a second cab and jumped in. "Follow that cab, and do not lose it!" he shouted to the driver. And so the chase began.

Minnie saw everything from the window. "He has gone out without his coat, and it is so cold!" she said. She grabbed his hat and coat and ran down the steps. She found a third cab and told the driver to follow the others. Soon three cabs were racing through the streets of London, one after another. Pedestrians on the pavement stopped and giggled at the peculiar sight.

In the first cab, the visitor held the tube tightly. "Faster!" he urged the driver, promising him extra money. Suddenly the cab hit a stone, and the tube fell from his hand. The fragile glass cracked, and most of the liquid trickled away. Only a few drops were still inside the broken tube. The visitor looked at them for a moment, and then he drank them. "Now the microbe lives in me," he said. "I will carry it to every corner of London." He paid the driver, got out near Waterloo Bridge, and walked away into the crowd.

The Bacteriologist's cab arrived a little later. He saw the visitor on the bridge, but he did not run after him. Then Minnie's cab stopped beside him, and she hurried over with his coat. To her astonishment, her husband was not frightened at all; in fact, he was laughing.

"What is so funny?" asked Minnie. "The poor man took the wrong tube," said the Bacteriologist. "The dangerous microbe is still safe on my table. He took my new microbe, the one I was studying this week. It cannot hurt anyone, but it does something very strange. It turns monkeys blue!" The Bacteriologist chuckled again. "Soon our visitor will have a bright blue face, just like my monkeys." Minnie began to laugh too, and she held out the coat. "Put this on at once," she said. "It is very cold, and you will catch a chill."

## Level B2

The Bacteriologist lived in a tall, quiet house in London. Most of his work was done in a small laboratory at the top of it. One gray afternoon he had a visitor, a young man who had arrived with a letter of introduction from a friend. The young man was pale and thin, with long dark hair and restless fingers that never stayed still. He said very little about himself, but he asked a great many questions.

The Bacteriologist was pleased to have such an eager listener. He let his guest look through the microscope at a few pink and purple specks on a glass slide. "Those tiny things are living creatures," he explained. "There are millions of them in a single drop." Then he took a small sealed tube from the rack on his table and held it up to the light. "This one is the most dangerous of all," he said. "It contains the microbe of cholera. If somebody poured it into the water supply of this city, the sickness would run through every street. Thousands of people would fall ill and die."

The visitor's eyes never left the tube. "So much power, locked up in a little glass tube," he said slowly. The Bacteriologist noticed the strange hunger in his face, but he could not explain it. He did not know that his guest belonged to a group of angry men with wild ideas. The young man dreamed of frightening the whole city and making his own name famous. Now, in this quiet room, the perfect tool lay a few inches away.

At that moment there was a knock at the door. It was Minnie, the Bacteriologist's wife, who wanted a word with her husband. He excused himself and stepped into the hall for a minute. When he came back, the visitor was already on his feet, looking at his watch. He said that he was late for another meeting, shook hands quickly, and hurried down the stairs. The Bacteriologist went back to his table, and suddenly his heart seemed to stop. One of the tubes was gone. "He has taken it!" he cried, and he ran for the door.

He rushed out into the street without stopping for his hat or his coat. On his feet he still wore the soft old slippers he used in the house. At the corner of the street, the visitor was just climbing into a cab. The Bacteriologist waved wildly at a second cab, jumped in and shouted, "Follow that man, and don't lose him!" The driver shook the reins, the horse set off at a run, and the chase began.

Minnie had seen the whole thing from the window. "He has gone out without his coat, in this cold wind!" she said in dismay. She caught up his hat, his coat and his boots and ran down the front steps. A third cab was passing, and she called it over. "Follow those two cabs!" she told the driver. So three cabs went racing through the streets of London, one behind another. Other drivers shouted jokes at them, and people on the pavements stopped to stare. Some of them thought it was a race, and they cheered.

In the second cab, the Bacteriologist sat on the edge of his seat, without a hat and with his hair blowing about. At first, he could think of nothing but the stolen tube. Then, little by little, he began to picture the rack on his table as he had left it. The cholera tube had stood at the very end of the row, close to the wall. The empty space, he now remembered, was in the middle, where he kept the newest of his samples. A slow smile spread over his face, but he told the driver to keep going all the same.

In the first cab, the visitor held the tube in his hand and leaned forward. "Faster, faster!" he called, and he promised the driver extra money. Suddenly a wheel struck a large stone, and the cab gave a sudden jump. The tube slipped from his fingers, fell to the floor and cracked. Most of the liquid ran away between the boards. A few drops were still left in the broken glass. The young man looked at them, and then, with a strange smile, he drank them. "Now I carry the microbe myself," he thought. "I will walk among the crowds, and the whole city will learn to fear me." The driver had turned round on his high seat and was staring at him. He was clearly wondering what sort of passenger he had picked up. The young man stopped the cab near Waterloo Bridge, paid the astonished driver, and walked off into the crowd.

The Bacteriologist's cab pulled up a few minutes later. He saw the young man moving through the people on the bridge, but he made no attempt to follow him. Then Minnie's cab arrived, and she climbed out with the coat over her arm. She expected to find her husband in despair. Instead, to her great surprise, he was laughing.

"Whatever is the matter with you?" asked Minnie. "My dear, the man took the wrong tube," said the Bacteriologist. "The cholera is still safe in its rack at home. In his hurry he grabbed the tube next to it, which holds a new microbe I have been studying. It is completely harmless, but it does something rather odd. It turns monkeys blue, in large bright patches." The Bacteriologist began to laugh again. "I only found it last week, and three of my monkeys are already blue. I am afraid our visitor is going to look very blue for a while." Minnie laughed with him, but she held out the coat firmly. "Put this on at once, and these boots too," she said. "You came out in your slippers, in this wind. If you are not careful, you will be the one who falls ill."

## Level C1

The Bacteriologist kept his laboratory at the top of a narrow house in London. It was there, among racks of glass tubes and the faint smell of chemicals, that he received his visitor one gray afternoon. The young man had come with a letter of introduction from an old acquaintance, and he looked, the Bacteriologist thought, rather like a poet who had not slept for a week. His face was pale and sharp, his dark hair fell untidily over his collar, and his thin fingers were never still for more than a moment at a time.

Scientists are not often asked about their work by people who genuinely want to know, and the Bacteriologist, flattered by his guest's attention, talked more freely than usual. He placed a slide under the microscope and invited the young man to look at the tiny pink and violet rods scattered across the glass. "Those little specks are alive," he said, "and there are more of them in a single drop than there are people in this city." Then, warming to his subject, he lifted a small sealed tube from the rack and held it against the window, so that the cloudy liquid inside caught the light. "This, now, is cholera," he announced, with the pride of a man showing off a rare bird. "Pour it into the water supply of London, and within a few days the sickness would be running through every street, from the docks to the palaces. A whole catastrophe, you might say, in something no bigger than my little finger."

The visitor said nothing for a while, but his eyes never once left the tube. "So much power," he murmured at last, "shut up in so small a thing." There was a hunger in his voice that the Bacteriologist noticed without quite understanding. What he could not know was that his guest belonged to a little circle of furious young men with wild theories. They believed that the world could only be changed by first being thoroughly frightened. For months this particular young man had dreamed of some great stroke that would terrify the whole city and fix his own name in everyone's memory. Now, quite unexpectedly, the instrument of that dream was lying on a table a few inches from his hand.

At that moment somebody tapped at the door. It was Minnie, the Bacteriologist's wife, who needed a word with her husband about some household matter that would not wait. He begged his guest's pardon and stepped out onto the landing for a minute or two. When he returned, the young man was already on his feet, buttoning his coat and glancing at his watch with an air of sudden urgency. He was late, he explained, for another appointment; he shook hands rather too quickly and was gone down the stairs before his host could ring for the servant. The Bacteriologist turned back to his work, and his eye fell on the rack. There was a gap in the row of tubes. "Good heavens," he cried, "the fellow has taken it!"

He was down the stairs and out of the front door in a moment, without hat or coat and still wearing the shabby carpet slippers he never wore outside the house. At the end of the street the young man was scrambling into a cab. The Bacteriologist hailed a second cab, threw himself inside and shouted to the driver to follow the first one and not to lose sight of it for anything in the world. With a shake of the reins and a clatter of hooves, the chase began.

Minnie, watching from the drawing-room window, had seen her husband dash out into the bitter wind in nothing but his indoor clothes. To her mind there could be only one explanation: the strain of his work had finally been too much for him. She snatched up his hat, his overcoat and his boots, hurried down the steps, and stopped a third cab that happened to be passing. "Follow those two cabs, and please be quick about it," she told the driver. Soon three cabs went rattling through the streets of London, one close behind another, like beads on a string. Other drivers called out cheerful advice as they passed, and errand boys whistled. More than one gentleman on the pavement took off his hat in mock salute, believing that he was watching some new kind of race.

In the second cab the Bacteriologist sat bolt upright, his hair blown about by the wind, and at first he could think of nothing but the empty place in the rack. Gradually, however, as the houses flew past, a clearer picture of his table came back to him. The cholera tube, he remembered, had stood at the far end of the row, against the wall, exactly where he always kept it. The gap had been in the middle, where only yesterday he had put the newest and most curious of all his samples. A slow smile spread over his face; nevertheless he leaned out and told the driver to keep going, for he had no intention of losing so interesting a specimen.

The young man in the first cab knew nothing of this. Clutching the tube, he urged his driver on with promises of extra money, until the wheel struck a loose stone and the whole vehicle gave a sudden jolt. The tube flew out of his fingers, struck the floor and cracked, and most of its contents trickled away between the boards. For a long moment he stared at the few drops that still clung to the broken glass. Then, with the strange calm of a man who has decided upon a dramatic gesture, he lifted the fragment to his lips and drank what remained. "Very well," he thought, "I shall carry it myself, and wherever I walk, the city will learn to fear me." He stopped the cab near Waterloo Bridge, pressed a coin into the hand of the bewildered driver, and walked off into the crowd with his head held high.

The Bacteriologist's cab drew up a few minutes later, and he caught sight of the young man striding among the people on the bridge. He made no attempt to follow him. Minnie's cab arrived almost at once, and she climbed down with the overcoat over her arm, fully prepared to find her husband in a state of collapse. Instead, to her considerable astonishment, she found him leaning against the railings and laughing until his eyes watered.

"Whatever is the matter with you?" she demanded. "My dear," said the Bacteriologist, wiping his eyes, "the poor fellow has stolen the wrong tube. The cholera is sitting quietly in its rack at home. What he snatched, in his hurry, was the little novelty I found last week, the one I have been studying ever since. It is perfectly harmless, but it has one peculiar habit: it turns monkeys blue, in large bright patches, and three of mine already look like a sky in summer." The Bacteriologist began to laugh again. "Our friend set out to terrify London, and instead, I am afraid, he is going to spend the next few weeks looking like a rather surprised blue monkey." Minnie laughed with him, but she held out the overcoat with great firmness. "Put this on at once, and these boots as well," she said. "You have come halfway across London in your slippers, in this wind, and if anyone in this family is going to fall ill today, it will certainly be you."
