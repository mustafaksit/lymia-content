# Hans Brinker and the Silver Skates

Summary: A poor Dutch boy and his sister dream of silver skates and hope to save their father.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): skates, canal, race, doctor

## Level A1

Hans and Gretel lived in Holland. Their family did not have much money. Their small house was next to a canal. In the cold months, the canal was ice. Hans made skates for Gretel and himself. But their skates were not very good.

Their father was Raff Brinker. Ten years ago, he fell at work. There was a big storm that night. He hit his head very hard. So he did not know his family now. He sat in the house all day. He could not work or talk. Their mother worked hard for them.

The family lost some money too. Raff Brinker put it in a safe place. Only he knew the place. Now he could not remember it.

Then Hans heard some big news. "There is a race soon!" said Hans. "The best girl and boy win silver skates." "I want to be in it!" said Gretel. A kind girl, Hilda, wanted to help. Hans made a chain for Hilda. She gave him money for it. Hans bought new skates for Gretel.

There was a great doctor, Dr. Boekman. One morning, he came to their town. Hans ran to him. "Please help my father!" said Hans. At first, the doctor did not listen. But Hans told him the whole story. "I can come," said the doctor.

The doctor came to the little house. He did his work there. And the father got well! Raff Brinker opened his eyes. "Is the storm over?" he asked. He knew his family again! Their mother held him.

Some days later, Raff Brinker remembered the money. "It is under the old tree," he said. Hans ran out to the old tree. He found the money in the ground! Now the family had money again.

Then the day of the race came. Many people stood by the canal. Gretel went very fast on her new skates. No other girl was as fast. Gretel won the silver skates! Hans was very happy for his sister.

## Level A2

Hans Brinker and his sister Gretel lived in Holland. Their small house stood next to a canal. The family was very poor, and they often had little food. In the cold months, the canal turned to hard ice. Then the children of the town came out on skates. Hans made skates for Gretel and himself from pieces of wood. There was another problem too. The family had some money in a safe place. Only Raff Brinker knew the place. Now he could not remember it, so the money was lost.

A kind girl, Hilda, wanted to help them. Hans made a wooden chain, and Hilda gave him money for it. With the money, Hans bought new skates for Gretel. Later he bought new skates for himself too.

Everyone in Holland knew the name of Dr. Boekman. He was a great doctor from Amsterdam. One morning, Dr. Boekman came to their town for a short visit. Hans ran to him. "Please, sir, help my father," said Hans. At first, Dr. Boekman did not want to listen. But Hans told him the whole story, and the doctor's face changed. "I can come and see your father," said Dr. Boekman.

The doctor came to the little house. "I can try to help him," said Dr. Boekman. "But it is not easy." Their mother was afraid, but she said yes. The doctor did his work, and the father slowly got well. Raff Brinker opened his eyes and looked around. "Is the storm over?" he asked. For him, it was still the night of his fall. Then he saw tall Hans and big Gretel. His mind was clear again, and he knew his family.

A few days later, Raff Brinker remembered the money. "I put it in the ground under the old tree," he said. Hans ran out and dug in the hard, cold ground. Soon he found the money. The family was not poor now.

Then the day of the race came. Many people stood along the canal. Gretel went faster than all the other girls. She won the silver skates! Hans was in the boys' race, and he was very fast too. But before the last race, the strap on Peter's skate broke. Peter was a kind boy and a good friend to Hans. Hans gave Peter the strap from his own skate, and Peter won. Hans did not win, but he was happy for his friend. That night, the family sat together by the fire.

## Level B1

Hans Brinker was fifteen, and his sister Gretel was twelve. They lived with their parents in a small, old house beside a canal in Holland. The family was very poor, and there was often very little bread on the table. In winter the canals froze, and it seemed that the whole country was on the ice. Rich children flew past on shining steel skates. Hans and Gretel only had skates which Hans made himself from pieces of wood. The wooden skates made a loud noise, and some of the other children laughed at them.

Ten years earlier, their father, Raff Brinker, was working on the dike during a terrible storm. The sea was rising, and the men were trying to make the great wall stronger. In the dark, Raff Brinker fell and hit his head. He lived, but he was never the same again. He did not know his wife or his children, and he could not work. Day after day he sat by the fire, staring at nothing. Their mother worked from morning until night to keep the family alive.

There was also the mystery of the money. Before the accident, the Brinker family had savings of a thousand guilders. After it, the money was gone, and nobody could find it. Only Raff Brinker knew where it was, and he could not tell them.

Early in the winter, Hans came home with exciting news. "There will be a great race on the canal on the twentieth of December," said Hans. "The fastest girl and the fastest boy will each win a pair of silver skates." Gretel wanted to race more than anything, but she looked sadly at her wooden skates. A kind, rich girl, Hilda, noticed the two poor children on the ice. She wanted to help, so she paid Hans well for a wooden chain which he carved. With this money, Hans bought real steel skates for Gretel. Later he bought a pair for himself too.

But Hans was thinking about something much more important than skates. Everyone in Holland knew the name of Dr. Boekman, the great doctor from Amsterdam. People said he was cold and unfriendly, and that he rarely smiled. One morning Hans heard that the famous doctor was visiting their town. He ran through the streets until he found him. "Please, sir," said Hans, "ten years ago my father fell, and he is still not well." Dr. Boekman looked down at the thin boy in his old clothes and frowned. But Hans spoke so honestly about his father that the doctor's face became softer. "Very well," said Dr. Boekman at last. "I will come and see him."

Dr. Boekman examined Raff Brinker carefully in the little house. "I can try to help him," he said, "but it is hard, and it may fail." Their mother was afraid, but she agreed. The doctor did his work, and then the family waited quietly. For many hours Raff Brinker slept. When he opened his eyes, he looked at his wife in surprise. "Is the storm over?" he asked. "Is the water still rising?" He believed that it was still the night of his fall. Slowly his wife told him about the ten lost years. He looked at tall Hans and at Gretel, and he could hardly believe it. But his mind was clear again, and he knew his family at last.

A few days later, Raff Brinker suddenly remembered something. "The money!" he cried. "I buried it under the old willow tree, just before the storm." Hans ran out and dug in the frozen ground under the tree. Soon he found an old pot, and the thousand guilders were all inside it. After ten hard years, the family was no longer poor.

On the twentieth of December, crowds gathered along the canal. Flags were flying, and people were laughing and calling to their friends. The girls raced first. Gretel was small, but she was light and very quick. She flew past the other girls and crossed the line first. The silver skates were hers!

Hans was in the boys' race, and he was one of the fastest skaters there. Just before the last race, the strap on Peter's skate broke. Peter was a kind boy who was always friendly to Hans. Without a word, Hans gave him the strap from his own skate. Peter won the race and the silver skates, but Hans did not mind at all. That evening, the whole family sat together by the fire, happier than ever before.

## Level B2

Hans Brinker was fifteen years old, and his sister Gretel was twelve. They lived with their parents in a small, crooked house beside a frozen canal in Holland. The family was so poor that there was often nothing but a little bread on the table. Yet when winter came and the canals turned to ice, even poor children could feel rich. All over the country, people tied on their skates and glided from town to town. Wealthy boys and girls flew past on bright steel blades, laughing and calling to one another. Hans and Gretel had only the clumsy wooden skates that Hans had carved himself. They creaked and scraped on the ice, and more than one child pointed at them and giggled.

The real sorrow of the family, however, had nothing to do with skates. Ten years earlier, their father, Raff Brinker, had been working on the dike during a terrible storm. The sea was rising, and every man in the district was needed to keep the great wall strong. In the darkness, Raff Brinker slipped, fell and struck his head. He survived, but the man who came home was not the man who had left. He no longer recognized his wife or his children, and he could not work or hold a conversation. Day after day he sat by the fire, staring at nothing, while his family grew poorer and poorer. Their mother spun wool and took in any work she could find, but it was never enough.

There was a second mystery as well. Before the accident, the Brinker family had carefully saved a thousand guilders, a large sum for a working family. After the accident, the money could not be found anywhere. Some neighbors whispered that Raff Brinker must have spent it, but his wife refused to believe them. Only her husband knew the truth, and his mind was closed to everyone.

Early in December, Hans came home breathless with news. "There's going to be a great race on the canal on the twentieth," said Hans. "The fastest girl and the fastest boy will each win a pair of silver skates." Gretel's eyes shone, and then she looked down at her wooden skates and sighed. Not everyone in the town was unkind, though. A generous girl named Hilda had noticed the Brinker children and decided to help them. She paid Hans well for a wooden chain that he carved for her. With that money, Hans bought Gretel a pair of real steel skates, and later he bought a pair for himself.

Still, Hans could not stop thinking about his father. Everyone in Holland had heard of Dr. Boekman, the famous doctor from Amsterdam. He was said to be brilliant, but also cold, impatient and very hard to approach. One morning Hans learned that the great man was paying a short visit to their town. He ran through the streets until he found him, and then he stood in front of him, cap in hand. "Please, sir," said Hans, "my father fell ten years ago, and he has never been well since." Dr. Boekman looked at the thin boy in his patched clothes and frowned impatiently. But Hans spoke with such honesty and love that the doctor's face slowly softened. "Very well," said Dr. Boekman at last. "I will come and see your father."

The doctor kept his word. In the little house, he examined Raff Brinker for a long time without speaking. "There is a chance that I can help him," he told their mother. "But I must warn you that the treatment is difficult, and it may not succeed." Their mother was frightened, yet she knew that her husband could not go on living in darkness. She gave her permission, and Dr. Boekman began his work. Afterward the house was very quiet while Raff Brinker slept for many hours. When he finally opened his eyes, he gazed at his wife in confusion. "Is the storm over?" he asked. "Is the water still rising on the dike?" To him, it was still the night of his fall. Gently, his wife explained that ten years had passed. He stared at Hans, who was now taller than he was, and at Gretel, who had been a tiny child. Then tears filled his eyes, because his mind was clear again, and he knew them all.

A few days later, as he sat thinking by the fire, Raff Brinker suddenly sat up straight. "The money!" he cried. "I buried it under the old willow tree, just before I went out to the dike." Hans grabbed a spade and ran outside. The ground was frozen hard, but he dug and dug until the spade struck something solid. It was an old pot, and inside it lay the thousand guilders, every single coin. After ten hard years, the family was no longer poor.

On the twentieth of December, crowds gathered along the canal. Flags fluttered in the wind, and music drifted across the ice. The girls raced first. Gretel was smaller than most of the others, but she was light and wonderfully quick. At first, she stayed behind, but near the end she flew past the leaders and crossed the line first. The silver skates were hers, and the crowd cheered for the little girl in the plain dress.

Hans had entered the boys' race, and he was one of the fastest skaters on the canal. Just before the final race, the strap on Peter's skate snapped. Peter was a kind boy who had always treated Hans as a friend. Without hesitating, Hans took the strap off his own skate and handed it to him. Peter won the race and the second pair of silver skates, and he never forgot what Hans had done. Hans himself did not win, but he did not mind. That evening the whole family sat by the fire, and for the first time in ten years their father was truly himself.

## Level C1

Hans Brinker was fifteen, broad in the shoulders and older than his years, and his sister Gretel was a slight, quick girl of twelve. They lived with their parents in a small, sagging cottage beside a canal in Holland, in a house so poor that a loaf of dark bread was often the whole of supper. Yet when winter came and the canals hardened into long gray roads of ice, even the poorest children could feel, for a few hours, as free as anyone. All over the country people strapped on their skates and glided from village to village, carrying baskets to market or simply racing the wind. Wealthy boys and girls swept past on polished steel blades, their scarves streaming behind them. Hans and Gretel had only the clumsy wooden skates that Hans had carved by hand, and they creaked so loudly that more than one well-dressed child turned to laugh.

The true sorrow of the house, however, had nothing to do with skates. Ten years earlier, on a night of terrible storm, their father, Raff Brinker, had been working on the dike with every other able man in the district. The sea was pressing against the great wall, and the men labored in the dark to keep it from giving way. Somewhere in that wild night Raff Brinker slipped, fell and struck his head. He survived, but the man carried home was not the man who had gone out; the cheerful, hard-working husband had become a silent stranger by the fire. He no longer knew his wife or his children, and for ten years he sat staring at the flames, while his wife spun wool and took in whatever work she could find.

As if this were not enough, there was the matter of the money. Before the accident, the Brinker family had put aside a thousand guilders, a small fortune for a working family and the fruit of many careful years. After the accident it had simply vanished. Some neighbors murmured that Raff Brinker must have spent it, but his wife would not hear a word against him, though she could offer no other explanation. The only person who knew the truth was the one person who could no longer tell it.

Early in December, Hans burst into the cottage with news that set Gretel's heart racing. "There's going to be a great race on the canal on the twentieth," said Hans. "The fastest girl and the fastest boy will each be given a pair of silver skates." For a moment Gretel's face shone; then her eyes fell to the wooden skates by the door, and the light went out of it. Not everyone in the town, however, was as unkind as the children who laughed. Hilda, a rich girl with a generous heart, had noticed the Brinker children on the ice and quietly made up her mind to help them. She paid Hans handsomely for a wooden chain that he carved for her, insisting that the work was worth every coin. With that money Hans bought Gretel a pair of real steel skates, and later, almost guiltily, a pair for himself.

Yet even the thought of silver skates could not keep his mind from his father for long. There was not a family in Holland that had not heard of Dr. Boekman, the celebrated doctor from Amsterdam. He was said to be the cleverest man of medicine in the land, and also the coldest; he charged great sums, smiled seldom and had little patience for strangers. So when Hans heard one morning that this formidable man was paying a brief visit to their town, he did not stop to think about how foolish it might seem. He ran through the narrow streets until he found the doctor, and then he stood before him, cap twisted in his hands. "Please, sir," said Hans, "my father fell ten years ago, and he has never been himself since." Dr. Boekman looked down at the thin boy in his patched jacket, and his heavy brows drew together. But Hans spoke so plainly and with such love that, little by little, the stern face before him began to soften. "Very well," said Dr. Boekman at last, in a voice gruffer than he perhaps intended. "I will come and see your father."

He was as good as his word. In the little cottage he examined Raff Brinker for a long while, saying nothing at all, while the family hardly dared to breathe. "There is a chance that I can help him," he told their mother finally. "But I will not deceive you: the treatment is difficult, and it may fail." Their mother was deeply frightened, yet she knew that her husband could not go on living in that twilight forever, so she gave her consent. Dr. Boekman did his work, and afterward the cottage fell into a silence so deep that the ticking of the clock seemed loud. For many hours Raff Brinker slept. When at last he opened his eyes, they rested on his wife with a puzzled, anxious look. "Is the storm over?" he asked. "Is the water still rising on the dike?" For him, no time had passed at all; it was still the night of his fall. Gently his wife told him that ten years had gone by, and he turned in wonder to the tall young man who was his son and to his little girl, now grown. Then his eyes filled with tears, for his mind was clear again, and he knew them, every one.

A few days later, sitting quietly by the fire, Raff Brinker suddenly straightened as if someone had called his name. "The money!" he cried. "I buried it under the old willow tree, just before I went out to the dike that night." Hans seized a spade and rushed outside, Gretel at his heels. The ground was frozen as hard as stone, but he dug on and on, until at last the blade rang against something solid. It was an old earthen pot, and inside it, safe after all those years, lay the thousand guilders, down to the last coin. In a single afternoon, ten years of poverty came to an end.

On the twentieth of December the banks of the canal were lined with people, and flags snapped in the cold wind above the ice. The girls raced first. Gretel was smaller than most of her rivals, and at first she hung back, so that some in the crowd forgot her altogether. Near the end, however, she seemed to find wings; she slipped past one skater and then another, and crossed the line ahead of them all. The silver skates were hers, and the crowd roared for the little girl in the plain, much-mended dress.

Hans had entered the boys' race, and those who watched him knew that he had a real chance of winning it. Just before the final race, however, the strap on Peter's skate snapped. Peter was a kind boy who had always treated Hans as an equal, never as a poor boy to be pitied. Without a moment's hesitation Hans unbuckled the strap from his own skate and pressed it into his friend's hand. Peter won the race and the second pair of silver skates, and he never forgot to whom he owed them. Hans went home without a prize, yet he could not remember a happier day. That evening the family gathered around the fire, and for the first time in ten years the man in the chair beside it was truly their father.
