# The Frog Princess

Summary: A young prince must marry a frog, but his strange new wife hides a big secret.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): frog, arrow, skin, needle

## Level A1

The Tsar had three sons. The youngest was Ivan. The Tsar said, "Shoot an arrow each. You must marry the girl near your arrow."

Two arrows fell near big houses. The two other sons found girls there. But Ivan's arrow fell in a swamp. A frog sat there with the arrow. "You must marry me," said the frog. Ivan did not want a frog. But the Tsar said, "Marry her, Ivan." So Ivan married the frog.

The Tsar said, "Your wives must make bread." Ivan went home and told the frog. "I can help you," said the frog. At night, she took off her frog skin. Now she was a beautiful girl. Her name was Vasilisa the Wise. Some women helped her make the bread. Next morning, Ivan took it to the Tsar. The Tsar loved it.

Then the Tsar had a big party. Ivan went to the party first. Soon a young woman came in. It was Vasilisa, Ivan's frog! She took Ivan's hand and danced. Then Ivan ran home. He found the frog skin and burned it. "Now she can stay a girl," thought Ivan.

Soon Vasilisa came home. "Ivan, why did you do it?" she said. "I needed only three more days. Now I must go to Koschei. Come and find me there." Then Vasilisa was gone.

Ivan went to look for her. On the way he had no food. He saw a bear. Ivan wanted to eat it. The bear said, "Please don't eat me. Later I can help you." So Ivan did not eat the bear. He met a duck and a hare. He did not eat them. They became his friends too.

At last, he came to a little house. Kind old Baba Yaga lived there. She gave Ivan food. "Koschei's life is in a needle," she said. "The needle is in an egg. It is high up in a tree. Break the needle, and Koschei is gone."

Ivan found the tree. The bear came and pulled the tree down. His other friends helped him too. At last Ivan had the egg. At Koschei's house, he broke the needle. At once, Koschei was gone. Ivan took Vasilisa home. They lived together for many good years.

## Level A2

Long ago, a Tsar lived with his three sons. The youngest son's name was Tsarevich Ivan. The Tsar said, "Each of you must shoot an arrow. The girl near your arrow must be your wife."

Two arrows fell near the houses of important men. So the two older sons found wives there. But Ivan's arrow flew far away into a swamp. After a long time, he found it with a green frog. "First you must marry me," said the frog. Ivan did not want a frog, but he took her home. "She has your arrow, so marry her," said the Tsar. So Ivan married the frog.

Soon the Tsar said, "Your wives must make bread by morning." Ivan came home and said nothing. The frog said, "Don't worry, Ivan. Go to sleep." When Ivan was in bed, the frog took off her skin. Now she was a beautiful young woman, Vasilisa the Wise. She called out, and some women came to help her. By morning, they made fine white bread. The Tsar liked it best. Next the Tsar wanted a carpet from each wife. Again Vasilisa made the most beautiful one.

Then the Tsar had a big party. "Go first, Ivan," said the frog. So Ivan went to the party alone. Then a beautiful woman came in. It was Vasilisa the Wise. She took Ivan's hand and sat next to him. Later she danced, and everyone loved it. During the dance, Ivan ran home. He found the frog skin and threw it into the fire. "Now she can never be a frog again," thought Ivan.

When Vasilisa came home, she could not find her skin. "Ivan, why did you do this?" said Vasilisa. "I needed only three more days. Now I must go to Koschei, far away. Look for me there." Then she was gone.

So Ivan left home to find her. He walked for a long time, and he had no food. He saw a bear and wanted to catch it. The bear said, "Don't catch me, Ivan. Some day I can help you." So Ivan did not catch the bear. He was kind to a duck and a hare too. They became his friends. Near the sea, he put a big fish back into the water.

At last Ivan came to a little house. There he met a kind old woman, Baba Yaga. She gave Ivan food and listened to his story. "Koschei's life is at the end of a needle," she said. "The needle is in an egg. The egg is in a duck. The duck is in a hare. The hare is in a box on a high tree. Break the needle, and Koschei is gone."

Ivan found the tree, but the box was too high. Then the bear came and pulled the tree out of the ground. The box fell and broke, and a hare ran out. Ivan's friend the hare caught it. A duck flew out of it, but Ivan's duck caught it. An egg fell from the duck into the deep sea. Then the big fish brought the egg to Ivan.

Ivan went to Koschei's house and broke the egg. He took out the needle and broke it. At once Koschei was gone. Ivan found Vasilisa and took her home. They lived together for many happy years.

## Level B1

Long ago, there was a Tsar who had three sons. The youngest was Tsarevich Ivan. The Tsar told them, "Each of you will shoot an arrow. Wherever your arrow lands, you will find your bride."

The eldest son's arrow landed in a great lord's yard, and he married the lord's daughter. The middle son's arrow fell near a rich merchant's house, and he married the merchant's daughter. Ivan's arrow flew far beyond the town into a swamp. There he found a frog holding his arrow. "First you must take me as your wife," said the frog. Ivan sadly told his father about the frog. "The frog found your arrow, so she will be your wife," said the Tsar. So Ivan married the frog.

"Ask your wives to make me soft white bread by tomorrow," the Tsar told his sons. Ivan came home with his head down, but the frog told him not to worry. As soon as Ivan was asleep, the frog threw off her skin. She was now Vasilisa the Wise, a young woman of great beauty. She stepped outside and called into the night, and her helpers came. By morning there was a loaf of bread that was fit for a feast. The Tsar praised it, but he sent the other two loaves to the kitchen. Next, he asked each wife to make him a carpet in a single night. Again Vasilisa and her helpers worked until dawn, and her carpet shone with gold.

Then the Tsar announced a great feast. The frog told Ivan, "Go ahead without me. When you hear knocking and thunder, tell the guests it is only your frog." At the feast, the whole palace suddenly shook with knocking and thunder. Ivan told the frightened guests, "Please sit down. It is only my frog, arriving in her little carriage." A golden carriage stopped at the door, and Vasilisa the Wise stepped out. She took Ivan's hand and sat beside him at the table. During the meal, she poured the rest of her cup into her left sleeve. Then she put the scraps from her plate into her right sleeve. The other two wives saw this and did the same thing. When the dancing began, Vasilisa waved her left sleeve, and a lake appeared. She waved her right sleeve, and white swans began to swim on the water. The other wives waved their sleeves too, but they only made a mess.

While everyone was watching the dancing, Ivan slipped away and ran home. He found the frog skin and threw it into the stove, where it burned. "Now she will never be a frog again," he thought. When Vasilisa came home, she could not find her skin. "Oh, Ivan, why did you do this?" Vasilisa cried. "I only needed three more days, and then I could stay with you forever. Now I must leave you. Look for me in the far kingdom of Koschei, the old wizard who lives forever." Then she was gone.

Ivan set out to find his wife. One day he met an old man and told him the whole story. The old man said, "Koschei was angry because Vasilisa was wiser than he was. So he turned her into a frog for three years. Take this ball of thread and follow it wherever it rolls."

Ivan was hungry, so he wanted to catch a bear. The bear said, "Don't catch me, Ivan. One day I will help you." So Ivan left the bear alone. He spared a duck and a hare in the same way. Near the sea, he threw a pike back into the water.

The ball rolled to a little hut that stood on chicken legs. Inside lived Baba Yaga, a very old woman who was kind to Ivan. She gave him food and listened to his story. "Koschei's life is at the end of a needle," said Baba Yaga. "The needle is inside an egg, and the egg is in a duck. The duck is in a hare, and the hare is in a stone box. The box is at the top of a tall oak tree. Break the needle, and Koschei will vanish."

Ivan found the oak, but the box was too high. Then the bear came and pulled the whole tree out of the ground. The box fell and broke open, and a hare jumped out and ran away. But Ivan's friend the hare chased it and caught it. A duck flew out of the hare, but Ivan's friend the duck caught it. An egg dropped from the duck and fell into the deep sea. Then the pike swam up to Ivan with the egg in its mouth.

Ivan carried the egg to Koschei's house. He broke the egg, took out the needle, and snapped it in two. At that moment, Koschei vanished. Ivan found Vasilisa the Wise and took her home. They lived together happily for the rest of their lives.

## Level B2

In a certain kingdom, long ago, there lived a Tsar who had three fine sons. The youngest of them was Tsarevich Ivan. When the young men were old enough to marry, the Tsar sent them out into an open field. "Each of you will shoot one arrow," he told them, "and wherever it falls, there you will find your bride."

The eldest son's arrow landed in the courtyard of a powerful lord, and the lord's daughter picked it up. The middle son's arrow came down in the yard of a wealthy merchant, where his daughter was waiting. Tsarevich Ivan's arrow, however, soared far beyond the town and vanished into a lonely swamp. He waded through reeds and mud for hours before he finally found it. A small green frog was sitting on a mound of earth, holding the arrow in her mouth. "Frog, please give me back my arrow," Ivan begged. "Only if you take me as your wife," the frog answered calmly. Ivan told his father everything, hoping that the Tsar would let him choose again. "The frog found your arrow, so you must marry her," the Tsar replied firmly. And so three weddings were held on the same day: two with lovely young women, and one with a frog.

"Tell your wives to bake me a loaf of soft white bread by tomorrow morning," the Tsar ordered. "What troubles you, Ivan?" asked the frog when he came home with his head hanging low. When he explained, she told him to stop worrying and go to sleep, since the morning was wiser than the evening. As soon as he was asleep, the frog threw off her skin. She became Vasilisa the Wise, a young woman of remarkable beauty. She went out onto the steps and called into the night, and her helpers hurried to her side. By dawn a beautiful loaf was waiting on the table. The Tsar praised it warmly, while he declared that the other two loaves were fit only for the kitchen.

Next, the Tsar demanded that each wife should weave him a carpet in a single night. Once again the frog shed her skin, and once again her helpers worked until the sun came up. The carpet shone with gold and silver threads. "This one will be laid out only on great feast days," he announced. The other two carpets, he said, were good enough to cover the horses.

Finally, the Tsar announced a grand feast, and he invited all three sons together with their wives. The frog told Ivan, "Go ahead without me. When you hear knocking and thunder, tell the guests it is only your little frog arriving in her carriage." Suddenly the whole palace shook with knocking and thunder, and the frightened guests leapt from their seats. Ivan said, "Please do not be alarmed. It is only my frog, arriving in her carriage." A golden carriage pulled up at the steps, and out stepped Vasilisa the Wise, so beautiful that the room fell silent. She took Ivan by the hand and sat down beside him. During the meal she poured what remained in her cup into her left sleeve. Then she slipped the scraps from her plate into her right sleeve. The brothers' wives noticed this and copied her at once. When the dancing began, Vasilisa waved her left sleeve, and a clear lake appeared in the hall. She waved her right sleeve, and white swans glided across the water. The other two wives tried the same trick, but they merely splashed the guests, and scraps of food flew across the hall.

While everyone was watching the dancing, Ivan slipped out and raced home. He found the frog skin and threw it into the stove, where it burned away to ashes. He believed that, without her skin, his wife would never have to be a frog again. When Vasilisa returned and discovered what he had done, her face grew pale. "Oh, Ivan, what have you done?" she cried. "If you had waited only three more days, I would have been yours forever. Now I must leave you. Look for me beyond many lands, in the kingdom of Koschei, the old wizard who lives forever." Then she was gone.

Ivan set out at once and wandered for a long time. One day he met an old man and told him the whole sad story. "Why did you burn the skin, Ivan?" the old man asked gently. "Vasilisa was born wiser than Koschei, and that made him furious. So he turned her into a frog for three long years. Take this ball of thread, and follow it wherever it rolls."

Growing hungry on the road, Ivan was about to catch a bear for his supper. The bear said, "Please spare me, Tsarevich. One day I will be useful to you." Ivan felt sorry for the creature and spared it. In the same way, he spared a drake and a hare, and both of them promised to help him. On the seashore he found a pike stranded on the sand. "Have pity on me and throw me back into the sea," the pike pleaded, and Ivan did so.

At last the ball rolled up to a little hut that stood on chicken legs and kept turning around. "Little hut, stand with your back to the forest and your front to me," Ivan said, and the hut obeyed. Inside lived Baba Yaga, a very old woman who turned out to be kind. She gave him food, let him rest, and then listened to his story. "Koschei's life is hidden at the end of a needle," she said, "and the needle is inside an egg. The egg is inside a duck, the duck is inside a hare, and the hare is locked in a stone chest. The chest sits at the top of a tall oak tree. If you break the needle, Koschei will vanish."

Ivan found the oak, but the chest was far too high for him to reach. Just then the bear appeared and pulled the whole tree out of the ground, roots and all. The chest crashed down and broke open, and a hare sprang out and dashed away. At once Ivan's hare raced after it and caught it. A duck burst out of the hare and flew high into the sky, but the drake chased it and caught it. An egg slipped from the duck and dropped into the deep sea. Ivan sat down on the shore, sure that all was lost. Then the pike came swimming to the shore with the egg in its mouth.

Ivan carried the egg to Koschei's house, broke it open, and took out the needle. The moment he snapped the needle, Koschei vanished, and nothing of him was ever seen again. Ivan found Vasilisa the Wise waiting there, and together they went home. They lived happily together for many years.

## Level C1

In a certain kingdom, in a certain land, there once lived a Tsar with three sons, the youngest of whom was called Tsarevich Ivan. When the young men reached the age for marriage, the Tsar summoned them to his chamber and laid out a plan before them. "Take your arrows out into the open field," he said, "and let each of you shoot one as far as he can. Wherever your arrow comes to earth, there you will find the woman who is meant to be your bride."

The eldest son's arrow dropped into the courtyard of a powerful lord, whose daughter came out and picked it up with a smile. The middle son's arrow landed in the yard of a wealthy merchant, and the merchant's daughter was just as quick to claim it. Ivan's arrow, by contrast, sailed high over the rooftops, beyond the last houses of the town, and disappeared into a lonely swamp. For hours, he waded through reeds and sucking mud, until at last he caught sight of it resting on a little mound of earth. Next to it sat a small green frog, holding the arrow in her mouth as if she had been waiting for him all along. "Frog, frog, give me back my arrow," Ivan pleaded. "Take me as your wife," the frog replied, "and the arrow is yours." Hoping that his father might release him from such a match, Ivan carried the frog home and told the Tsar everything. The Tsar, however, was unmoved: the frog had found the arrow, and so the frog was the bride fate had chosen. Three weddings were held on the same day, two with blushing young women and one with a frog.

It was not long before the Tsar wished to learn which of his daughters-in-law was the most accomplished. "Let your wives bake me a loaf of soft white bread by tomorrow morning," the Tsar told his sons. Ivan trudged home with his head bowed, and the frog, hopping out to meet him, asked what could possibly be the matter. When he had explained, she told him not to grieve but to go to sleep, since, as the saying goes, the morning is wiser than the evening. No sooner had his breathing slowed than she shrugged off her frog skin and stood up as Vasilisa the Wise, a young woman of rare beauty. She stepped out onto the porch, clapped her hands, and called into the darkness for her helpers, who came hurrying to her side. By dawn a loaf stood on the table, so light and so fine that it seemed fit for a holiday. The Tsar praised it at length, while the other two loaves, heavy and badly baked, were quietly sent off to the kitchen.

Not content with one trial, the Tsar next demanded a carpet, to be woven by each wife in a single night. Once again the frog cast off her skin, and once again her helpers labored until the sun came up. What lay on the table in the morning was a carpet threaded with gold and silver, patterned so richly that the Tsar could scarcely look away. "This one," he declared, "shall be spread out only on the greatest of feast days." As for the other two carpets, he remarked that they might do, at a pinch, to keep the rain off the horses.

At last the Tsar proclaimed a grand feast and commanded his three sons to come and to bring their wives. "Go on ahead, Ivan," said the frog, "and when you hear knocking and thunder, do not be afraid. Simply tell the guests that it is your little frog arriving in her little carriage." So Ivan went alone, and his brothers made merry at his expense, asking where he had left his bride. All at once the palace shook with knocking and thunder, and the guests sprang to their feet in alarm. Ivan said quite calmly, "Do not be frightened. It is only my frog, arriving in her carriage." A gilded carriage drawn by six horses swept up to the steps, and out of it stepped Vasilisa the Wise, so radiant that the hall fell silent. She took Ivan by the hand and led him to the table, and this time it was his brothers who were lost for words.

During the meal Vasilisa poured what remained in her cup into her left sleeve and tucked the scraps from her plate into her right. The wives of the two elder brothers, watching closely, promptly did the same. When the music began and Vasilisa rose to dance, she waved her left sleeve, and a clear lake spread across the floor of the hall. With a wave of her right sleeve, she sent white swans gliding over the water, and the guests gasped in wonder. The two sisters-in-law tried the same trick, but all they managed was to splash the guests and send scraps of food skittering across the floor.

While every eye in the hall was on the dancing, Ivan slipped away and ran home as fast as he could. He found the frog skin where she had left it and flung it into the stove, watching until it had burned to ashes. Without her skin, he reasoned, his wife could never again be forced to hop about as a frog. When Vasilisa came home and saw what he had done, the color drained from her face. "Oh, Ivan," she said, "what have you done? Had you waited just three more days, I would have been yours forever. Now I must leave you; seek me beyond many lands, in the kingdom of Koschei, the old wizard who lives forever." With that, she was gone.

Ivan set out the very next morning and walked for a long, long time. On the road he met an old man, to whom he poured out the whole sorry tale. The old man sighed and said, "Ah, Ivan, why did you burn that skin? It was not yours to burn. Vasilisa was born wiser than Koschei, and he could not bear it, so in his anger he turned her into a frog for three years." Then he handed Ivan a ball of thread and told him to follow it wherever it chose to roll.

The ball rolled on and on, and Ivan followed it, growing hungrier with every mile. Spotting a bear in a clearing, he thought of catching it for his supper, but the bear spoke to him. "Spare me, Tsarevich," said the bear, "and one day I shall be of use to you." Ivan took pity on the creature and spared it. The same thing happened with a drake and with a hare, and each of them promised to repay his kindness. On the seashore he found a pike stranded on the sand. "Have pity, Tsarevich, and throw me back into the sea," it begged, and Ivan gently did so.

In the end the ball rolled up to a little hut that stood on chicken legs and kept turning slowly round and round. "Little hut, little hut, turn your back to the forest and your front to me," said Ivan, and the hut creaked obediently around. Inside sat Baba Yaga, a very old woman who, as it turned out, had a surprisingly kind heart. She fed him, let him rest beside her stove, and only then asked him what had brought him so far. When she had heard his story, she nodded slowly, for she knew exactly where Koschei kept his life. "It lies at the point of a needle," she said, "and the needle is in an egg, and the egg is in a duck. The duck is in a hare, the hare is in a stone chest, and the chest sits at the top of a tall oak that Koschei guards with great care. Break the needle, and Koschei will vanish."

Ivan found the oak, but the chest was far beyond his reach. At that very moment the bear came crashing out of the trees and tore the oak from the ground, roots and all. The chest fell, burst open on the ground, and out leapt a hare, which bounded away at once, only to be caught by Ivan's own hare. From the hare a duck broke free and soared high into the air, but the drake was after it in an instant and brought it down. In the struggle an egg slipped from the duck and plunged into the deep sea. Ivan sat down on the shore, quite certain that all was lost, until he saw the pike swimming toward him with the egg held gently in its mouth.

He carried the egg to Koschei's house, cracked it open, and drew out the needle. The instant he snapped its point, Koschei vanished, as completely as if he had never been. Ivan found Vasilisa the Wise inside, took her hand, and brought her home, where the two of them lived together in peace and great happiness for the rest of their days.
