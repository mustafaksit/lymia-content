# Rebecca of Sunnybrook Farm

Summary: A lively, dreamy girl from a poor farm moves in with two strict aunts to go to school.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): aunts, soap, school

## Level A2

Rebecca Randall grew up at Sunnybrook, a small farm far from town. She lived there with her mother and six brothers and sisters. Her father was dead, and the family had very little money. Rebecca was ten years old. She had dark eyes and a head full of dreams.

Her mother had two older sisters, Miranda and Jane. The aunts lived in an old brick house in Riverboro. One day they wrote to Rebecca's mother. They wanted one of her girls to live with them. Then the girl could go to a good school. At first, they asked for Rebecca's older sister. But the family needed that sister at home, so Rebecca went instead.

Rebecca made the trip to Riverboro in a coach. At first, she sat inside alone, but she did not like it. So she asked the driver for a seat up front. All along the road she asked him questions and told him stories. The driver laughed a lot. When they reached Riverboro, he was sorry to see her go.

Aunt Miranda was a hard woman with many rules. "You talk too much, and you forget everything," Aunt Miranda said. Aunt Jane was much kinder, and she often helped Rebecca. Rebecca missed her home, but she did well at school. She found a best friend there, a girl called Emma Jane.

Near Riverboro lived a very poor family with many children. A soap company offered a big lamp. Children could win it by selling a lot of soap. Rebecca wanted the lamp for the poor family. So she and Emma Jane went from house to house with soap.

At one big house, a young man sat outside. His name was Adam Ladd. Rebecca told him all about the poor family and the lamp. He listened with a smile. Then he bought three hundred pieces of soap! Now the poor family could have their lamp. In an old story, a boy called Aladdin has a magic lamp. Adam Ladd was like magic too, so Rebecca called him Mr Aladdin.

Rebecca loved books, and she wrote well. Her teachers saw this and helped her. Later she went to a bigger school in another town. She worked very hard there and became one of the best students. On her last day there, Adam Ladd came to hear her.

Then Rebecca went home to Sunnybrook. Her mother had a bad fall and needed her. Soon a letter came from Aunt Jane. Aunt Miranda was dead. She died quietly in her sleep one night. Rebecca went back to Riverboro at once.

There she heard big news. Aunt Miranda left the brick house to Rebecca. Aunt Jane could stay in the house with her. Now Rebecca had a home of her own. She could help her mother and family too. She stood at the door and remembered her first day there. "Thank you, Aunt Miranda," Rebecca said.

## Level B1

Rebecca Randall grew up at Sunnybrook, a small farm far from any town. She was the second of seven children, and her father was dead. Her mother worked very hard, but the farm never made much money. Rebecca was ten years old, with dark eyes and a busy mind. She loved words, and she was full of stories and plans.

Her mother had two older sisters, Miranda and Jane. The two aunts lived together in an old brick house in Riverboro. One day a letter came from them. The aunts offered to take one of the girls and pay for her schooling. They really wanted Rebecca's older sister, who was quiet and careful. But that sister was too useful at home, so the family sent Rebecca instead.

Rebecca travelled to Riverboro in an old horse coach. For a while she sat inside alone with her small bag. Then she asked the driver whether she could sit up front with him. All along the road she talked and asked questions. The driver was a kind old man, and he laughed all the way. At the brick house, he was almost sorry to leave her.

Life in the brick house was not easy. Aunt Miranda liked order and silence, and Rebecca brought neither. "That child will never learn to behave," Aunt Miranda often said. She did not like Rebecca's clothes, her manners or her constant talk. Aunt Jane was softer, and she quietly helped the girl as much as she could. Rebecca sometimes cried for her mother and the farm. But at school she was quick and bright. There she also met Emma Jane, who became her closest friend.

Not far away lived a very poor family with many children. Their little house was dark, and they had almost nothing. A soap company was offering a large lamp to children who sold enough soap. Rebecca wanted to bring that lamp and its light into their home. So one afternoon she and Emma Jane went from door to door with soap.

At one large house, they found a young man sitting outside. He was Adam Ladd, a rich young man who was visiting the area. Rebecca told him the whole story of the poor family and the lamp. He listened carefully, and he seemed to enjoy her lively way of speaking. Then, to the girls' surprise, he bought three hundred bars of soap. That was enough to win the lamp for the poor family. Rebecca remembered the old story of Aladdin and his magic lamp. Like Aladdin, Adam Ladd made a wish come true, so she called him Mr Aladdin.

The years went by, and Rebecca kept growing and learning. She wrote stories and verses, and her teachers loved her work. Later she went to a larger school in a nearby town. She studied hard there and became one of the best students. On her last day at that school, Adam Ladd came to listen to her. He was proud of the bright girl from the soap trip.

Soon after that, Rebecca went home to Sunnybrook. Her mother had a bad fall, so Rebecca ran the house for her. While Rebecca was there, a letter came from Aunt Jane. It said that Aunt Miranda died quietly one night. Rebecca went back to Riverboro at once. She felt sorry because she was not with her aunt at the end.

In the brick house, she got a big surprise. Aunt Miranda left the brick house to her. Aunt Jane was going to stay there too, and the two of them could share it. Now Rebecca had a real home. She could also help her mother and the younger children. She stood by the front door and thought about her hard old aunt. "Thank you, Aunt Miranda," Rebecca said, and she meant every word.

## Level B2

Rebecca Randall was born at Sunnybrook, a small farm at the end of a quiet country road. She was the second of seven children, and since her father's death the family had lived on very little. Her mother worked from morning to night, yet the farm barely fed them all. At ten, Rebecca was thin and lively, with dark eyes that seemed to notice everything. She collected words the way other children collected stones, and she was always making up stories.

Her mother had two older sisters, Miranda and Jane, who lived in an old brick house in Riverboro. Neither of them had ever married, and they were careful with every penny. One spring they wrote to offer a home and an education to one of their sister's daughters. They had hoped for the eldest girl, who was calm and dependable. But she could not be spared from the farm, so it was Rebecca who packed her small bag.

The journey to Riverboro was made in an old coach pulled by two horses. At first Rebecca sat inside on her own, feeling very small and rather lonely. Before long she asked the driver whether she might sit up beside him instead. He agreed, and for the rest of the trip she chattered about books, flowers, her family and her plans. The driver, a gentle old man, had not been so well entertained in years. When he finally set her down at the brick house, he was genuinely sorry to see her go. Rebecca stood on the step with her bag, took a deep breath and knocked on the door.

From the first evening, Aunt Miranda found Rebecca difficult. The house was kept spotless and silent, and the girl seemed to fill it with noise and questions. "She is all her father's side, full of foolish dreams," Aunt Miranda complained to her sister. Rebecca was scolded for dirty shoes, for forgetting her chores and for talking too much. Aunt Jane, who was softer by nature, did her best to protect her. She taught Rebecca to sew and often reminded Miranda that the child meant no harm. On some nights Rebecca cried quietly for her mother and the farm. Yet she never lost her spirit for long.

At the village school she quickly rose to the top of her class. Her teachers admired the way she wrote, and other children gathered round to hear her stories. She also found a loyal friend in Emma Jane, a cheerful girl who lived nearby. The two became almost inseparable, sharing secrets on the way to and from school. Emma Jane admired Rebecca's ideas, and Rebecca valued Emma Jane's steady good sense.

Not far from the brick house lived a family who were poorer than anyone Rebecca had ever known. There were many children, and their small house was dark and nearly bare. At that time, a soap company was offering a large lamp to any child who sold enough of its soap. Rebecca was sure that such a lamp would change that gloomy little home. So one afternoon she and Emma Jane set out with their soap to knock on doors.

At one fine house they came upon a young man resting outside. His name was Adam Ladd, and he was a wealthy visitor from the city. Rebecca explained the whole plan to him with such feeling that he could not help smiling. "How many bars do they need to sell?" Adam Ladd asked. "Three hundred, sir, and so far we have only sold a few," Rebecca admitted. He asked Rebecca about her school and her favorite books, and he listened closely to every answer. Then, to the girls' astonishment, he ordered three hundred bars of soap, enough to win the lamp at once. Rebecca thought of the old tale of Aladdin, whose magic lamp could make any wish come true. From that day on, she always called her new friend Mr Aladdin. Adam Ladd, for his part, never forgot the bright, honest girl who had sold him all that soap.

The years passed, and Rebecca grew into a thoughtful young woman. Aunt Miranda's sharp words came less often, although she still rarely praised anyone. Rebecca, in turn, learned to keep her shoes clean and her temper calm, at least most of the time. After the village school she was sent to a larger school in a nearby town. She studied hard, wrote for the school paper and became one of its best students. On the day her studies ended, she read one of her own pieces in front of the whole school. Adam Ladd was in the audience, and he was deeply proud of the girl he had once met on a soap trip. Afterwards he shook her hand and told her that she had a real gift for words.

Rebecca had hoped to start teaching soon after that. But news came from Sunnybrook that her mother had suffered a bad fall. Without hesitating, Rebecca went home to look after her and to keep the household running. While she was there, a letter arrived from Aunt Jane. Aunt Miranda had died quietly in her sleep one night. Rebecca returned to Riverboro at once, sorry that she had not been there to say goodbye.

A few days later, Rebecca learned something she had never expected. Aunt Miranda, who had criticized her so often, had left the brick house to her. Aunt Jane would go on living there, and the two of them would share it as a family. For the first time, Rebecca had a home of her own and a way to help the people she loved. Her mother could rest at last, and the younger children could have the chances she had been given. Standing at the front door, Rebecca remembered the frightened little girl who had first arrived there. "Thank you, Aunt Miranda," she whispered, and she understood her aunt a little better than before.

## Level C1

Sunnybrook was the kind of farm that looked charming from the road and broke the hearts of the people who tried to make a living from it. Rebecca Randall, the second of seven children, had grown up there among stony fields, a cheerful little brook and a mother who never seemed to stop working. Her father, a gentle dreamer with more charm than sense, had died some years earlier, leaving behind a heap of debts and a houseful of hungry children. At ten, Rebecca had inherited his dark eyes and his love of words, and very little else in the way of worldly goods.

In the village of Riverboro, some miles away, her mother's two older sisters kept house together in an old brick building that had belonged to their family for generations. Miranda and Jane had never married, and they ran their lives with the same careful economy they applied to candles and firewood. When they wrote to offer one of their nieces a home and a proper education, it was not entirely out of tenderness; they also felt it was their duty. The niece they had in mind was the eldest, a sensible girl who could be trusted not to break the china. But the eldest was far too useful at Sunnybrook to be given up, and so it was Rebecca, with her untidy hair and her head full of poetry, who was sent instead.

She made the journey in an old coach drawn by two horses, clutching a small bag that held nearly everything she owned. For the first stretch she sat inside in solitary state, which pleased her for about a mile and bored her for the rest. Eventually she put her head out of the window and asked the driver, with great politeness, whether there might be room for her up beside him. There was, and from that moment the road to Riverboro was transformed. Rebecca asked about every farm they passed, invented histories for the cows and recited verses she had learned. She described her brothers and sisters in such detail that the driver felt he had met them all. He was a kindly old man who had driven that route for years, and he could not remember a passenger who had amused him half as much. When at last he lifted her down in front of the brick house, he found himself oddly reluctant to drive away.

Inside, the welcome was a good deal cooler. Aunt Miranda took one look at her niece's dusty shoes and windblown hair and decided that the girl had too much of her father in her. "All Randall and no sense," she remarked to Jane that evening, in a voice that was not quite low enough. The brick house was a place of polished floors, closed shutters and fixed routines, and Rebecca seemed to disturb every one of them simply by existing. She forgot to wipe her feet, left doors open, sang while she worked and asked questions that Aunt Miranda considered both unnecessary and impertinent. Aunt Jane, quieter and more tender-hearted, did what she could to soften her sister's sharpness, teaching Rebecca to sew neatly and reminding her sister that the child meant well. There were nights when Rebecca lay awake missing her mother and the noisy, crowded farmhouse with a longing she could hardly bear. Yet sorrow never held her for long; by morning she was usually composing a new poem or planning some fresh adventure.

School, at least, was a place where her particular gifts were valued rather than corrected. She learned with astonishing speed, and her compositions were so lively that her teachers sometimes read them aloud to the class. It was there, too, that she found her dearest friend, Emma Jane, a good-natured girl whose admiration for Rebecca knew no limits. Emma Jane had none of Rebecca's imagination, but she had a loyal heart, and the two were seldom seen apart.

Not far from the brick house lived a family whose poverty was plain to everyone in Riverboro. There were a great many children and very little of anything else, and their small house had a cheerless, half-empty look even on the brightest day. At about that time a soap company was promising a splendid lamp to any young seller who could dispose of a large enough quantity of its soap. Rebecca, who could never see a sad thing without wanting to put it right, became convinced that such a lamp would bring a little glory into that bare front room. She enlisted Emma Jane at once, and the two of them set off one afternoon with their bundles of soap and an enormous amount of hope.

Their luck turned at a handsome house where a young gentleman was sitting outside, apparently with nothing in particular to do. He was Adam Ladd, a wealthy man from the city who was staying in the neighborhood for a short while. Rebecca, unaware of his fortune and uninterested in it, launched into the full story of the poor family, the lamp and the number of bars still to be sold. She spoke with such sincerity and such comical earnestness that he found himself smiling in spite of himself. "And how many does the lamp require?" Adam Ladd asked gravely. "Three hundred, sir, which seems a great many when you are carrying them from door to door," Rebecca replied. To the girls' amazement, he bought all three hundred on the spot, and the lamp was won before the sun went down. Rebecca, who had read the tale of Aladdin and the lamp that granted every wish, decided that no other name would suit him. Ever afterwards he was Mr Aladdin to her, and he, for his part, never forgot the earnest little saleswoman who had marched up to him that afternoon.

The years that followed changed Rebecca without diminishing her. She grew taller and quieter, learned to keep her shoes clean and her temper in check, and discovered that even Aunt Miranda could be silent in a way that was almost approving. In time, she was sent on to a larger school in a nearby town, where she threw herself into her studies with all her old energy. She wrote for the school paper, won the respect of her teachers and ended her course as one of the most promising students of her year. On the day her studies were completed, she read one of her own compositions aloud before the assembled school and their guests. Among those listening was Adam Ladd, who had followed her progress over the years and who now saw, with considerable pride, how far the girl from the soap trip had come.

Rebecca had imagined that she would begin teaching soon afterwards, sending part of her wages home each month. Instead, word arrived from Sunnybrook that her mother had taken a bad fall and could no longer manage the house. Rebecca went home without a moment's hesitation, and for some weeks she cooked, cleaned and cared for the younger children as if she had never left. It was during this time that a letter came from Aunt Jane with news she had not expected so soon. Aunt Miranda had died peacefully in her sleep one night, with no long illness and no warning. Rebecca returned to Riverboro immediately, grieved that she had not been there at the end. She was surprised, too, by how deeply she felt the loss of a woman who had rarely shown her any warmth.

What she discovered there surprised her still more. Aunt Miranda, who had found fault with her for years, had quietly left the brick house to her, on the understanding that Jane would always have a home there. It was a gift that said, more clearly than any words the old woman had ever spoken, what she had truly thought of her niece. For the first time in her life, Rebecca had a home of her own and the means to help the family that had always struggled. Her mother could rest at last, and the younger children could look forward to the opportunities that Rebecca herself had been given. Standing at the front door, she remembered the small, travel-stained girl who had once knocked there with her bag in her hand and her heart in her mouth. "Thank you, Aunt Miranda," she said softly, and for once the silent old house seemed to answer her with a gentle kind of peace.
