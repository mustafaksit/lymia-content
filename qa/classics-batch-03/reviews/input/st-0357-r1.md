# Gulliver and the Flying Island

Summary: A traveler from England finds an island in the air where everyone thinks only of numbers.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): island, magnet, academy

## Level A1

Gulliver was a doctor from England. He loved to see new lands. So he went on a ship again. But some bad men took the ship. They put Gulliver in a little boat. Five days later, he found an island.

Then the light went away. Gulliver looked up. An island was in the air over him! People on it sent a seat down. Then they brought Gulliver up. This island was Laputa.

The people of Laputa looked very different. Their heads went to one side. One eye looked in, and one looked up. They thought about numbers and music all day. So they often did not hear people. Important men had helpers with bags.

Gulliver met the king of Laputa. At first, the king did not see him. His mind was on a number problem. A helper put a bag on his mouth. Now the king talked to Gulliver. Gulliver learned their language. The island had a big magnet. It moved the island up and down. Under the island was a country, Balnibarbi.

Gulliver did not like Laputa very much. Nobody there wanted to talk with him. So he asked to go down. The king said yes.

Gulliver went to Lagado, a city in Balnibarbi. The houses there were old and bad. Very little food grew there. "Why is this?" Gulliver asked a man. "Some men went to Laputa," the man said. "They came back with new ideas. They started the Academy of Lagado. But the new ideas do not work."

Gulliver went to the academy. There he met an old man with cucumbers. "The sun's light is in them," he said. "I want to get it out. Then it can help people on cold days. I need eight more years, and money." Gulliver gave him a little money. Another man made houses from the top down!

After that, Gulliver went to Japan. From Japan, a ship took him to England. At last, he was home with his family.

## Level A2

Gulliver was a doctor from England, and he loved to travel. After two long trips, he went to sea again. This time, some bad men took the ship. They put Gulliver in a small boat with a little food. For five days, he moved from island to island. At last, he stopped on a small island of rocks.

The next day, the light suddenly went away. Gulliver looked up and saw something very large in the air. It was an island, and it moved over the sea! Many people looked down at him from its side. After some time, they let down a seat on a long line. Gulliver sat on it, and they pulled him up. The name of this island was Laputa.

The people of Laputa did not look like other people. Their heads always went to one side. One eye turned in, and the other eye looked up. They thought about numbers and music all the time. Because of this, they often forgot to listen or speak. So every important person had a helper with a small bag. The helper touched the person's mouth with the bag. Then the person remembered to speak.

Gulliver met the king of Laputa. The king had a hard number problem in front of him. For an hour, he did not see Gulliver at all. Then a helper touched the king's mouth with the bag. Now the king looked up and spoke to Gulliver. The king sent teachers, and Gulliver learned the language. A man made new clothes for Gulliver. He used numbers, not his eyes and hands. The clothes did not fit at all.

In the center of the island was a very large magnet. It moved the island up and down, and from place to place. Under the island was a big country, Balnibarbi.

Gulliver stayed on Laputa for two months. The people only wanted to talk about numbers and music. Nobody cared about Gulliver. So he asked to go down, and the king said yes.

Gulliver went to Lagado, the biggest city in Balnibarbi. The houses there were old and bad. Many people worked on the land, but very little food grew. "Why is this?" Gulliver asked a kind man. "Forty years ago, some men went up to Laputa," the man said. "They came back with new ideas about land and houses. They started the Academy of Lagado. Now everyone uses the new ideas, but they do not work."

Gulliver visited the academy. There he met an old man with many cucumbers. "The sun's light is in these cucumbers," the old man said. "I want to take it out and keep it. Then it can help people on cold days. I started eight years ago, and I need eight more years. Can you give me some money for my work?" Gulliver gave him a little money. In another room, a man made houses from the top down.

After that, Gulliver went by sea to Japan. From Japan, a ship took him to England. At last, he was home with his family again.

## Level B1

Gulliver was a ship's doctor from England who could never stay at home for long. After two strange voyages, he agreed to sail again on a long voyage. Not long after they left port, pirates came and took the ship. They put Gulliver into a small boat with a little food and left him alone. For five days he rowed from one little island to the next. At last, he landed on a bare, rocky island and slept in a small cave.

The next day, while he was walking on the rocks, the sun suddenly disappeared. Gulliver looked up and saw a huge, dark shape in the sky. It was an island, and it was moving slowly through the air! People were standing on its edge and looking down at him. After a while, they lowered a chain with a seat at the end. Gulliver sat on it, and they pulled him up into the sky. This was the flying island of Laputa.

The people of Laputa were very strange. Their heads always leaned to the right or to the left. One of their eyes turned inward, and the other looked straight up at the stars. They thought about mathematics and music so deeply that they forgot everything else. In the middle of a conversation, they often stopped listening. So every important person had a servant who carried a short stick. At the end of the stick was a small bag with a few dry peas inside. When his master needed to speak, the servant gently tapped his mouth with the bag. When his master needed to listen, the servant tapped his ear.

Next, the servants brought Gulliver to the king. The king was sitting at a table with papers and mathematical tools. For a whole hour, he worked on a problem and did not notice his visitor. Finally, a servant tapped the king's mouth with the bag, and the king looked up. He asked Gulliver many questions, but they did not share a language. So the king sent teachers, and Gulliver learned quickly.

Life on Laputa was full of odd things. At dinner, the cooks cut the meat into triangles and circles. A tailor came to make Gulliver a new suit. He measured him with mathematical instruments instead of a tape. Six days later, the suit arrived, and it did not fit at all. The people of Laputa also worried all the time. "One day the sun will grow cold," they said to each other.

In the center of the island there was a huge magnet. By turning it, the people could make the island rise or fall. They could also move it from place to place over the country below. That country was Balnibarbi, and the king of Laputa ruled it.

Gulliver stayed on the island for two months. Nobody was unkind to him, but nobody cared about him either. They only wanted to talk about lines and numbers, and he soon felt lonely. At last, he asked to go down, and the king agreed.

Gulliver went to Lagado, the capital of Balnibarbi. The city surprised him. Most houses were old and half broken, and the people wore poor clothes. Many people were working in the fields, but very little grew there. A kind lord took Gulliver to his own land outside the city. There, the fields were green, and the houses were strong. "I still farm in the old way," the lord explained. "Forty years ago, some men from Balnibarbi went up to Laputa. They came back full of new ideas, and they started the Academy of Lagado. Now everyone follows the new methods, but none of them work."

Gulliver decided to visit the academy. It had more than five hundred rooms, and every room held a new project. In the first room, he met a thin old man with a dirty face. The man was sitting among piles of cucumbers. "There is sunlight inside these cucumbers," the man explained. "I want to take it out and keep it in closed jars. Then, in a cold summer, people can open the jars and warm the air. I started eight years ago, and I need eight more years." Then he asked Gulliver for some money, and Gulliver gave him a little.

In another room, a man was building a house from the roof down. Another man wanted pigs to dig the fields while they looked for food. Gulliver saw dozens of such projects, and none of them worked.

After that, Gulliver left Balnibarbi on a ship and sailed to Japan. From Japan, another ship carried him across the oceans and home to England. His family was waiting for him, and he was very happy to see them again.

## Level B2

Gulliver, a ship's doctor from England, had promised to stay home after two extraordinary voyages. But the sea soon called him back. He accepted a place on a ship that was setting out on a long trading voyage. Some weeks into the journey, pirates captured the ship. Instead of keeping Gulliver as a prisoner, they put him in a small boat with a little food. For five days he rowed from one little island to another, sleeping wherever he could. At last, he reached a bare, rocky island, where he spent the night in a cave.

The following day, as he was walking among the rocks, the sunlight was suddenly cut off. Gulliver looked up and saw an enormous, solid shape hanging in the sky between him and the sun. To his amazement, it was an island, and it was drifting slowly through the air. Crowds of people stood along its edge, staring down at him and pointing. Gulliver waved his hat and shouted until at last they seemed to understand. After some time, a chain was lowered with a seat fixed to the end of it. He sat down, held on tightly, and was pulled up into the sky. The flying island, he later learned, was called Laputa.

The people of Laputa were unlike anyone he had met before. Every head leaned to one side, and one eye turned inward while the other gazed straight up at the stars. Their minds were so completely filled with mathematics and music that they could hardly follow a normal conversation. For this reason, every person of rank kept a servant with a short stick. A small bag was tied to the end of it. The bag held a few dry peas or tiny stones. When the master needed to speak, the servant gently tapped his mouth with the bag. When he needed to listen, the servant tapped his ear instead. Outside, the servant also tapped his eyes now and then, so that he would not walk into a wall.

Gulliver was led to the palace and brought before the king. The king sat at a table covered with papers and mathematical instruments, deep in a problem. For a full hour he did not even notice that a stranger was standing in front of him. At last a servant tapped the royal mouth, and the king looked up as if he had just woken from a dream. He asked many questions, but Gulliver could not understand a word, so teachers were sent to him. Within a few weeks, he could speak the language of the island well enough.

Everything on Laputa was shaped by numbers and music. At dinner, the meat was cut into triangles and circles, and the bread came in the shapes of musical instruments. A tailor arrived to make Gulliver a new suit. Instead of a tape, he measured him with instruments for studying the stars. When the suit was delivered six days later, it fit him very badly. The people were also full of anxieties, and they could never fully enjoy a quiet night. They feared that the sun might one day grow cold, or that a passing comet might strike the earth.

What kept the island in the air was a huge magnet in its center. By turning this magnet, the island's astronomers could make it rise, sink, or move from place to place. The island could only travel above the country below it, Balnibarbi, which the king of Laputa ruled.

After two months, Gulliver had grown tired of Laputa. Nobody mistreated him, but nobody showed any interest in him or his travels either. The only people who would talk with him freely were the servants, and he felt lonelier every day. At last, he asked permission to go down to the land below, and the king allowed it.

Gulliver was lowered into Balnibarbi and made his way to Lagado, its capital. He was shocked by what he found there. The houses were oddly built and many were half ruined, and the people looked hungry and wore ragged clothes. In the fields, workers were busy with strange tools, yet hardly anything grew. A kind lord who had once held high office invited Gulliver to his own estate in the countryside. There, by contrast, the fields were rich, the trees were full of fruit, and the houses were sound. "I farm the way my father did," the lord explained, "and for that, people think I am a fool. About forty years ago, some men from Balnibarbi went up to Laputa and came back with heads full of new ideas. They founded the Academy of Lagado to improve farming, building and every other art. Not one of their plans has worked, yet everyone follows them and lets the old ways disappear."

Curious, Gulliver went to see the academy for himself. It filled a long row of buildings with more than five hundred rooms, each with its own project. In the first room he found a thin old man with a dirty face, surrounded by cucumbers. "I am extracting sunbeams from these cucumbers," the old man explained proudly. "Once I have them, I will seal them in jars and keep them safe. Then, whenever a summer turns cold, people can open the jars and warm the air. I have been at this work for eight years, and I need only eight more." He complained that cucumbers were expensive and asked Gulliver for a small gift of money, which Gulliver gave him.

The other rooms were no better. One architect was building a house by starting with the roof and working down to the foundations. Another man planned to plow fields by burying food in the soil and letting pigs dig for it. In a large room, a professor showed his students a wooden frame covered with words, which they turned with handles. "With this machine," he said, "even the most ignorant person can write books on any subject." Gulliver saw dozens of such projects, and not one of them was any use.

Having seen enough, Gulliver left Balnibarbi on a ship and sailed to Japan. From Japan, another ship carried him across the oceans and home to England. He found his family in good health, and he was very glad to be back with them.

## Level C1

Twice already Gulliver, a ship's doctor from England, had come home from voyages so improbable that his neighbors scarcely believed a word, and twice he had sworn to stay home. Yet a restless mind is a poor keeper of promises, and before long he had accepted a post on a merchant ship bound for distant markets. The voyage had barely begun to pay its way when pirates overtook the vessel and seized everything on board. Gulliver, whom they judged more trouble than he was worth, was set adrift in a small boat with a few days' food and the whole ocean for company. For five days he rowed from one scrap of island to the next, and at last he came ashore on a bare heap of rock with a shallow cave.

The next day, while he was picking his way among the rocks, the sunlight vanished as abruptly as if someone had drawn a curtain across the sky. Looking up, Gulliver saw a vast, dark body hanging between him and the sun, its underside as flat and smooth as polished stone. It was, beyond any doubt, an island, and it was moving through the air with the slow dignity of a cloud. Along its edge stood rows of people, some of them looking down at him, though most seemed to be looking at nothing in particular. He waved his hat and shouted himself hoarse before anyone appeared to take an interest in him. Eventually a chain was let down with a seat fastened to its end, and Gulliver, deciding that curiosity outweighed caution, climbed on and was hauled into the sky. In this way he arrived on Laputa, the flying island.

Its inhabitants were, he soon concluded, the most absent-minded people ever to walk the earth, or rather to float above it. Every head tilted to one side, and every face wore a curious arrangement of eyes: one turned inward, as though examining its owner's thoughts, while the other stared fixedly at the stars. So wholly were their minds given over to mathematics and music that ordinary conversation slipped past them like water past a stone. To manage this difficulty, each person of standing kept a servant who carried a short stick, at the end of which hung a little bag of dried peas or pebbles. When the master was required to speak, the servant tapped his mouth with the bag; when he was required to listen, the servant tapped his ear. Out of doors the same faithful attendant would tap his eyes now and then, sparing him the indignity of walking into walls or tumbling into ditches.

Gulliver was led into the presence of the king, who sat at a table heaped with papers, globes and mathematical instruments. For a full hour His Majesty wrestled with a problem and remained entirely unaware that a stranger stood before him. Only when a servant tapped the royal mouth did the king start, look up, and remember that he had a visitor. He put a great many questions to Gulliver, not one of which Gulliver could understand, and so teachers were sent to his rooms that very day. Within a few weeks he could manage the language well enough, though he found the islanders far more eager to talk than to listen.

Daily life on the island was arranged entirely around the twin passions of its people. At dinner the meat arrived carved into triangles and circles, and the bread came baked in the shapes of musical instruments. A tailor was summoned to make Gulliver a suit, and instead of a tape he measured his customer with an instrument for observing the stars, covering pages with figures as he worked. Six days later the suit was delivered, and it fit so badly that Gulliver could only admire the confidence with which it had been calculated. For all their learning, the islanders were also the most anxious people he had ever known. They could not sleep soundly for fear that the sun might gradually grow cold, or that a passing comet might one day brush against the earth and spoil it entirely.

The secret of the island lay at its very center, where a magnet of enormous size rested upon a cleverly balanced frame. By turning this magnet one way or another, the island's astronomers could make it rise or sink, and could steer it slowly from place to place. Its range, however, was limited to the country spread out beneath it, Balnibarbi, over which the king of Laputa ruled from the sky.

After two months Gulliver had seen enough of Laputa, and, if he was honest, Laputa had seen almost nothing of him. No one was unkind, but no one ever asked him about his travels, and the only people who would talk with him freely were the servants. Weary of a society that looked straight through him toward the stars, he asked leave to descend, and the king, who was busy with a calculation, granted it without looking up.

Gulliver was lowered into Balnibarbi and made his way to Lagado, its capital, where nothing looked quite as it ought to. Houses leaned at odd angles or stood half finished, the people walked fast and wore ragged clothes, and the fields, though crowded with workers, produced almost nothing at all. A kind lord, who had once held high office and had since fallen out of favor, invited Gulliver to his estate outside the city. There, by contrast, the fields were green, the orchards heavy with fruit, and the farmhouses sturdy and neat. "I farm exactly as my father did," the lord explained, with a smile that held more sadness than amusement, "and for that my neighbors regard me as an enemy of progress. Some forty years ago, a few gentlemen of Balnibarbi went up to Laputa and returned with a taste for mathematics and very little sense. They founded the Academy of Lagado to improve farming, building and every other art, and since then the whole country has been following schemes that never succeed. The odd thing is that failure only makes them more determined."

Curious to see this famous institution for himself, Gulliver paid it a visit. The academy filled a whole row of buildings, and each of its five hundred rooms housed at least one project for the improvement of mankind. In the first room he met a lean, untidy old man whose face and hands were as grimy as his clothes, and who sat surrounded by heaps of cucumbers. "I am extracting sunbeams from these cucumbers," the old man announced with great pride. "When I have collected enough, I will seal them in airtight jars, so that in a cold, wet summer the people may open them and warm the air. I have devoted eight years to the work, and I am confident that eight more will see it finished." He then complained that cucumbers had become shockingly expensive, and Gulliver, who had brought a little money for this purpose, gave him a small gift.

The other rooms offered wonders of the same kind. One architect had devised a new way of building, beginning with the roof and working downward to the foundations, a method he defended by pointing to the bee and the spider. Another man proposed to plow the land with pigs: bury nuts and vegetables in the soil, and the pigs, digging for their dinner, would turn the earth over for free. In a large hall, a professor showed his pupils a great wooden frame covered with words, which they shuffled by turning a set of handles. By this means, he assured Gulliver, the most ignorant person alive might soon write learned books on philosophy, poetry and law, without the least effort of thought. Gulliver thanked him politely, praised his ingenuity, and privately concluded that the academy had never yet produced a single useful thing.

By now his appetite for marvels was thoroughly satisfied. He left Balnibarbi by sea and, after a long passage, reached Japan, where he found a ship ready to carry him across the oceans. Months later he set foot once more in England and found his family in good health. For the moment at least, he was content to live among people whose eyes both looked the same way.
