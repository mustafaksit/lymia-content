# Daedalus and Icarus

Summary: A great inventor makes wings of feathers and wax so he and his son can leave an island.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): wings, feathers, wax

## Level A1

Daedalus could make many great things. He lived on Crete with his son, Icarus. There was water all around Crete. Daedalus wanted to go home. But King Minos did not let them go. Minos wanted Daedalus to work for him. He watched the land and the water.

Daedalus often looked up at the sky. Daedalus said, "Minos can stop us on land. He can stop us on the water. But he cannot stop us up there." Then Daedalus had an idea.

He found many feathers from birds. He put the feathers in a long line. Small feathers came first, then big ones. He put them together with wax. Daedalus made big wings for himself. He also made small wings for Icarus. Icarus played next to his father.

Daedalus put the small wings on Icarus. Daedalus said, "Look at me, Icarus. Do not go too low. Water makes feathers heavy. Then you cannot fly. Do not go too high. The sun can melt the wax. Then the feathers come off. Stay close to me." Daedalus held Icarus for a long time.

The two of them began to fly. They moved their wings like birds. Soon Crete was far behind them. People on the land looked up. "Are they gods?" the people said.

Icarus loved flying. He felt strong and free. Icarus left his father and went up. He went higher and higher. Soon he was near the sun. The sun began to melt the wax. His feathers came off, one by one. Soon he had no feathers. He could not fly now. "Father!" called Icarus. Then he fell into the sea. He did not come back.

Daedalus turned around. Icarus was not there. "Icarus! Where are you?" called Daedalus. He looked down at the water. There he saw only feathers. Daedalus cried for his son. "Why did I make these wings?" he said. Later, Daedalus went on to Sicily without Icarus.

## Level A2

Daedalus was very good at building and making things. He lived on Crete with his young son, Icarus. Crete was in the middle of the sea. Daedalus was not from Crete, and he wanted to go home. But King Minos did not let him leave. Minos wanted Daedalus to work for him. Minos had power over the land and over the sea. So Daedalus and Icarus stayed on Crete for a long time.

Daedalus thought about this problem for many days. Then he looked up at the sky. He said to himself, "Minos can close the land and the sea. But the sky is open. We can go that way." So he began a new kind of work.

First, he brought together many bird feathers. He put them in a line, from small to big. He used thread in the middle and wax at the ends. Now they looked like real bird wings. Icarus stood next to his father and played. He ran after feathers in the wind. He put his hand in the wax and laughed. It was all a game to him.

When the wings were ready, Daedalus tried his own wings first. He moved them, and he went up into the air. Then he put the other wings on Icarus. Daedalus said to his son, "Listen to me, Icarus. Do not fly too low, near the sea. The water can make your feathers heavy. Then you cannot stay in the air. Do not fly too high, near the sun. The sun can melt the wax. Then your feathers can come off. Fly in the middle and follow me." Then Daedalus held his son close. There were tears in his eyes.

The two of them went up into the air together. Daedalus flew first, and Icarus followed him. They moved their wings like birds. Crete got smaller and smaller behind them. Below them, a man stood by the sea. Another man stopped his work in a field. These people looked up and saw the two of them. "They must be gods!" the people said.

At first, Icarus stayed close to his father. But soon he felt happy and free. He forgot his father's words. Icarus wanted to go up and up into the sky. So he left Daedalus and flew higher and higher. Near the sun, the air was very hot. The sun began to melt the wax. One by one, his feathers fell away. Icarus moved his arms, but now there were no feathers on them. The air could not hold him up. "Father!" called Icarus. Then he fell into the deep sea. He did not come back.

Daedalus turned and looked behind him. His son was not there. "Icarus! Icarus, where are you?" called Daedalus. He looked down at the sea for a long time. Then he saw feathers on the water. Now Daedalus understood everything, and he cried for his lost son. "Why did I ever make these wings?" he said. Then he flew on to Sicily without his son.

## Level B1

Daedalus could build and invent wonderful things, but he was not a free man. He lived on the island of Crete with his young son, Icarus. Daedalus came from another land, and he missed his home very much. He often stood on the shore and looked toward the land where he was born. King Minos did not want to lose such a clever man. So he kept Daedalus and Icarus on Crete. Minos ruled the land, and his ships watched every part of the sea. For a long time, Daedalus could not see any way to leave.

Then one day he looked up at the open sky. He said quietly to himself, "Minos can close the roads and the sea. But nobody owns the sky. We will leave through the air." It was a strange idea, but Daedalus was sure about it. From that moment, his clever mind was busy with a new kind of work.

He collected feathers from many different birds and laid them out in a line. The shortest feathers came first, and the longest ones came last. He tied them together in the middle with thread and fixed the ends with wax. Then he bent them gently, so they curved like the wings of a real bird. While his father was working, Icarus was playing beside him. The boy ran after feathers which the wind was carrying away. He pressed the soft yellow wax with his thumb and laughed. He did not understand the danger in his hands.

When he finished the wings, Daedalus tried his own pair first. He moved them up and down, and his body rose into the air. Then he fixed the second pair to his son's arms and shoulders. He showed Icarus how to move his arms slowly and evenly. Daedalus said to Icarus, "Listen to me carefully, my son. Stay in the middle way, between the sea and the sun. Do not fly too low, because the water will make your feathers heavy. Do not fly too high, because the sun will melt the wax. And never choose your own path; just follow me and stay close." As Daedalus spoke, tears ran down his face. He kissed his son, and his hands were shaking a little.

Then Daedalus rose into the air, and Icarus followed close behind him. Like a mother bird with her young one, Daedalus kept looking back at his son. Soon Crete was only a small shape far behind them. They passed several islands on their left and on their right. Down below, a fisherman was standing by the water with his fishing rod. A shepherd was leaning on his stick, and a farmer was working in his field. All three looked up in surprise and thought the two flyers were gods.

At first, Icarus stayed close to his father, as Daedalus told him. But flying was so wonderful that he began to feel bold and free. For the first time in his life, the whole sky seemed to belong to him. He wanted more of the open sky, so he left his father and climbed higher. The closer he came to the sun, the warmer the air became. Slowly the heat softened the wax which held his feathers together. One by one, the feathers came loose and fell away. Icarus waved his bare arms, but without feathers they could not hold him up. He called out his father's name, and then he fell into the sea. He did not come back.

Daedalus turned around, but the sky behind him was empty. "Icarus! Icarus, where are you?" cried Daedalus. He searched the air and the water below him. He flew low over the sea and called again and again, but there was no answer. At last, he saw feathers on the waves, and he understood. Now Daedalus hated the clever skill which made the wings. With a heavy heart, he flew on alone and finally came down in Sicily.

## Level B2

Daedalus was famous as the greatest inventor of his age, a man whose hands could turn almost any idea into something real. Yet for all his skill, he was not free. He and his young son, Icarus, were living on the island of Crete, far from the land where Daedalus was born. Icarus, still a young boy, had grown up on the island and knew no other home. King Minos valued the inventor far too highly to let him go. As a result, father and son were kept on the island year after year. Daedalus had built many wonderful things for the king, but his hard work earned him no freedom. Daedalus grew tired of this long exile and longed to see his home again. But escape seemed impossible, because the king's ships controlled every stretch of sea around Crete. Every harbor was watched, but nobody kept watch over the clouds.

One day, as Daedalus stood looking out over the water, his eyes moved slowly upward. He said to himself, "Minos may block the land and the waves. The sky, however, belongs to no king. That is the road we shall take." The idea was bold, even dangerous, but it was the only way out he could see. From then on, he turned all his skill to an art nobody had ever practiced before.

Day after day he worked in silence, testing each feather and keeping only the best. He gathered feathers of every size and arranged them in a careful row, beginning with the smallest and ending with the longest. The effect was rather like the pipes of a shepherd's flute, which rise little by little in length. He tied the feathers together in the middle with thread and sealed their ends with wax. Finally, he bent the finished rows into a gentle curve, so that they looked exactly like the wings of a real bird. All the while, Icarus was playing at his father's side. He had no idea that he was handling the very thing that would decide his fate. Laughing, he chased the feathers that the breeze blew away, and he pressed the soft yellow wax with his thumb. His games often got in the way of the work, but Daedalus was patient with him. He worked on, his mind fixed on the long journey ahead.

When the last feather was in place, Daedalus fixed one pair of wings to his own arms. He beat them a few times and rose, hanging in the air above the ground. Satisfied, he fitted the second pair to his son and began to teach him how to fly. He showed the boy how to lift his arms, how to glide on the wind and how to come down gently. Daedalus said to Icarus, "Listen to me closely, my son, because your life depends on it. You must keep to the middle course, between the sea and the sun. If you fly too low, the spray from the waves will make your feathers heavy. If you fly too high, the heat of the sun will melt the wax. Do not try to find your own way across the sky; simply follow me." While he was giving these warnings, the inventor's cheeks grew wet with tears, and his hands trembled. He kissed his son one last time, although he could not have known it would be the last.

Then he rose into the air and flew ahead, glancing back again and again. He was like a mother bird leading her young from the nest for the first time. He moved his own wings slowly and carefully, so that the boy could copy every movement. Far below, a fisherman stood with his rod, and a shepherd was resting on his stick. Nearby, a farmer stopped behind his plow. Staring up in wonder, all three decided that the two figures crossing the sky must be gods. Below them the sea stretched in every direction, wide, blue and empty. Soon Crete had disappeared behind them, and they were passing island after island on their left and right. Icarus copied his father's steady movements without difficulty.

At first, Icarus obeyed his father and stayed close behind him. Gradually, however, the joy of flying filled him with a wild new courage. He forgot every warning, left his guide behind and climbed toward the open heavens. The wind rushed past him, the sea shone far below, and he felt as if he could reach the sun itself. The nearer he came to the burning sun, the softer the sweet-smelling wax became, until at last it melted completely. For a moment, he hardly noticed, because he was too happy to look at his wings. His feathers drifted away, and Icarus found himself waving his bare arms in empty air. Without feathers, his arms could no longer catch the wind, and nothing could hold him up. He cried out his father's name as he dropped, and then he fell into the dark blue sea. He did not come back.

Daedalus, now a father without a son, turned in alarm. "Icarus!" he called. "Icarus, where are you? Where should I look for you?" He flew back and forth above the water, calling the boy's name until his voice was hoarse. Then he saw feathers floating on the waves, and he understood what had happened. In his grief, he cursed the clever art that had made the wings possible. He no longer felt any pride in the invention that had once seemed so wonderful. Heavy with sorrow, Daedalus continued his journey alone and finally came to rest in Sicily.

## Level C1

Of all the craftsmen of the ancient world, none was more celebrated than Daedalus, an inventor whose hands seemed able to give solid shape to any idea that crossed his restless mind. Yet his genius had brought him no freedom. Years earlier he had come to Crete, and now, together with his young son Icarus, he found himself unable to leave it. Icarus, still only a boy, knew no other home and hardly seemed to notice the walls of water around him. King Minos, who knew exactly how valuable such a man could be, had no intention of letting him go. He had served the king faithfully, designing works that astonished everyone who saw them, but his loyalty had earned him nothing except a longer stay. Thus, the island that had once welcomed the inventor had slowly become his prison. Daedalus hated the long exile and ached for the land of his birth, but every harbor was watched, and every ship answered to the king.

For weeks, he had studied the problem from every side, as he would study a block of marble, and every answer had ended at the same wall of water. Standing on the shore one day, gazing at the water that hemmed him in, Daedalus let his eyes drift upward to the wide, unguarded sky. He said to himself, "Minos may bar my way across the land and over the waves. The sky, though, is not his to close. That is the road by which we shall leave." It was an audacious plan, and he knew it, for no human being had ever risen from the ground like a bird. Having made up his mind, he turned his whole intelligence toward an art nobody had ever practiced, and in doing so he set out to change the very laws of nature.

He began by gathering feathers of every size and laying them in a careful row, the smallest first and each one a little longer than the one before it. Arranged in this way they rose like the reed pipes of a shepherd's flute, climbing gradually from short to long. He bound the feathers together at the middle with thread and sealed their bases with wax. Then, with great care, he bent the finished rows into a gentle arc, so that they imitated the wings of a living bird. He worked mostly in silence, sorting the feathers again and again, rejecting any that were bent or thin and keeping only those strong enough to bear weight. Through all these patient hours, Icarus was at his elbow, a cheerful little nuisance who had no notion that he was playing with the instrument of his own fate. Now he would chase a feather that a passing breeze had snatched away; now he would knead the soft yellow wax with his thumb. With every game, he got a little more in his father's way. Daedalus, absorbed in the work, merely smiled and let the boy play.

When the final feather had been set in place, the inventor fastened one pair of wings to his own arms and beat them experimentally. Slowly he rose until he was hanging, perfectly balanced, in the air above the ground. Satisfied with the test, he fitted the second pair to his son's shoulders and began to explain, with a seriousness the boy had rarely seen in him, exactly how he must fly. He showed the boy how to spread his arms, how to lean into a current of air and how to come down gently, repeating each lesson patiently. Daedalus said to Icarus, "Listen to every word, my son, because your life may depend on it. Always keep to the middle path, halfway between the sea below and the sun above. Should you fly too low, the spray of the waves will soak your feathers and drag you down. Should you fly too high, the fire of the sun will melt the wax that holds your wings together. Do not set your own course by the stars or wander wherever the wind invites you; follow me, and stay close." Even as he gave these instructions, the inventor's cheeks were wet with tears, and his fingers trembled as he worked. Then he kissed his son, a kiss that neither of them could have known would be their last.

Rising on his new wings, Daedalus led the way, turning his head again and again to watch the boy behind him. He was like a mother bird coaxing her young out of the nest for their first flight. He moved his own wings slowly and deliberately, inviting Icarus to copy every stroke and to trust the air that now carried them both. Far beneath them, a fisherman paused with his trembling rod, a shepherd leaned upon his crook, and a farmer let go of his plow to stare upward in astonishment. Watching the two figures sail across the heavens, they could only conclude that these were gods passing overhead. Crete soon sank behind the travelers, and island after island slipped past on either side, small and bright in the sea. Beneath them the water spread out in every direction, vast and blue and empty to the horizon. The boy copied his father's steady strokes, and Daedalus began, cautiously, to hope that the hardest part of the journey lay behind them.

For a while Icarus did exactly as he had been told, keeping obediently in his father's wake. Little by little, however, the sheer delight of flying went to his head, and a reckless courage began to swell inside him. Forgetting every warning, he broke away from his guide and, drawn by a longing for the open sky, climbed higher and higher toward the blazing sun. The wind roared in his ears, the sea glittered far beneath him, and it seemed to him that nothing in the world could ever bring him down again. The closer he came, the softer grew the fragrant wax that bound his feathers, until at last it gave way altogether and melted. At first, he hardly noticed, so lost was he in the thrill of height and speed. One by one the feathers drifted loose, and Icarus discovered that he was beating his bare arms against nothing at all. Without their feathers, his arms could catch no wind, and there was nothing left to hold him in the air. His father's name was on his lips as he dropped; he fell into the dark blue sea, and he did not come back.

Daedalus, a father who no longer had a son to look back at, turned in sudden fear. "Icarus!" he called. "Icarus, where are you? In what part of the sky should I search for you?" Back and forth he flew above the water, crying the boy's name until his voice grew hoarse, but the waves gave back no answer except their own restless sound. He was still calling when he caught sight of feathers floating on the waves, and at once the whole truth broke upon him. Overcome with grief, he cursed the very skill that had made him famous, the clever art that had given his son wings only to take him away. Even the pride he had once taken in his craft now felt like a bitter joke. Every beat of his wings seemed heavier than the last, for he now carried a weight that no invention of his could ever lift. Weighed down by sorrow, Daedalus flew on alone across the sea until, at the end of his long and bitter journey, he came to rest in Sicily.
