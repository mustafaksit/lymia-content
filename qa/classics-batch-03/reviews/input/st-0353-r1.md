# The Truth About Pyecraft

Summary: A very large man begs his club friend for an old family recipe to lose weight.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): recipe, ceiling, weight, lead

## Level A1

My name is Formalyn. I often ate at a club. A man called Pyecraft ate there too. Pyecraft was a very big, round man. He liked his food very much. He often sat with me and talked.

One evening I talked about my family. "My family has some old recipes," I said. "They came from a land far away. They are not for food. They can change your body." Pyecraft sat near me and heard every word.

"Please give me a recipe," said Pyecraft. "I want to lose weight." I did not want to give him one. The recipes were very old. But he asked me every day. At last, I gave him one recipe. "Take care with it," I said.

Pyecraft stayed away for a few days. Then a short message came from Pyecraft. "Please come now and help me!" it said. So I went to his home. I opened his door and went in. "Close the door, please," said a voice. I looked up. Pyecraft was at the ceiling, above the door!

"Look at me!" said Pyecraft. "Your recipe did this! Now I can't come down." "What did you ask for?" I said. "I asked to lose weight," said Pyecraft. "And you did," I said. "Your weight is gone. But your size is the same. Now you are as light as air."

I pulled him down to the floor. But then he went up again. "No one must see me," said Pyecraft. "Please help me."

I thought for a long time. Then I had an idea. "Lead has a lot of weight," I said. "Put some lead under your clothes. Then you can stay down."

Pyecraft did this. He wore lead under his clothes every day. Now he could walk on the floor again. Soon he came back to the club. He was still big and round. But no one knew about the lead. Only Pyecraft and I knew. And now you know too.

## Level A2

My name is Formalyn. For many years I often ate at a club in the city. Another member of the club was a man called Pyecraft. Pyecraft was large and round, and he loved food. He always ate a lot, and he always wanted more. He often sat at my table and talked and talked.

One evening I told two friends about my family. "My family has some very old recipes," I said. "They came from a land far away. They are not recipes for cooking. They are recipes for changing the body." Pyecraft sat at the next table and heard every word.

From that day, Pyecraft did not leave me alone. "Please give me one of those recipes," said Pyecraft. "I want to lose weight. Nothing else works." I did not like this plan. The recipes were old and strange, and I did not understand them. But Pyecraft asked me every day for many weeks. In the end, I gave him one recipe, just to stop him. "Be very careful with it," I told him.

After that, Pyecraft did not come to the club. A few days later, I got a short message from him. "Please come to my home now and help me!" it said. So I went to Pyecraft's home. I opened his door and went in. At first the room looked empty. "Close the door, please," said a voice from above. I looked up, and there was Pyecraft. He was at the ceiling, near the top of the door!

"Your recipe did this!" said Pyecraft. "I took it, and then I started to go up. Now I can't come down." "What was the recipe for?" I asked. "It was for loss of weight," said Pyecraft. "Well, the recipe did its job," I said. "You asked to lose weight, not to get smaller. Your body is the same size, but it has no weight." Pyecraft looked very worried.

I took Pyecraft's arm and pulled him down to the floor. Pyecraft held on to the table, but soon he went up again. "No one must know about this," said Pyecraft. "The people at the club must never see me like this."

I sat down and thought for a long time. Then I had a good idea. "You need more weight," I said. "Lead has a lot of weight. Put some lead under your clothes, and wear it all the time. Then you can walk on the floor again." Pyecraft liked this idea.

So Pyecraft wore lead under his clothes every day. Soon he could walk again, and he came back to the club. Pyecraft still ate a lot, and he was still large and round. But no one at the club knew about the lead. Only Pyecraft and I knew. And now you know too.

## Level B1

My name is Formalyn, and I want to tell the truth about a man called Pyecraft. For many years we were both members of the same quiet club in the city. He was the largest man I ever knew, round and soft and very fond of food. He ate more than anyone else at the club, and he always ordered a second pudding. He liked to sit near me and talk, although I did not always want to listen.

One evening I was telling two friends about my great-grandmother. She was a clever, unusual woman, and she traveled far to the east as a girl. She came home with a leather case full of strange old recipes. "They are not for cooking," I told my friends. "They are for changing the body, and some of them are very powerful." I did not notice Pyecraft at the next table, but he heard every word.

The next day he was waiting for me at the door of the club. "Formalyn, I need one of those recipes," said Pyecraft. "I have tried everything to lose weight, and nothing works. Your great-grandmother is my last hope." I told him that the recipes were old and strange. I also said that I did not understand most of them myself. But Pyecraft did not give up. He asked me at lunch, at dinner and in the hall, day after day, for weeks.

In the end I opened the leather case and copied one recipe for him. My great-grandmother wrote it long ago in her small, careful hand. "Take it slowly and be careful," I warned him. Pyecraft thanked me again and again. Then he hurried away with the paper in his pocket.

For the next few days Pyecraft did not come to the club. I was starting to feel a little worried when a telegram arrived. "For heaven's sake come at once," it said, and his address followed. I put on my coat and went straight to his flat.

The door of the flat was not locked, so I went in. The sitting room looked empty, and everything was very quiet. "Shut the door, please," said a weak voice somewhere above me. I looked up, and there was Pyecraft. He was floating against the ceiling in the corner near the top of the door. His round face was pink, and he looked very unhappy.

"Look what your recipe did to me!" said Pyecraft. "I took it, and the next morning I started to rise. Now I can't come down, and I can't let anyone see me like this." "What was the recipe for?" I asked. "Loss of weight, of course," he said. "Then it worked perfectly," I said. "You wanted to lose fat, but you asked to lose weight. You are still the same size, but now you weigh nothing at all." Pyecraft stared at me, and I could see that this idea was new to him.

I climbed onto a chair, took hold of his arm and pulled him down. He held tightly to the edge of the table. But the moment he let go, he floated slowly up again. "Nobody must ever know about this," he said. "Everyone at the club will laugh at me."

I sat down and thought about the problem for a long time. Then I had an idea. "You need more weight, Pyecraft, and lead is very heavy," I said. "Put flat pieces of lead inside your underclothes, and wear them all the time. Then you can walk on the floor like everyone else." Pyecraft's face lit up, and for the first time that day he smiled.

That is exactly what he did. He wore lead under his clothes from morning to night, and soon he could walk again. A few weeks later he came back to the club and ate a large dinner. He is still the same size, and he still eats more than anyone else. Nobody at the club knows about the lead, and Pyecraft keeps his secret carefully. Only he and I know the truth, and now you know it too.

## Level B2

My name is Formalyn, and I have decided to tell the truth about Pyecraft, because I am tired of keeping it. Pyecraft and I belonged to the same quiet club, a place of deep armchairs, soft carpets and very good dinners. He was, without question, the largest man in the building, round and soft and endlessly fond of his food. I have never seen anyone eat with such joy; he took a second helping of everything and a third of the pudding. He also liked to sit beside me and talk, and I did not always enjoy it as much as he did.

The trouble began one evening when I was chatting with two friends about my great-grandmother. She was a remarkable woman who had traveled far to the east as a girl. She had come home with a leather case full of old recipes, and the family still kept it. "They aren't for cooking," I explained to my friends. "They're for changing the body, and some of them are said to be extremely powerful." I had no idea that Pyecraft was sitting at the next table, listening to every word with great interest.

From that night on, he gave me no peace. He caught me in the hall, followed me into the reading room and found a seat beside me at every dinner. "Formalyn, my dear fellow, you must let me have one of those recipes," he would say. "I've tried every diet in the world, and nothing has worked. Your great-grandmother is my last hope." I told him again and again that the recipes were old and that I understood very little of them. None of this made any difference. For weeks, he begged, praised and sighed, until I began to dread the sight of him.

In the end, mainly to win back my peace and quiet, I opened the leather case and copied one recipe. At the top, in my great-grandmother's small and careful hand, were the words "Loss of Weight." "Be very careful with it," I warned him as I handed him the paper. "If anything strange happens, stop at once." He thanked me so warmly that I almost felt ashamed of my unkind thoughts. Then he hurried off with the paper in his pocket.

For several days after that, Pyecraft was not seen at the club. The club seemed wonderfully calm without his voice in every room. At first, I was simply glad of the quiet, but after a while I began to feel uneasy. Then a telegram arrived. "For heaven's sake come at once," it said, and his address followed. I put on my coat and went straight round to his flat.

The door of the flat had been left unlocked, so I let myself in. The sitting room appeared to be empty, and the whole flat was strangely silent. "Shut the door, please," said a small, unhappy voice somewhere above my head. I looked up, and there, floating against the ceiling in the corner above the door, was Pyecraft. He hung in the air as lightly as a child's balloon, and his round face was pink with worry and embarrassment.

"Look what your recipe has done to me!" cried Pyecraft. "I took it, and the very next morning I began to rise. I've been up here ever since, and I can't get down." "What exactly did you ask for?" I said, trying very hard not to smile. "Loss of weight, of course!" said Pyecraft. "Well, then, the recipe has done its job perfectly," I replied. "What you really wanted was to lose fat, but what you asked for was to lose weight. You are just as large as you were before, but you no longer weigh anything at all." Pyecraft stared at me, and I could see that this idea had never crossed his mind.

I climbed onto a chair, took a firm hold of his arm and pulled him gently down to the floor. He clung to the edge of the table, but the moment he let go, he drifted slowly back up again. He had been sleeping against the ceiling, he told me, and moving about by pushing himself along it. "Nobody must ever know about this," he begged. "If the members of the club found out, I could never show my face there again."

I sat down in his armchair and thought about the problem for a long while. I had no recipe for gaining weight, so I could not simply undo the first one. What he needed, I realized, was something to hold him down. Then the answer came to me. "Lead, Pyecraft," I said. "It is one of the heaviest things a man can easily get. Have flat sheets of lead sewn into your underclothes, and wear them all day long. With enough weight under your clothes, you will be able to walk on the floor like everyone else." For the first time that day, Pyecraft's face broke into a broad, grateful smile.

That is exactly what he did. The lead was sewn into his underclothes, and once he wore them, he could stand, walk and sit like anyone else. A few weeks later he returned to the club and ate a very large dinner, as if nothing had happened. He is still the same size, and he still eats more than anyone else in the room.

Nobody at the club knows why he moves so slowly and carefully, and Pyecraft guards his secret closely. Only he and I know the truth, and now, at last, I have told it. So if you ever see a large man at my club walking slowly to dinner, you will know what he carries.

## Level C1

My name is Formalyn, and I have kept a certain secret for long enough; it is time, I think, that somebody told the truth about Pyecraft. We belonged, he and I, to the same quiet club, a place of deep leather armchairs, rustling newspapers and dinners of a quality that encouraged a man to linger. Among its members Pyecraft stood out at once, for he was quite simply the largest man in the building, round, soft and gloriously devoted to his food. I have never met anyone who ate with such innocent happiness: he took a second helping of everything, a third of the pudding, and looked about hopefully for a fourth. He was also, unfortunately for me, a great talker, and from my first week at the club he had decided that the empty chair beside mine was the one he liked best.

It was my own careless tongue that started the whole affair. One evening, chatting with two friends after dinner, I happened to mention my great-grandmother, a woman of remarkable independence who had traveled far to the east as a girl. She had returned with a leather case of recipes copied out in her small, precise hand, and the family had kept it ever since with a mixture of pride and suspicion. "They aren't for the kitchen," I explained, rather enjoying the effect on my audience. "They are meant to change the body itself, and some of them, if the family stories are true, are alarmingly powerful." What I failed to notice was that Pyecraft was sitting at the next table, perfectly still for once, taking in every word.

From that night onward, he gave me no peace whatsoever. He waited for me in the hall, appeared at my elbow in the reading room and somehow always managed to find a seat beside me at dinner. "Formalyn, my dear fellow, you really must let me have one of those recipes," he would murmur, leaning toward me with an expression of the deepest sincerity. "I've tried every diet ever invented, and not one of them has done me the slightest good. Your great-grandmother is my last hope." I explained, patiently and then less patiently, that the recipes were ancient, that I understood almost nothing about them and that I could not possibly answer for the results. None of it made the smallest impression. For weeks, he pleaded, flattered and sighed, until the mere sight of his large, hopeful face across the dining room was enough to spoil my appetite.

In the end it was not kindness but sheer exhaustion that defeated me. I unlocked the leather case one evening, chose a recipe more or less at random and copied it out for him word for word. At the top, in my great-grandmother's neat lettering, stood the title "Loss of Weight," which seemed to me to settle the matter rather neatly. "Be very careful with it," I warned him as I handed over the paper, "and if anything strange happens, stop at once." He thanked me with such warmth and such obvious gratitude that I felt a twinge of shame for every unkind thought I had ever had about him. Then he folded the paper into his pocket and hurried out into the night like a man who had been handed the key to paradise.

For several days afterwards Pyecraft was nowhere to be seen, and the club seemed almost spacious without him. I confess that I enjoyed the silence at first; it was only toward the end of the week that a small, nagging unease began to creep in. Then a telegram arrived, brief and urgent: "For heaven's sake come at once," followed by nothing but his address. I put on my coat without finishing my breakfast and went straight round to his flat.

The door of the flat had been left unlocked, so I let myself in and stood for a moment in the hall, listening. The sitting room appeared to be quite empty, and the whole place had the heavy stillness of a house where something has gone wrong. "Shut the door, please," said a small and very unhappy voice from somewhere above my head. I looked up, and there, pressed softly against the ceiling in the corner above the door, floated Pyecraft. He hung there as weightlessly as a child's balloon, his round face pink with worry and embarrassment, his hands spread against the plaster to steady himself.

"Look what your wretched recipe has done to me!" cried Pyecraft. "I took it exactly as it was written, and the very next morning I began to rise. I've been up here ever since, and I simply cannot get down." "What exactly did you ask for?" I said, struggling to keep my face perfectly serious. "Loss of weight, of course!" said Pyecraft. "Then the recipe has done precisely what it promised," I replied. "What you wanted, my dear Pyecraft, was to lose fat; what you asked for was to lose weight, and those, it turns out, are two quite different things. You are every bit as large as you were before, but you no longer weigh anything at all." He stared down at me in silence, and I could see the full meaning of it settling slowly upon him.

I climbed onto a chair, took a firm hold of his arm and drew him gently down until his feet touched the carpet. He clung to the edge of the table with both hands, but the instant he loosened his grip, he drifted serenely back up to his corner. In a rush of words he described his last few days: sleeping against the ceiling, moving about by pushing himself along it, and dreading every knock at the door. "Nobody must ever know about this," he begged. "If the members of the club found out, I could never show my face there again."

I settled into his armchair and gave the problem the most serious thought I had given anything in years. There was no recipe in the leather case for gaining weight, at least none that I could find, so undoing the damage was out of the question. What he needed, I realized, was not a cure but a kind of anchor, something that would hold him to the earth whatever his body chose to do. And then, quite suddenly, the answer presented itself. "Lead, Pyecraft," I said. "It is about the heaviest thing an ordinary man can lay his hands on. Have flat sheets of lead sewn into your underclothes, wear them from morning till night, and you will walk the floor like everybody else." For the first time that day his face broke into a broad, astonished smile, the smile of a man who has been shown a door where he had seen only a wall.

That, in short, is exactly what he did. The lead was sewn into his underclothes, and once he had struggled into them, he could stand, walk and sit like any other gentleman, if somewhat more deliberately than before. A few weeks later he reappeared at the club, lowered himself into his favorite chair and ordered a dinner of truly heroic size, as though nothing whatever had happened. He is still precisely the same shape, and he still eats more than anyone else in the room.

Nobody at the club understands why he moves with such slow and careful dignity, and Pyecraft guards his secret as closely as any man has ever guarded anything. Only he and I know the truth, and I have now, at last, set it down. So if you should ever visit my club and notice a very large gentleman making his way, with great deliberation, toward the dining room, you will know exactly what he carries.
