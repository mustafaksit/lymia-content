# The Three Golden Apples

Summary: The strong hero Hercules travels to the end of the world to find three golden apples.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): apples, sky, giant

## Level A1

Hercules was a very strong man. A king gave him a hard job. "Bring me the golden apples," said the king. They grew in the garden of the Hesperides. But Hercules did not know the way.

Then he met some women by a river. "Where is the garden?" asked Hercules. "Ask the Old Man of the Sea. He knows the way," said the women.

Hercules found the old man by the sea. The old man was asleep. Hercules took hold of him. The old man opened his eyes. He became a deer. Then he became a bird. Next he was a dog, then a snake. But Hercules held on. At last the old man stopped. "Go to the giant Atlas. He can help you," said the old man.

On the way, Hercules met another giant, Antaeus. Antaeus wanted to fight. Antaeus got his power from the ground. So Hercules held him high above the ground. Soon Antaeus had no power left. Hercules won.

Then Hercules came to the sea again. A big cup came over the water. Hercules sat in it. It carried him across the sea.

There he saw Atlas. Atlas was very, very big. He held the sky on his shoulders. "I can get the apples for you. But you must hold the sky," said Atlas. So Hercules took the sky. It was very hard to hold. Atlas walked away into the sea.

Soon Atlas came back with three apples. Atlas said, "Now I am free. You hold the sky. I can take the apples to the king."

Hercules said, "All right. But please hold the sky for a minute. I want my coat on my shoulders. Then the sky is not so heavy."

Atlas put the apples down. He took the sky again. Hercules took the apples. "Thank you, Atlas!" said Hercules. He walked away with the apples. Atlas still holds the sky today.

## Level A2

Hercules was the strongest man in the world. One day a king gave him a very hard job. "Bring me the golden apples of the Hesperides," said the king. The apples grew in a garden, but nobody knew the way there. So Hercules went out to find it.

At last, he came to a river. Some young women sat near it. "Where is the garden of the Hesperides?" asked Hercules. The women said, "We do not know the way. But the Old Man of the Sea knows it. Find him and do not let him go."

Hercules thanked the women and went to the sea. There he found the old man asleep by the water. Hercules came close and took hold of him. The old man opened his eyes and changed into a deer. Then he changed into a seabird, then a big dog. Last of all, he became a long snake. But Hercules held on to him the whole time.

At last the old man stopped. "What do you want from me?" he asked. "I want to know the way to the garden," said Hercules. The old man said, "Go to the giant Atlas. He lives at the end of the world. He can help you." Hercules let him go and went on his way.

On the road, Hercules met another giant, Antaeus. Antaeus wanted to fight him. Antaeus got his power from the ground. Each time he touched the ground, he became stronger. Hercules saw this, so he held Antaeus above the ground. Up there, Antaeus lost all his power. Hercules won the fight and walked on.

Soon Hercules came to the sea again. A huge golden cup came toward him on the water. Hercules sat down in it. It carried him across the sea to the end of the world.

There Hercules saw a giant as big as a mountain. It was Atlas, and he held the sky on his shoulders. "I am Hercules, and I want the golden apples," said Hercules. Atlas said, "I know the garden well. I can get the apples for you. But first you must hold the sky for me." Hercules took the sky. Nothing in the world was so hard to hold. Atlas walked into the sea with long steps.

After some time, Atlas came back with three golden apples. He felt light and free without the sky. Atlas said, "I do not want the sky back. I can take the apples to your king myself."

Hercules thought fast. Then he said, "All right. But please hold the sky for one minute. I want to put my coat on my shoulders. Then the sky is not so heavy on them."

Atlas did not understand Hercules's plan. He put the apples down and took the sky again. At once, Hercules took the three apples. "Thank you, Atlas!" said Hercules. He walked away with the apples. And Atlas still holds the sky today.

## Level B1

Long ago there lived a man called Hercules, who was stronger than anyone in the world. He wore the skin of a lion as a coat, and he never felt afraid. One day a king gave him a very difficult task. "Bring me the golden apples of the Hesperides," the king said. These apples grew in a wonderful garden, but nobody could say where it was. Hercules did not ask any questions. He simply put on his coat and set off.

He traveled for many days through strange lands. At last, he came to a river, where some young women were sitting on the grass. They were making long chains of flowers. Hercules greeted them politely. "Can you tell me the way to the garden of the Hesperides?" he asked. The women looked surprised, and one of them answered him. "We do not know the way ourselves. But the Old Man of the Sea knows it. You must find him, catch him and hold him very tight. He will try everything to get away from you." Hercules thanked them warmly and walked toward the sea.

After a long search, he found the old man lying asleep on the sand. Hercules came closer without a sound and caught him with both hands. At once the old man woke up and turned into a deer. A moment later he was a seabird, beating his wings. Then he became a fierce dog, and after that a long, slippery snake. But Hercules kept his grip the whole time and did not let go.

At last the old man gave up and took his own shape again. "Who are you, and what do you want from me?" he asked. "I am Hercules, and I am looking for the garden of the Hesperides," said Hercules. The old man told him, "You must go to the giant Atlas. He stands at the end of the world and holds up the sky. He knows where the garden is, and he can help you." Hercules let the old man go and continued his journey.

On the road he met another giant, whose name was Antaeus. Antaeus was huge and loved to fight with every traveler he met. His strength came from the earth under his feet. Every time he touched the ground, he grew stronger than before. Hercules soon noticed this, so he lifted Antaeus high into the air and held him there. Away from the ground, the giant became weaker and weaker. In the end Hercules won the fight, and he went on his way.

Soon he reached the sea again, but there was no ship and no bridge. Then he saw something bright on the waves. It was a huge golden cup, and it was floating toward him. Hercules climbed into the cup and sat down. The cup carried him over the water to the end of the world.

There he saw what looked like a mountain, with clouds around its top. But it was not a mountain at all. It was the giant Atlas, and he was holding the sky on his shoulders. "Who are you, little man?" Atlas called down. "I am Hercules, and I have come for the golden apples," Hercules replied. Atlas said, "I know that garden well. I can get the apples for you, but only on one condition. You must hold the sky while I am away." Hercules agreed, and the giant carefully put the sky onto Hercules's shoulders. Nothing in the world felt heavier. Atlas walked into the sea with huge steps and soon disappeared.

Hercules waited for a long time under his terrible load. At last Atlas came back, holding three golden apples in his hand. The giant looked very pleased with himself. Atlas said, "It feels wonderful to be free. I do not want the sky back. I will take the apples to your king myself, and you can stay here."

Hercules understood the danger, but he stayed calm. He said, "Very well. But please hold the sky for just one minute. I want to fold my coat and put it on my shoulders. Then the sky will not press so hard on them."

Atlas did not see the trick. He put the golden apples on the ground and lifted the sky back onto his shoulders. At once Hercules picked up the apples. "Thank you for your help, Atlas, and goodbye!" he called. Then he turned and walked away with the apples. As far as anyone knows, Atlas is still holding up the sky today.

## Level B2

Long ago, when the world was younger, there lived a hero named Hercules, whose strength was known in every land. He wore a coat made from the skin of a lion, and he walked with long, steady steps. One day a king, who enjoyed giving Hercules impossible tasks, called him to the palace. "Bring me the golden apples of the Hesperides," the king ordered. Everyone had heard stories about those apples, which grew in a garden at the far edge of the world. Yet nobody had ever been there, and nobody could tell Hercules which way to go. Without complaining, he put on his coat and set off to find the place himself.

For many days he wandered through forests, deserts and strange countries, asking everyone he met about the garden. At last, he reached a quiet river, where young women sat on the bank, weaving long chains of flowers. They looked up in surprise when the tall stranger in the lion skin stepped out of the trees. Hercules greeted them politely and asked whether they knew the road to the garden of the Hesperides. The women exchanged glances, and then one of them answered him kindly. "We cannot show you the way ourselves, but the Old Man of the Sea knows every path on earth. Look for him on the shore, and if you catch him, hold him as tightly as you can. He will change his shape again and again, so you must not let go, whatever he becomes." Hercules thanked them warmly and turned toward the sea.

After a long search, he spotted the old man on the sand, fast asleep in the sun. Moving without a sound, Hercules crept closer and seized him firmly with both hands. The old man woke with a start, and in an instant he was no longer a man but a deer, kicking wildly. A moment later the deer had become a seabird that beat its wings wildly. Then the bird turned into a fierce dog, and the dog in turn became a long, slippery snake. Through every change, however, Hercules kept his grip, exactly as the women had told him. Finally, the old man grew tired of the game and returned to his own shape. "What do you want from me, stranger?" he asked crossly. "Only the way to the garden of the Hesperides," Hercules replied. The old man sighed and said, "Then you must find the giant Atlas. He stands at the very end of the world and holds up the sky on his shoulders. Nobody knows that garden better than he does, so he is the one who can help you." Hercules released him, and the old man slipped back into the waves.

The road to the end of the world was long, and on it Hercules met another giant, called Antaeus. Antaeus challenged every traveler to a wrestling match. The secret of his strength was the earth itself. Each time his feet touched the ground, his power came back stronger than before. Hercules realized this after a few moments, so he lifted Antaeus high into the air and held him there. Cut off from the earth, the giant grew weaker and weaker until he could no longer struggle. Hercules had won, and without further delay he continued on his journey.

Eventually he arrived at the shore of a great sea, but he could see no ship or bridge to carry him across. As he stood wondering what to do, something bright appeared far out on the waves. It was a huge golden cup, big enough to hold a man, and the current was carrying it straight toward him. Hercules climbed in, sat down, and let the cup carry him over the water until it reached the end of the world.

There he saw what looked at first like a mountain, with clouds gathered around its top. As he came nearer, however, he realized that the mountain was in fact a giant. It was Atlas, and on his enormous shoulders rested the whole weight of the sky. "Who are you, little man, and why have you come here?" Atlas called down from among the clouds. "I am Hercules, and I am looking for the golden apples of the Hesperides," Hercules shouted back. Atlas answered, "I know that garden better than anyone. I could fetch the apples for you, but only if you hold up the sky while I am gone." Hercules agreed, and the giant carefully lowered the sky onto his shoulders. Never in his life had Hercules felt such a weight, yet he stood firm and did not bend. Atlas, meanwhile, waded into the sea with enormous strides and soon vanished from sight.

Hercules waited for what seemed like a very long time, while the sky pressed down on him. At last Atlas came striding back through the waves, holding three golden apples in his hand. But instead of taking back his burden, the giant stopped a little way off and looked very pleased with himself. The giant said, "Do you know how wonderful it feels to be free? I have held up that sky for ages, and I have no wish to hold it again. I will take these apples to your king myself, and you can stay here in my place."

Hercules understood at once that he was in serious trouble, but he kept his voice calm. As if the idea did not bother him at all, he said, "Very well. But please hold the sky for just one minute. I would like to fold my coat into a cushion for my shoulders." The suggestion sounded perfectly reasonable, and Atlas did not suspect a trick. He set the golden apples down on the ground and lifted the sky back onto his own shoulders.

That was all Hercules needed. He picked up the three golden apples, thanked Atlas for his help, and said goodbye. Then he turned and walked away, and the giant could do nothing to stop him. As far as anyone knows, Atlas is still standing at the end of the world today, holding up the sky.

## Level C1

In the days when the world was still young and full of marvels, there lived a hero named Hercules, whose strength had become a byword in every land he crossed. He went about wrapped in a coat made from the skin of a lion. He walked with the long, unhurried stride of a man who had never yet met a task too large for him. It so happened that a certain king, who seemed to take a quiet pleasure in setting Hercules impossible labors, summoned him one day and gave him a new command. "Bring me the golden apples of the Hesperides," the king said, as casually as if he were asking for a basket of plums from the market. Everyone had heard of those apples, which were said to grow in a garden somewhere near the edge of the world, yet nobody could say where that garden lay. Hercules, who was not in the habit of complaining, simply put on his coat and set out to discover the way for himself.

For a great many days he wandered through forests and deserts and countries whose names he had never heard. Wherever he went he asked about the garden, and everywhere he received nothing but puzzled looks in return. At length, he came to a quiet river, and there, on the grassy bank, sat a group of young women who were weaving long chains of flowers and chatting among themselves. They looked up in some surprise when the tall stranger in the lion skin stepped out from among the trees, for travelers of his size were rare in that peaceful place. Hercules greeted them with great courtesy and asked whether any of them could tell him the road to the garden of the Hesperides. The women glanced at one another, and after a moment one of them answered on behalf of all. "None of us can show you the way, but there is someone who knows every path on land and sea, and that is the Old Man of the Sea. Look for him along the shore, and if you are lucky enough to catch him, hold him as tightly as you possibly can. He will change his shape again and again to frighten you into letting go, so whatever he turns into, you must not loosen your grip." Hercules thanked them sincerely for their advice, and, leaving them to their flowers, he turned his steps toward the sea.

The search took him a long while, but at last he caught sight of the old man stretched out on the sand. The old man lay fast asleep in the warm sunshine, while the foam of the waves crept up close to him. Moving so softly that not even a grain of sand stirred beneath his feet, Hercules crept up to the sleeper and seized him firmly with both hands. The old man woke with a start, and in that very instant there was no longer an old man in Hercules's arms but a deer, kicking and twisting to break free. Hardly had Hercules adjusted his hold when the deer became a seabird, beating its great wings against his face, and then a fierce dog, snapping and growling. Last of all the dog melted into a long, slippery snake that coiled itself about his arms. Yet through every one of these changes Hercules held on, just as the women had advised. At last the old man, seeing that his tricks were useless, gave up the struggle and returned to his own shape, looking thoroughly annoyed. "What do you want from me, that you come and disturb an old man's sleep?" he demanded. "Nothing more than the way to the garden of the Hesperides," Hercules replied. The old man grumbled for a while and then said, "In that case you must find the giant Atlas. He stands at the very end of the world and holds the sky upon his shoulders. Nobody knows that garden better than he does, and if anyone can help you, it is Atlas." Satisfied, Hercules released him, and the old man slid back into the waves without another word.

The road to the end of the world was long and lonely. Somewhere along it Hercules met another giant, a huge fellow named Antaeus, who insisted on wrestling with every traveler who passed his way. What made Antaeus so hard to beat was that his strength came from the earth itself, so that every time his feet touched the ground, he rose up stronger than before. Hercules saw the trick of it within the first few moments of their match. Rather than throwing the giant down, he lifted him high into the air and simply held him there. Separated from the earth that fed his strength, Antaeus grew weaker and weaker until he could struggle no longer, and the contest was over. Hercules set off again without wasting another moment, for the end of the world was still far away.

In time, he came to the shore of a vast sea, and there he stopped, since he could see neither ship nor bridge nor any other way of crossing it. While he stood on the sand wondering what to do, something bright caught his eye far out among the waves, shining like a second sun. It was a golden cup of enormous size, large enough to hold a grown man, and the current was carrying it steadily toward the very spot where he stood. Without hesitating, Hercules climbed into it and sat down, and the cup bore him smoothly across the water until at last it brought him to the end of the world.

What he saw there looked at first like a mountain, so high that clouds had gathered around its summit, and he could not make out where it ended. Only as he drew nearer did he understand that the mountain was in fact a giant, standing as still as stone, with the whole weight of the sky resting upon his shoulders. "Who are you, little man, and what brings you to the end of the world?" called Atlas, for that was who it was, from somewhere up among the clouds. "I am Hercules, and I am looking for the golden apples of the Hesperides," Hercules shouted back, his voice sounding very small in that empty place. Atlas considered this for a moment before he answered, "I know that garden better than anyone alive, and I could fetch the apples for you easily enough. There is only one difficulty, which is that somebody must hold up the sky while I am gone." Hercules agreed at once, and the giant, with great care, shifted the sky from his own shoulders onto those of his visitor. Never in all his labors had Hercules felt a weight like it, yet he planted his feet, set his jaw and refused to bend. Atlas, meanwhile, stretched his enormous limbs with a sigh of relief, then waded out into the sea with huge strides and soon vanished from sight.

How long Hercules stood there he could not have said, but it felt like an age, with the sky pressing down on him and nothing to do except wait. At last Atlas came striding back through the waves, holding three golden apples in his hand. Yet instead of taking back his burden, he stopped a little way off and regarded Hercules with an expression of great contentment. He said, "You cannot imagine how delightful it is to be free. I have carried that sky for ages, and I have no intention of carrying it again. I shall take these apples to your king myself, and you may stay here and hold the sky in my place."

Hercules saw at once how serious his position was, but he took care not to let any alarm show in his face or in his voice. As though the matter hardly concerned him, he replied, "Very well. Only, before you go, would you be good enough to hold the sky for just one minute? I should like to fold my coat into a cushion and lay it on my shoulders, so that the weight will not press so hard upon them." The request sounded perfectly reasonable, and Atlas, who was a great deal stronger than he was clever, suspected nothing at all. He laid the golden apples on the ground and took the sky back onto his own broad shoulders, exactly as he had carried it for so long.

That was all Hercules had been waiting for. He gathered up the three golden apples, thanked the giant politely for his trouble, and bade him a cheerful goodbye. Then he turned his back on the end of the world and walked away, and Atlas, with the sky once more upon his shoulders, could do nothing whatever to prevent him. As far as anyone knows, the giant is standing there still, holding up the sky over all our heads.
