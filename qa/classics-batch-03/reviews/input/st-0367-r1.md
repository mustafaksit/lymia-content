# Clod Hans

Summary: Two clever brothers try to win a princess with words, but their simple younger brother follows on a goat.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): goat, crow, clog, princess

## Level A1

Three brothers lived with their old father. Two of them knew a lot. One knew every word in a big book. The other knew all the laws. The last one was Clod Hans. He did not know much at all.

One day there was news from the princess. She wanted to marry the best talker. The two older ones wanted to go. Their father gave each of them a horse. One was black, and one was white.

"I want to go too!" said Clod Hans. "No horse for you," said his father. "I can take my goat!" said Clod Hans. He got on the goat. Then he went after the other two.

On the way, he found an old crow. Soon he was next to the other two. "This is for the princess!" he said. They laughed at him. Then Clod Hans found an old clog. "And this is for her too!" he said. Next he found some mud. He took it with him.

Many men waited at the princess's home. Her room was very hot. The first brother came in. He did not know what to say. "Go away!" said the princess. The second brother came in. He did not know what to say either. "Go away!" said the princess again.

Then Clod Hans came in on his goat. "It is so hot here!" he said. "Yes, because my father cooks chickens," she said. "Then cook my crow too!" said Clod Hans. "But I have no pot," said the princess. "I have one!" said Clod Hans. He put the crow in the old clog. "What can we put on top?" she asked. Clod Hans took out the mud. He put it on the crow.

The princess laughed. "You can talk and answer!" she said. "I want to marry you." So Clod Hans won the princess. Later he became king.

## Level A2

A father and his three sons lived in an old house. The two older sons were very clever. The first one knew every word in a very big book. He also knew three years of the town newspaper by heart. The second one knew all the laws of the town. The youngest son was Clod Hans, and nobody thought much of him.

One day the princess sent news to every town. She wanted to marry the man with the best words. "He must speak well for himself," she said. The two older brothers wanted to try. Their father gave each of them a horse. One horse was black, and the other was white.

Clod Hans saw them leave in their best clothes. "Where are you going?" he asked. "To the princess," said the first brother. "We want to talk our way to her." "Then I want to go too!" said Clod Hans. His brothers laughed and rode away. Clod Hans asked his father for a horse. "You get no horse," said his father. "Then I can take my goat," said Clod Hans. "He is mine, and he can carry me." So he followed his brothers on the goat.

The two brothers went on ahead. They thought hard about what to say. Soon Clod Hans caught up with them. "Look, I found an old crow!" he called. "What do you want with that?" asked the second brother. "It is a present for the princess," said Clod Hans. His brothers laughed and went on.

A little later, Clod Hans found an old clog. "This is for the princess too," he said. Then he filled his pockets with mud. "The princess can have the best mud in the land!" he said.

The brothers got to the town first. Many men came to see the princess. They stood in long lines, and each got a number. Inside, the princess sat in a very hot room. The first brother came in. "It is very hot in here," he said. "Yes, because my father cooks young chickens today," said the princess. The first brother did not know what to say. "No good!" said the princess. "Go away!" The second brother came in and said almost the same thing. He got the same answer and had nothing to say. So she sent him away too.

Then Clod Hans came in on his goat. "It is so hot in here!" he said. The princess told him about the chickens. "Good!" said Clod Hans. "Then you can cook my crow too." "Of course," said the princess. "But I have no pot." "I have one!" said Clod Hans. He took out the old clog and put the crow in it. "But what can we put over it?" asked the princess. "I have lots in my pocket," said Clod Hans. He took out some mud and put it over the crow.

The princess laughed and laughed. "You can answer, and you can speak for yourself!" she said. "I want you for my husband." So Clod Hans married the princess, and later he became king.

## Level B1

In an old house in the country lived an old man with three sons. Two of the sons were so clever that people expected great things from them. The oldest knew a whole Latin dictionary by heart. He also knew three years of the town newspaper, forwards and backwards. The second son knew every law of the town's trade groups. He could talk about politics, and he could sew pretty patterns too. Everyone called the youngest son Clod Hans, and nobody thought much of him.

One day a drummer went through the land with important news. The princess was going to marry the man who could speak best for himself. The two clever brothers decided to try. Their father gave each of them a fine horse. The oldest got a coal-black horse, and the second got a milk-white one. Before they left, they rubbed a little fish oil on the corners of their mouths. They believed it helped clever words slip out easily.

Clod Hans saw them leaving. "Where are you going in your Sunday clothes?" he asked. "To the palace," said the oldest brother. "We are going to talk our way to the princess." "Then I'm coming too!" said Clod Hans. His brothers laughed at him and rode off. "Father, give me a horse," said Clod Hans. "You get nothing from me," said the old man. "You can't talk, but your brothers are fine young men." "Then I'll take the goat," said Clod Hans. "He belongs to me, and he can carry me well." He climbed on the goat and went racing down the road, singing loudly.

The two brothers rode ahead in silence. They were thinking of all the clever things they wanted to say. Soon Clod Hans caught up with them. "Look what I found on the road!" he shouted. In his hand was an old crow. "What are you going to do with that?" asked the oldest brother. "I'm going to give it to the princess," said Clod Hans. "Yes, do that," said his brothers, laughing, and they rode on.

A little later Clod Hans shouted again. "Look at this! You don't find one of these on the road every day!" It was an old wooden clog with the top part missing. "Is that for the princess too?" asked the second brother. "Of course," said Clod Hans. After that he stopped at a ditch and filled his pockets with the finest mud.

The brothers reached the town an hour before Clod Hans. A crowd of young men was already there, all hoping to win her. They stood in rows, and each man got a number. One by one they went into the hall, and one by one they lost their words. The hall was terribly hot. At every window stood three writers and an old town official. They wrote down every word.

When the oldest brother came in, his whole dictionary went out of his head. "It's very hot in here," he said. "Yes, my father is cooking young chickens today," said the princess. He stood there with his mouth open and could not think of a single word. "No good!" said the princess. "Take him away!" The second brother had no better luck. "It's terribly hot in here," he said. The princess gave him the same answer about the chickens. "What, what?" he said, and that was all. So he had to leave too.

Then Clod Hans rode into the hall on his goat. "It's as hot as an oven in here!" he said. "That's because my father is cooking young chickens," said the princess. "How lucky!" said Clod Hans. "Then I can cook my crow." "Of course," said the princess. "But do you have anything to cook it in? I have no pot or pan." "I do!" said Clod Hans, and he put the crow into the old clog. "That's a whole meal," said the princess. "But where will we get the sauce?" "I have plenty in my pocket," said Clod Hans. He poured some mud over the crow.

The princess laughed. "I like you!" she said. "You can answer, and you can speak for yourself. I will marry you. Those men write down every word we say. Tomorrow it will all be in the newspaper. The old official is the worst, because he understands nothing." She said this to frighten him, but Clod Hans was not afraid at all. "Then the old official gets the best present," he said. He tossed his last bit of mud, and it landed on the old man's face. "Well done!" said the princess. "I couldn't do that myself, but I will learn."

So Clod Hans married the princess, wore a crown and sat on a throne. We know this from the old official's newspaper, but that newspaper is not always true.

## Level B2

Far out in the country stood an old manor house, and in it lived an old squire with his three sons. Two of those sons were so clever that they had more brains than they knew what to do with. The elder had learned the entire Latin dictionary by heart, along with three years' worth of the town newspaper. He could recite all of it forwards or backwards, whichever his listeners preferred. The younger had studied every rule of the town's trade guilds, the sort of thing an important town official ought to know. He could also discuss politics, and he was skilled at sewing fine patterns onto braces. The third son was known as Clod Hans, and nobody in the family really counted him as a brother at all.

One day it was announced throughout the land that the princess wanted a husband. She would marry whichever man could speak up best for himself. The two clever brothers were sure that the prize belonged to one of them. Their father gave the dictionary brother a coal-black horse and the guild expert a milk-white one. Before setting off, they rubbed fish oil into the corners of their mouths to make their words flow smoothly. All the servants gathered in the yard to watch them ride away.

At that moment Clod Hans came along and stared at his brothers in their Sunday best. "Where are you off to, dressed up like that?" he asked. "To court, to talk our way into the princess's heart," replied the elder brother. "Haven't you heard the drummer going all over the country?" "Then I'm coming too!" cried Clod Hans. His brothers burst out laughing and rode off.

"Father, let me have a horse," said Clod Hans. "I feel like getting married as well." "Nonsense," said the old squire. "I won't give you a horse. You don't know how to talk, while your brothers are splendid fellows." "If I can't have a horse, I'll take the billy goat," said Clod Hans. "He belongs to me, and he can carry me perfectly well." He sat astride the goat, dug in his heels and went charging down the road, singing at the top of his voice.

The brothers rode on ahead without a word, each of them going over the brilliant remarks he planned to make. Soon they heard Clod Hans behind them. "Look what I found on the road!" he called, holding up an old crow. "You fool, what do you want with that?" said the elder brother. "I'm going to give it to the princess," said Clod Hans. "Yes, you do that," they said, and they laughed and rode on.

A little later he called out again. "Look what I've found now! You don't come across a thing like this every day." It was an old wooden clog with the upper part missing. "Is that for the princess too?" asked the younger brother. "It certainly is," said Clod Hans. The next time they looked back, he was filling his pockets with mud from a ditch. "This is the very finest kind of mud," he told them happily.

The brothers reached the town gate a whole hour before Clod Hans. Each suitor was given a number on arrival. They were lined up in rows of six, packed so tightly that nobody could move his arms. The rest of the townspeople crowded around the palace and pressed against the windows to watch the princess receive her suitors. As each young man entered the hall, all his fine words seemed to vanish. The hall was terribly hot. At every window stood three clerks and an old alderman. They wrote down every word that was said, so that it could be printed in the next day's paper.

When the elder brother stepped forward, the whole dictionary had gone clean out of his head. "It's terribly hot in here," he said. "That's because my father is roasting young chickens today," said the princess. He stood there with his mouth open, unable to think of a single clever thing. "No good!" said the princess. "Take him away!" The younger brother fared no better. "It's dreadfully hot in here," he said. "Yes, we're roasting young chickens today," the princess replied. "What, what?" he stammered, and that was the end of him. "No good!" said the princess, and he was led out as well.

Then in came Clod Hans, riding his goat straight into the hall. "It's as hot as a furnace in here!" he announced. "That's because my father is roasting young chickens," said the princess. "What luck!" said Clod Hans. "Then I suppose I could get a crow roasted?" "With pleasure," said the princess, "but have you anything to roast it in? I have neither pot nor pan." "I certainly have," said Clod Hans, and he produced the old clog and set the crow inside it. "That's enough for a whole meal," said the princess. "But where shall we find some gravy?" "I've got a pocketful," said Clod Hans, and he poured some mud over the crow.

The princess burst out laughing. "I like that!" she said. "You can answer, and you can speak up for yourself, so I'll have you for my husband. But do you realize that every word we say is being written down and will appear in tomorrow's paper? Look at each window: three clerks and an old alderman, and the alderman is the worst, because he understands nothing." She said this only to frighten him. The clerks giggled, and a few drops of ink fell onto the floor. "So those are the gentlemen," said Clod Hans. "In that case, the alderman shall have the best of it." He turned out his pocket and tossed the rest of the mud at the alderman, where it landed on his face. "That was neatly done," said the princess. "I could never have done it myself, but I shall learn."

And so Clod Hans became king, with a wife, a crown and a throne of his own. We have this straight from the alderman's newspaper, though it must be said that the paper cannot always be trusted.

## Level C1

Deep in the countryside stood an old manor house, home to an old squire whose two sons were so clever that half their cleverness would have been plenty. Both had set their hearts on the princess, for she had let it be known that she would marry whichever man could best speak up for himself. Neither doubted his chances, since their heads were already crammed with learning that was bound to come in useful. The elder had memorized the entire Latin dictionary, together with three full years of the town newspaper, and could recite the lot forwards or backwards, as his audience pleased. The younger, for his part, had mastered every statute of the trade guilds, precisely the sort of knowledge an alderman is expected to carry about with him. He was also able to hold forth on matters of state, and he had a delicate hand for sewing patterns onto braces, being refined in taste as well as quick in mind.

"I shall win her," said each of them, and their father gave each a handsome horse: a coal-black one for the dictionary brother, a milk-white one for the guild expert. Then they rubbed the corners of their mouths with fish oil, so that their words might slide out more smoothly. The servants, meanwhile, crowded into the yard to watch them mount. It was at this moment that the third brother appeared, for there were three of them after all, though nobody had ever really counted the third one. Everyone called him Clod Hans.

"Where are you two off to in your Sunday best?" he asked, gazing at them with open curiosity. "To court, of course, to talk our way into the princess's favor," replied the elder, "or haven't you heard what the drummer has been announcing all over the country?" "Goodness, then I had better come along too!" cried Clod Hans, at which his brothers laughed out loud and rode away without another word. "Father, let me have a horse," Clod Hans pleaded, "for I've taken a sudden fancy to getting married, and if she'll have me, she'll have me." "What nonsense," said the old squire, who had no intention of wasting a horse on a son with nothing to say for himself, while the other two were such splendid fellows. "Very well," said Clod Hans, quite undismayed, "then I'll ride the billy goat, which belongs to me and can carry me perfectly well." And so he climbed astride the goat, dug his heels into its sides, and went careering down the high road, bellowing a song as loudly as his lungs allowed.

Up ahead, the two brothers rode in dignified silence, each rehearsing the dazzling remarks with which he meant to astonish the princess. Their thoughts were interrupted by a cry from behind, and there came Clod Hans, bouncing along on his goat and holding something aloft as though it were a great treasure. "Look what I found lying in the road!" he shouted, and in his hand was an old crow. "You fool," said the elder brother, wrinkling his nose, "what on earth do you mean to do with that?" "Why, I'll give it to the princess," said Clod Hans, and his brothers, laughing, told him to go ahead and do exactly that, before spurring their horses on.

It was not long before Clod Hans called out once more, waving a fresh discovery over his head with even greater delight. "Look what I've found now, a thing you certainly don't come across on the road every day!" he cried. It turned out to be an old wooden clog, the upper part of which had long since gone missing. "Is the princess to have that as well?" the younger brother asked, and Clod Hans assured him that she most certainly was. The brothers laughed again and rode on, but when they next glanced back, he was leaning over a ditch and stuffing his pockets with mud. It was, he declared with great satisfaction, the very finest kind.

The brothers reached the town gate a full hour ahead of him. There the suitors were being handed numbers and arranged in rows of six, squeezed together so tightly that nobody could lift an arm. The townsfolk, for their part, had gathered around the palace and were pressing their faces against the windows, eager to watch the princess receive her suitors. Yet no sooner did a young man set foot in the hall than his eloquence deserted him entirely, and one after another they were sent away. The room was stiflingly hot, and at every window stood three clerks and an old alderman, recording each word so that it might appear in print the following morning.

When the elder brother's turn came, he discovered, to his dismay, that the whole dictionary had flown clean out of his head. "It's terribly hot in here," he managed to say. "That is because my father is roasting young chickens today," the princess replied, and he could find nothing whatever to add, though he had meant to say something brilliant. "No good!" said the princess, waving him away. The younger brother fared no better, for all his guild statutes and his politics. "It's dreadfully hot in here," he remarked. When the princess explained that they were roasting young chickens, all he could produce was a feeble "What, what?" before he, too, was shown the door.

Just then Clod Hans came riding into the hall on his goat, as calmly as if he were trotting into his own barnyard. "Well, it's as hot as a furnace in here!" he exclaimed. "That is because my father is roasting young chickens," said the princess, watching him with interest. "How very convenient," said Clod Hans, "for in that case I suppose I might get a crow roasted along with them?" "With the greatest pleasure," said the princess, "but have you anything to roast it in, since I have neither pot nor pan to offer you?" "Indeed I have," said Clod Hans, and he produced the old clog with a flourish and settled the crow inside it. "Why, that is a whole meal," said the princess, "but where are we to find gravy to pour over it?" "I have a pocketful," said Clod Hans, "and enough to spare," and he poured a little of the mud over the crow.

The princess burst out laughing, delighted in a way that none of the polished speakers had managed to delight her. "I like that," she said, "for you have an answer ready, and you can speak up for yourself, so you are the husband for me. But are you aware that every word we say is being taken down and will be in the paper tomorrow? At each window, you see, stand three clerks and an old alderman, and the alderman is the worst of the lot, because he understands nothing at all." She said this purely to unsettle him, and the clerks, enjoying the joke, giggled so much that several drops of ink spattered onto the floor. "So those are the fine gentlemen," said Clod Hans, not in the least alarmed, "in which case the alderman had better have the best of what I've brought." He turned out his pocket and tossed the rest of the mud at the alderman, and it landed squarely on the old man's face. "Neatly done," said the princess, "for I could never have managed that myself, though I dare say I shall learn."

So it was that Clod Hans became king, with a wife, a crown and a throne to sit upon. All of this comes to us straight from the alderman's own newspaper, which, it must be admitted, is not a paper whose word can always be relied upon.
