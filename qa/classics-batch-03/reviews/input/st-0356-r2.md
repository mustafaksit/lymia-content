# The Clock That Went Backward

Summary: Two cousins inherit a strange old clock whose hands move backward, and it hides a secret.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): clock, time, siege

## Level A1

Harry and Robert were cousins. As boys, they often visited Aunt Gertrude. Her old house had a very old clock. The clock came from Holland. Its hands moved back, not forward.

Every night, she turned a key in it. "Never let this clock stop," she said. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died. She left the clock to Harry and Robert.

Then the cousins went to Leiden, in Holland. The clock went with them. Their teacher was Professor Van Stopp. One evening, he came to their room. He saw the clock, and his face changed. "I know this clock," said Professor Van Stopp. "I read about it in an old book. It can take people back in time."

"Back to what year?" asked Robert. "To fifteen seventy-four," said Professor Van Stopp. "That year, Leiden was under siege. Many men from another country stood around it. No food came into the city. The people were very hungry and tired. But the leader of the city was strong."

All three put their hands on the clock. Professor Van Stopp turned the hands back. Everything went black. Then they stood in old Leiden. The clock stood next to them. "Wait here," said Professor Van Stopp. "To go home, turn the hands forward." Then he walked away into the city.

Harry and Robert waited by the clock. Soon they heard a lot of people. Harry and Robert went to look. Hungry people stood near a big house. "Please give up!" said the people. Then the leader of the city came out. "No!" said the leader. "We must not give up. Our friends come soon with food." Harry looked at the leader's face. It was the face of Professor Van Stopp!

"It is our teacher!" said Robert. They ran back to the clock. Harry turned the hands forward. Then they were back in their room. Professor Van Stopp was not there. Only the clock stood in the corner. "The clock was always his," said Harry.

## Level A2

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up together. As boys, they spent every summer with their Aunt Gertrude. She lived alone in a big old house. In her front room stood a very old clock. It came from Holland many years ago. But this clock was different from all other clocks. Its hands did not move forward. They moved back.

Later, the cousins went to study in Leiden, in Holland. They took the clock with them and put it in their room. Their teacher there was Professor Van Stopp, a kind old man. One evening, he came to visit them. When he saw the clock, his face changed. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I read about it in a very old book. A man in Leiden made it long ago. It can take people back in time."

"Back to what year?" asked Robert. "To fifteen seventy-four," said the old teacher. "In that year, Leiden was under siege. Many men from another country stood around the city for months. Nothing could come into the city, not even food. The people of Leiden became very hungry and tired. But the leader of the city did not give up. Do you want to see it?"

Harry and Robert said yes. The three of them put their hands on the clock. Professor Van Stopp turned the hands back, again and again. The room went black. Then the black went away, and they stood in an old street. The clock stood next to them. "Wait here with the clock," said Professor Van Stopp. "To go home, turn its hands forward." Then he walked away down the street.

The city was very still. No children played in the streets. The cousins waited, but their teacher did not come back. After a while, they heard many voices. They followed the sound to a large house. Many hungry, tired people stood in front of it. "Please give up the city!" the people said. "We have no food!"

Then the leader of the city came out of the house. "No," the leader said. "We must not give up. Our friends come soon with food. Wait a little longer." The people listened to him and went back to their homes. Harry looked at the leader's face, and he could not believe it. It was the face of Professor Van Stopp!

Robert saw it too. "It is our teacher!" said Robert. "Yes," said Harry. "And the clock is his." The cousins ran back to the old street. They put their hands on the clock. Harry turned the hands forward. The street went black, and then they were back in their room. Professor Van Stopp was not there. Only the old clock stood there, and its hands moved back.

## Level B1

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up together. As boys, they spent every summer with their Aunt Gertrude. She was a quiet woman who lived alone in a large old house. In her front room stood a very old clock that came from Holland long ago. The boys soon noticed something strange about it. Its hands never moved forward like the hands of other clocks; they moved back.

Aunt Gertrude cared for the clock the way a mother cares for a child. Every night she opened a little door in its side and turned a small key. "You must never let this clock stop," she told the boys more than once. Harry and Robert did not understand her, but they remembered her words. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died quietly. She left the old clock to Harry and Robert.

When they were older, the cousins went to Holland to study at the university in Leiden. They took the clock with them and put it in the corner of their room. There it went on ticking backward, just as before. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a kind old man who loved history. One evening he came to visit them. When he saw the clock, his face went pale. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I read about it in a very old book in the university library. A clockmaker of Leiden built it long ago, in the hardest years of this city. It does more than count time backward. It can carry people back in time."

"Back to which year?" asked Robert, who did not believe a word. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor answered. "That was the year of the great siege. An army from another country stood around Leiden for months. Nothing could come in, not even food. The people were very hungry and tired, and many of them wanted to give up. My book says that the brave leader of the city kept their hope alive. Will you come with me and see it?"

Harry and Robert looked at each other, and then they agreed. The three of them put their hands on the old clock. Then the professor turned its hands back, again and again. The room grew dark, and the ticking became louder and louder. Then the darkness went away, and they were standing in a narrow street of old houses. The clock stood next to them on the stones. "Stay here with the clock," the professor told them. "When you want to go home, turn its hands forward." Before they could ask him anything, he walked away down the street.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four, and the city was terribly still. No children were playing, nobody was selling anything, and the people looked thin and tired. The cousins waited for a long time, but the professor did not come back. Then they heard many voices from the center of the city. They followed the sound and came to a large house with wide steps. A crowd of hungry people was standing in front of it. "We have no food!" the people were shouting. "Please give up the city!"

At that moment, the leader of the city came out onto the steps. He was a tall old man with a tired face. He spoke calmly to the crowd. "I know that you are hungry," he said. "I am hungry too. But we will not give up. Our friends will come soon with food. Wait a little longer, and Leiden will be free." The crowd grew quiet, and slowly the people went back to their homes. Harry looked up at the leader's face, and suddenly he could not breathe. It was the face of Professor Van Stopp.

"Robert," Harry said quietly, "look at him." Robert looked, and his face went white. "It is our teacher," he said. "But how?" Harry thought of the old book, the clock from Leiden and the professor's pale face. "The clock was never really ours," Harry said. "It is his." The cousins hurried back to the narrow street, where the clock was still ticking. They put their hands on it, and Harry turned the hands forward. The street went dark, and a moment later they were back in their room. Professor Van Stopp was not there. Only the old clock stood in its corner, ticking backward as always.

## Level B2

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up almost like brothers. Every summer of their boyhood was spent at the house of their Aunt Gertrude. She was a quiet, serious woman who lived alone among old furniture and older memories. In her front room stood a tall clock of dark wood, which her family had brought from Holland generations before. The boys found it fascinating, because it did something no other clock in the world seemed to do. Its hands did not move forward around the face; they moved slowly and steadily backward. Sometimes the boys found their aunt sitting in front of it for an hour, silently watching the hands creep back.

Aunt Gertrude treated the clock with a care that bordered on love. Every night before bed, she opened a small door in its side and turned a little brass key. "Whatever happens, never let this clock stop," she told the boys more than once. Neither Harry nor Robert understood what she meant, but neither of them forgot it. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died peacefully, and in her will she left the old clock to her two nephews.

When they were old enough, the cousins sailed to Holland to study at the famous university in Leiden. They brought the clock with them and set it up in a corner of their room. There it went on ticking backward, exactly as before. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a gentle old scholar who seemed to know everything about the history of the city. One evening he came to their room for a cup of tea. The moment he saw the clock, the color left his face. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I have read about it in a very old book in the university library. It was built by a clockmaker of Leiden in the darkest years this city has ever known." He laid a hand on its wooden case almost tenderly. "Most people think of time as a road that runs in only one direction. This clock does not simply count the hours backward; it can carry people back along that road."

"Back to when?" Robert asked. He clearly did not believe a word of it. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor replied. "That was the year of the great siege. For months an army from another country stood around Leiden, and nothing could get in, not even food. The people were desperately hungry and tired, and many of them begged to give up. According to my book, it was the city's brave leader who kept their hope alive." The professor turned to the two young men. "Would you like to see it with your own eyes?"

Harry and Robert exchanged a long look, and then they both nodded. The three of them placed their hands on the old clock, and Professor Van Stopp turned its hands back, around and around. The room grew dark, and the ticking became so loud that it seemed to fill the whole world. Then the darkness lifted, and they found themselves in a narrow street of old brick houses. Robert touched the wall of the nearest house, as if to make sure it was real. The clock stood next to them on the cobblestones, ticking quietly. "Stay here with the clock," the professor told them. "When you wish to go home, simply turn its hands forward." Before either of them could ask a question, he walked quickly down the street and disappeared around a corner.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four, and the city was terribly still. No children were playing, no one was selling anything in the market, and the people who passed looked thin and exhausted. The cousins waited by the clock for a long time, but their teacher did not return. At last, they heard the sound of many voices from the center of the city. They followed it to a square in front of a large house, where a crowd of hungry people had gathered. "We have no food left!" the people were crying. "Please give up the city!"

Then the door opened, and the mayor, the leader of the city, stepped out onto the steps. He was a tall old man with a tired face, yet he stood straight and spoke calmly. "I know you are hungry," he told the crowd. "I am hungry too, like every one of you. But we will not give up Leiden. Our friends are coming with food, and they will not be long. Hold on a little longer, and this city will be free." Something in his steady voice reached the people, and one by one they went quietly back to their homes. Harry looked up at the mayor's face, and for a moment he forgot to breathe. The kind eyes, the lines around the mouth, the way he tilted his head: it was the face of Professor Van Stopp.

"Robert," Harry whispered, "look at him." Robert looked, and the color left his face. "It is our teacher," he said. "But how can that be?" Harry thought of the old book, the clock built in Leiden in these very years, and the professor's pale face. "The clock was never really ours," Harry said slowly. "It belongs to him. It always did."

The cousins hurried back to the narrow street, where the clock was still ticking on the cobblestones. They laid their hands on it, and Harry turned its hands forward, around and around. The street faded into darkness, and a moment later they were back in their room. Professor Van Stopp was not there, and his cup of tea stood cold on the table. In its corner, the old clock went on ticking backward, as if nothing had happened.

## Level C1

Harry and his cousin Robert were raised almost as brothers, and every summer of their boyhood was spent in the rambling old house of their Aunt Gertrude. She was a reserved, serious woman who lived alone among heavy furniture and heavier memories, and she rarely spoke unless there was something worth saying. In her front room stood a tall clock of dark, polished wood, which her family had carried over from Holland so many generations before that nobody remembered exactly when. To the two boys it was the most fascinating object in the house, for it did something that no other clock in the world appeared to do. Its hands did not travel forward around the face, as every honest clock's hands should; they crept slowly, steadily and quite deliberately backward. More than once the boys came upon their aunt sitting before it in the half-light, her hands folded in her lap, watching the minutes return to wherever minutes come from.

Aunt Gertrude tended the clock with a devotion that bordered on love. Each night, just before she climbed the stairs to bed, she opened a little door in its side and turned a small brass key that she kept on a ribbon. "Whatever else happens, never let this clock stop," she told the boys on more than one occasion, without ever explaining why. Neither Harry nor Robert understood what she meant, yet neither of them could quite forget the strange urgency in her voice. Many years later Aunt Gertrude died peacefully, and her will, to the surprise of the rest of the family, left the old clock to her two nephews.

When they were old enough, the cousins sailed to Holland to study at the ancient university of Leiden, and naturally the clock went with them. They set it up in a corner of their lodgings, where it resumed its backward ticking as calmly as if it had never left home. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a gentle, absent-minded scholar who seemed to carry the whole history of the city in his head. One evening he climbed the stairs to their room for a cup of tea, and the instant his eyes fell on the clock, the color drained from his face. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice, as though afraid of waking it. "I have read of it in a very old book in the university library, a book few people have ever bothered to open. It was built by a clockmaker of Leiden during the darkest years this city has ever known." He laid a hand on its wooden case, almost tenderly. "Most people imagine time as a road that runs in only one direction, from yesterday to tomorrow. This clock does not merely count the hours backward, my young friends; it can carry those who touch it back along that road."

"Back to when, exactly?" Robert asked. His tone made it plain that he did not believe a single word. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor replied, quite unoffended. "To the year of the great siege. For months an army from another country surrounded Leiden, and nothing could pass through its lines, not even a loaf of bread. The people grew desperately hungry and tired, and more than once they begged their leaders to give the city up. According to my book, it was the leader of the city, standing firm when everyone else wavered, who kept their hope alive." He looked from one cousin to the other, and his eyes were strangely bright. "Would you care to see it for yourselves?"

Harry and Robert exchanged a long glance, in which curiosity slowly won out over doubt, and then they both nodded. The three of them placed their hands on the old clock, and Professor Van Stopp began to turn its hands back, around and around, faster and faster. The room grew dark, and the ticking swelled until it seemed to fill not only their ears but the whole of the world. Then, as suddenly as it had come, the darkness lifted, and they found themselves standing in a narrow street of tall brick houses. The clock stood beside them on the cobblestones, ticking away as quietly and patiently as ever. Robert reached out and pressed his palm against the rough wall of the nearest house, as if the bricks might dissolve at his touch. "Stay here and look after the clock," the professor said, with a curious eagerness in his voice. "When you wish to go home, you need only turn its hands forward." Before either cousin could ask what he meant to do, he was striding away down the street, and a moment later the corner had swallowed him.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four, and the silence of the city was the first thing they noticed. No children played in the lanes, no one called out prices in the market, and the few people who passed by were thin, pale and visibly exhausted. The cousins waited beside the clock for what felt like hours, but their teacher did not return. At last a murmur of many voices rose from somewhere near the heart of the city, and, unable to bear the waiting any longer, they followed it. It led them to a square before a large house, where a crowd of hungry men and women had gathered, their faces drawn and their patience at an end. "We have nothing left to eat!" someone cried, and the cry passed from mouth to mouth. "Give up the city, and let us live!"

At that moment the door of the house opened, and the mayor, the leader of the city, came out onto the steps. He was a tall old man, worn thin like everyone else, yet he held himself as straight as a mast and spoke without raising his voice. "I know that you are hungry," he said. "So am I, and so is every soul within these walls. But Leiden will not give up. Our friends are on their way with food, and they will not be long. Hold on a little longer, and this city will be free." There was no anger in his words, only a steady, unshakable certainty, and something in it reached the crowd. One by one the people fell silent, and then, slowly, they turned and went back to their homes. Harry, standing at the edge of the square, looked up at the mayor's face and felt the ground shift beneath him. The kind eyes, the deep lines around the mouth, the slight tilt of the head as he listened: it was, beyond any doubt, the face of Professor Van Stopp.

"Robert," Harry whispered, gripping his cousin's arm, "look at him." Robert looked, and the color drained from his face exactly as the professor's had when he first saw the clock. "It is our teacher," Robert breathed. "But how can that be?" Harry thought of the forgotten book, of the clock built in Leiden in these very years, and of Aunt Gertrude turning its key night after night without ever explaining why. "The clock was never really ours," he said slowly. "It belongs to him, and perhaps it always has."

They hurried back through the silent streets to the narrow lane, where the clock was still ticking faithfully on the cobblestones. Together they laid their hands on its case, and Harry turned the hands forward, around and around, until the street dissolved into darkness. A moment later they were standing in their own room again, blinking in the lamplight. Professor Van Stopp was not there; his cup of tea stood cold and half finished on the table, as though he had stepped out only for a moment. In its corner the old clock went on ticking backward, calm and unhurried, keeping the time of a city three hundred years away.
