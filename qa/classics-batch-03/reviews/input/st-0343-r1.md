# Little Lord Fauntleroy

Summary: A kind boy from New York learns that his cold grandfather is a rich English earl.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): earl, castle, heir

## Level A1

Cedric lived in New York with his mother. He called her Dearest. His father died years ago. They did not have much money. Cedric had two good friends. Mr Hobbs had a small shop. The other friend was a poor young man.

One day a lawyer came to their house. "The Earl is your grandfather," he told Cedric. The Earl lived far away in a castle. All the Earl's sons died. Cedric's father was one of them. So Cedric was the heir to the castle.

The Earl also sent money for Cedric. Cedric gave it to his young friend. Then Cedric and Dearest left New York.

The Earl was a hard man. He did not like Dearest. He wanted Cedric, but not her. She got a small house near the castle. Dearest did not tell Cedric the reason.

At the castle, Cedric met the Earl. "Thank you for the money. You are a very good man," said Cedric. The Earl was not a good man. But he liked Cedric very much.

Cedric believed in the Earl. So the Earl began to change. He helped the people on his land. He built new houses for them. Now the people loved the Earl too.

Then a woman came to the lawyer. "I married the Earl's first son. My child is the real heir!" she said. "Can I still be your boy?" asked Cedric. "Yes, always," said the Earl.

Cedric's young friend was still in New York. There he saw a newspaper. It had a picture of the woman. He knew her face! She was his brother's wife. Her child was not from the Earl's family. So her story was not true. He and his brother went to the Earl. The woman saw them and left at once. Her child went home with his real father.

Then the Earl went to Dearest's small house. "I was wrong. Please live in the castle," said the Earl. Dearest smiled and came. Now Cedric, Dearest and the Earl lived together.

## Level B2

In a modest house on a quiet street in New York, a boy named Cedric Errol lived with his mother. His father, an Englishman who had once been a soldier, had died when Cedric was too young to remember him. Cedric called his mother Dearest, because that was the name his father had always used for her. Money was scarce in their home, but love and laughter were not. At seven, Cedric was a friendly, curious boy who would talk with anyone at all. His closest friend was Mr Hobbs, who kept a shop on the corner and had firm opinions about everything. Another friend was a poor young man who earned his living by cleaning shoes on the street.

One afternoon, a thin, careful old lawyer arrived at their door with remarkable news. He had been sent from England by the Earl of Dorincourt, who was Cedric's grandfather. The Earl had three sons, and Cedric's father had been the youngest of them. When that son married an American woman, the Earl had been so furious that he never spoke to him again. Now the two older sons had died as well, leaving no children behind. "That makes you the heir, young man. One day you will be the Earl of Dorincourt, and until then you will be known as Lord Fauntleroy," the lawyer explained. Cedric listened with a troubled face, because he had no wish to leave his friends.

The Earl had given the lawyer money for the boy, and Cedric knew exactly what to do with it. He made sure that the young shoe cleaner had enough to set up his own small business. He helped a family whose father was ill and could not work. For Mr Hobbs he chose a gold watch, so that his old friend would not forget him. The lawyer, who had expected a spoiled child, found himself deeply impressed. He began to suspect that the proud Earl was about to meet his match.

When mother and son reached England, they did not travel to the castle together. The Earl still disliked Dearest, and he had refused to let her live under his roof. Instead, she was given a small house near the castle gates. Dearest asked the lawyer not to explain the reason to Cedric. "If he knew how his grandfather felt about me, he could never love him. I want him to love him, and I think it will do them both good," she said.

The Earl received his grandson in a great, gloomy library. He was sitting in an enormous chair with one painful foot resting on a stool, and he was in a terrible temper. A huge dog lay beside him, and most visitors were frightened of both of them. Cedric, however, walked straight up to the dog and laid a gentle hand on its head. Then he looked at his grandfather with clear, honest eyes. "Thank you for the money you gave me. It helped my friends and a lot of other people, so you must be a very kind man," he said warmly. Nobody had ever called the Earl kind before, and for once he could find nothing to say.

In the weeks that followed, the old man watched his grandson with growing interest. Cedric believed completely that his grandfather was generous and good, and he told everyone so. Little by little, the Earl began to want to deserve that trust. When a poor farmer could not pay his rent, Cedric asked for mercy, and the Earl allowed the family to stay. He gave Cedric a pony and watched with secret pride as the boy learned to ride. Later he even ordered new houses to be built for the poorest families in the village. The people of Dorincourt, who had feared the Earl for years, began to speak of him with respect.

Then, one morning, the lawyer appeared at the castle looking deeply worried. A woman had come to his office with a little boy about Cedric's age. She claimed that she had been secretly married to the Earl's oldest son. "This child is his," she had told the lawyer, "so he is the true heir." The Earl did not believe a word of it, but he could not prove that she was lying. He was angry, and more unhappy than he had ever been, because he had come to love Cedric deeply. When Cedric was told, he was quiet for a while, and then he asked a single question. "Can I still be your boy, even if I am not going to be an earl?" "You will be my boy as long as I live," said the Earl.

Far away in New York, the young shoe cleaner was reading a newspaper in Mr Hobbs's shop when something caught his eye. The paper told the strange story of the English heir, and next to it was a picture of the boy's mother. He stared at it for a long moment and then jumped to his feet. "I know that woman! She was married to my brother!" he shouted to Mr Hobbs. Years earlier, the woman had left his brother and taken their small son with her. The child she was presenting as the Earl's grandson was therefore his own nephew.

The young man and Mr Hobbs wrote to the lawyer without delay. Soon afterwards the two brothers crossed the ocean, and Mr Hobbs insisted on coming too. When the woman found herself face to face with the husband she had left, she had nothing left to say. She hurried away, and her boy went home with his real father. Cedric was the heir once more, and the whole village rejoiced.

Soon after, the Earl drove to the little house near the gates to see Dearest himself. "I was wrong about you," he admitted, and it was not easy for him to say. "Will you come and live with us at the castle?" the Earl asked. Dearest smiled and agreed. On Cedric's eighth birthday the castle held a great party, and every family on the estate was invited. Mr Hobbs enjoyed himself so much that he decided to stay in England for good. As for the Earl, the lonely old man in the dark library had disappeared, and a grandfather had taken his place.

## Level C1

In a modest house on a quiet New York street, a boy named Cedric Errol lived with his mother, and the two of them were, by any reasonable measure, perfectly content. His father, an Englishman who had once worn a soldier's uniform, had died while Cedric was still too young to keep any memory of him. It was from that vanished father that Cedric had borrowed the name he used for his mother, Dearest, and he used it with the same tenderness. There was never much money in the house, yet nobody who stepped through its door would have called it a poor home. At seven, Cedric was a cheerful, trusting child who addressed strangers as if they were old acquaintances, and most of them were charmed. His dearest friend was Mr Hobbs, who kept a shop on the corner and held strong opinions on every subject, especially on the faults of earls and lords across the sea. Another friend was a poor young man who earned a hard living by cleaning shoes on the street, and who thought Cedric the finest boy in the city.

One afternoon, a thin, precise old lawyer appeared at the door with news that would turn their small world upside down. He had crossed the ocean on behalf of the Earl of Dorincourt, an old nobleman famous for his pride, his temper and his enormous fortune, who was Cedric's grandfather. The Earl had once had three sons, and Cedric's father had been the youngest and, by all accounts, the best of them. When that son married an American woman, the Earl had flown into such a rage that he had cut him off entirely and never spoken his name again. Now the two elder sons were dead as well, neither of them leaving a child behind. "It follows, young man, that you are the heir," said the lawyer, studying the boy over his spectacles. "One day you will be the Earl of Dorincourt," he went on, "and in the meantime you will bear the title of Lord Fauntleroy." Cedric received this announcement not with delight but with dismay, for it meant leaving Mr Hobbs, the young shoe cleaner and the only street he had ever known.

The Earl had placed a sum of money in the lawyer's hands for the boy's use, presumably expecting it to vanish on toys and sweets. Cedric, however, had other ideas, and he set about spending it with the seriousness of a small banker. He arranged for the young shoe cleaner to buy his own business, so that he would no longer have to share his earnings with anyone. He sent help to a family whose father had fallen ill, and he remembered several other neighbors whose troubles he had quietly noticed. For Mr Hobbs he chose a gold watch, hoping that every glance at it would remind his old friend of him. The lawyer, who had come prepared to meet a spoiled, ill-mannered child, watched all this with growing astonishment and a private smile.

On their arrival in England, mother and son did not go up to the castle side by side. The Earl's hostility toward Dearest had not softened in the least, and he had refused outright to receive her under his roof. She was to live instead in a small house near the castle gates, within reach of her son but shut out of his new life. Before they parted, Dearest asked the lawyer to keep the reason from Cedric. "If he understood how his grandfather feels about me, it would poison his heart against him," she said quietly. "I would rather he learned to love the old man," she added, "because I believe such love may do them both more good than anything else."

The Earl received his grandson in a vast, shadowy library, seated in an enormous chair with one aching foot propped on a stool and his temper at its very worst. Stretched out beside him lay a huge dog, and between the two of them most visitors lost their courage before they had crossed the room. Cedric, to the old man's surprise, walked straight up to the dog, rested a small hand on its great head, and then turned to his grandfather with a frank, untroubled gaze. "I want to thank you for the money. It helped my friends and so many other people who needed it. You must be the kindest man in the world," he said. It was, the Earl reflected, a remarkable thing to be told, since in all his long and selfish life nobody had ever dreamed of calling him kind.

Over the following weeks the old nobleman found himself watching his grandson with an interest that gradually deepened into something very like devotion. Cedric believed, without the faintest shadow of doubt, that his grandfather was generous and good, and he repeated this conviction to anyone who would listen. Little by little, and rather against his own nature, the Earl began to wish that the boy's faith in him were justified. When a struggling farmer could not pay his rent, it was Cedric who pleaded for him, and the Earl, grumbling, allowed the family to keep their home. He gave the boy a pony and, from the castle windows, followed his first wobbling rides with a pride he took care to conceal.

Eventually, prompted by his grandson's innocent questions, he ordered the miserable old cottages in one corner of the village to be pulled down and replaced with clean, sound houses. The people of Dorincourt, who had feared and resented their landlord for decades, were at first bewildered by the change and then deeply grateful. Everyone understood perfectly well whose influence lay behind it, and the small heir was soon greeted in every lane with smiles.

It was at this happy moment that the lawyer arrived at the castle one morning wearing an expression of grave concern. A woman had called at his office with a little boy of about Cedric's age, and she had told an extraordinary story. She claimed to have been secretly married to the Earl's eldest son, and she insisted that her child, not the American boy, was the rightful heir. The Earl dismissed the tale with contempt, yet he could not immediately disprove it, and the law might well be on her side. He was furious, but beneath his fury lay a misery he had never known, for he had come to love Cedric more than his title, his lands or his pride. When the news was gently broken to him, Cedric sat silent for a while before asking the only question that seemed to matter. "Even if I am not going to be an earl, can I still be your boy?" "You will be my boy as long as I live," the Earl replied, and his voice was not quite steady.

Far away in New York, the young shoe cleaner happened to be glancing through a newspaper in Mr Hobbs's shop when a familiar face leapt out at him from the page. The article recounted the strange affair of the English heir, and next to it was printed a picture of the claimant's mother. He stared at the picture for a long moment, then sprang to his feet and pushed the page across the counter. "I know that woman, Mr Hobbs! She was married to my brother!" he cried. Years before, she had abandoned his elder brother and taken their small son with her, and it was that very child she was now presenting as the Earl's grandson.

The young man and Mr Hobbs wasted no time writing to the lawyer, and within weeks the two brothers were crossing the ocean with Mr Hobbs, who refused to be left behind. Confronted with the husband she had deserted, the woman found that her story had nowhere left to stand, and she hurried away without another word. Her boy, who had never been to blame for any of it, went home with his real father. Cedric was the heir once more, and the news ran through the village faster than any messenger could have carried it.

Not long afterwards, the Earl drove down to the little house by the gates and asked to see Dearest. "I was wrong about you," he told her, and those few words cost him more than any fortune he had ever spent. "Will you come and live with us at the castle?" he asked. Dearest, who had never once spoken a bitter word about him to her son, smiled and accepted.

On Cedric's eighth birthday the castle threw open its doors for a great celebration, and every family on the estate came to share in it. Mr Hobbs, who had arrived in England determined to disapprove of everything aristocratic, enjoyed himself so thoroughly that he resolved to remain there for the rest of his days. The bitter old man who had once sat brooding in a dark library was nowhere to be found; in his place stood a grandfather, with his grandson's hand in his.
