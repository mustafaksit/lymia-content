# Prince Ahmed and the Fairy Peri Banou

Summary: Three princes search distant lands for the rarest gift to win the cousin they all love.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): carpet, tube, apple, fairy, arrow

## Level A1

Long ago, a sultan had three sons. Their names were Houssain, Ali and Ahmed. A young girl, Nouronnihar, lived with them. The sultan loved her like his own child. All three young men loved her too. Each of them wanted to marry her.

"Go out into the world," said the sultan. "Bring me back something very special. The best thing wins Nouronnihar."

Houssain went to Bisnagar. There he bought a carpet. It could take people far in a moment. Ali bought a small tube. With it, he could see any person. Ahmed bought an apple. Its smell could make sick people well.

After one year, the three met again. Ali looked through his tube. He saw Nouronnihar. She was in bed, very sick. She could die soon. "Come, sit on my carpet!" said Houssain. The carpet took them home in a moment. Ahmed held the apple near her face. Nouronnihar opened her eyes. Soon she sat up, strong and well again.

"You all helped her," said the sultan. "So now we need a test. Each of you must send an arrow far. The farthest arrow wins."

Houssain's arrow went far. Ali's arrow went past it. Ahmed's arrow went very, very far. Nobody could find it. "I see only two arrows," said the sultan. "Ali wins." So Ali married Nouronnihar. Houssain left the city for a quiet life.

But Ahmed wanted to find his arrow. He walked for a long, long time. At last, he found it far away. Near it, he saw an old door. Ahmed opened the door and went in.

A young woman sat in a great room. "Come in, Ahmed," said the woman. "I am Peri Banou, a fairy. My power brought your arrow here to me." Ahmed loved her at once. Soon, Ahmed and Peri Banou married. They lived together in her great house.

## Level A2

Long ago, a sultan lived in a great city with his sons. Their names were Houssain, Ali and Ahmed. His brother's daughter, Nouronnihar, lived with them too. She was beautiful and kind, and all three brothers loved her. Each brother wanted to marry her.

Their father did not want to choose one son over the others. "Travel to far countries," he said to them. "Find the most special thing in the world. The son with the best present can marry Nouronnihar." The brothers left home together. They agreed to meet again in one year. Then each brother went a different way.

Houssain went to Bisnagar, a great city full of shops. In the market, a man showed him an old carpet. "Sit on this carpet and think of a place," said the man. "It takes you there in a moment." Houssain tried it, and it really worked.

Ali went to a big city in another country. There he bought a small white tube. He looked through it and saw his father at home. Ahmed went to a city far away. In the market, he found a man with an apple. "Its smell makes sick people well again," said the man. Ahmed bought it at once.

After a year, the three brothers met again. Ali said, "Let's look at Nouronnihar." He looked through his tube, and his face went white. Nouronnihar was in bed, and doctors stood around her. She was close to death.

"Come, sit on my carpet!" Houssain called. In a moment, they were in Nouronnihar's room. Ahmed held the apple near her face. She opened her eyes, and soon she sat up, well again.

"The tube showed you her trouble," the sultan said to his sons. "The carpet brought you home, and the apple saved her. All three presents helped. So each of you must send an arrow. The son with the farthest arrow can marry her."

The next day, everyone went out to a wide field. Houssain went first, and his arrow flew far. Ali's arrow flew past his brother's. Then Ahmed sent his arrow up into the air. It flew and flew, and nobody could find it. "Ali's arrow went farthest," said the sultan. So Ali and Nouronnihar married. Houssain felt very bad and went away to live a simple life.

Ahmed did not stay for the party. Instead, he went to look for his arrow. He walked for many hours, far from the city. At last, he found it near some high rocks. There was an old door in the rocks. Ahmed pushed the door, and it opened.

He went down into a large and lovely room. A young woman sat there with many people around her. "Welcome, Ahmed," she said with a smile. "I am Peri Banou, a fairy. I carried your arrow here because I wanted to meet you." Ahmed looked at her and loved her at once. Peri Banou asked him to stay and be her husband. So Ahmed and the fairy married, and they lived happily together.

## Level B1

Long ago, a great sultan lived in a fine palace with his three sons. Their names were Houssain, Ali and Ahmed. Nouronnihar, the daughter of the sultan's brother, lived in the same palace. Her parents died when she was small, so the sultan raised her like his own child. She grew into a clever and beautiful young woman. All three of the sultan's sons were in love with her.

The sultan did not want to make any of his sons unhappy. So he called them together. "I love each of you the same," he said. "Travel to distant countries and bring me back the rarest thing you can find. The one who brings the most wonderful gift will marry Nouronnihar."

The three princes rode out together. On the first evening, they stopped at an inn where the road divided into three. There they agreed to meet again in one year. The next morning, each brother took a different road.

Houssain traveled to Bisnagar, a rich city with enormous markets. One day, a man in the market offered him a carpet. It looked quite ordinary, but the price was very high. "Sit on it and think of any place," said the seller. "In a moment, the carpet will carry you there." Houssain did not believe him, so they sat down on it together. He thought of his room at the inn, and at once they were there. Delighted, Houssain paid the high price.

Ali traveled to a busy city in another kingdom. There a man offered him a small ivory tube with a glass at each end. "Look through it and think of someone," the man said. Ali put the tube to his eye and thought of his father. At once, he saw the sultan at home, healthy and well. Ali bought the tube without another word.

Ahmed went to a far city famous for its gardens. In the market he met a man who held an apple in his hand. It was not a real apple but a clever piece of work. "Its smell can cure any illness," the man explained. To prove it, the man let a very sick person smell the apple. The sick man stood up at once, strong and well. Ahmed gladly paid for the apple.

When the year ended, the three brothers met again at the inn. Then Ali said, "Let's look at Nouronnihar through my tube." He looked, and his face turned pale. Nouronnihar lay in bed, and doctors stood sadly around her. She was very ill, and she seemed close to death. "Quick, sit on my carpet!" cried Houssain. He thought of the palace, and in a moment they arrived in her room. Ahmed held the apple under her nose. Slowly, she opened her eyes and sat up, healthy again.

The sultan felt great joy, but now he had a harder decision. "The tube told you about her illness," he said. "The carpet brought you home in time. And the apple saved her life. You needed every gift, so I cannot choose between them. Instead, each of you will shoot an arrow. The prince with the longest shot will marry Nouronnihar."

The whole court came out to a wide, open field. Houssain shot first, and his arrow flew a long way. Ali shot next, and his arrow landed even farther. Last of all, Ahmed pulled his bow with all his strength. His arrow flew high into the sky and disappeared. Servants searched the field for hours, but nobody found it. So the sultan chose Ali, and soon there was a great wedding. Houssain felt too sad to stay at court. He gave up his place and went to live a quiet life alone.

Ahmed did not go to the wedding. Instead, he set out to find his lost arrow. He walked for hours, far beyond the other arrows. At last, he found it lying flat among some high rocks. "Nobody can shoot this far," he thought. Looking around, he noticed an iron door in the rocks. He pushed it, and it opened onto a long stairway.

At the bottom, Ahmed found a splendid hall full of light. A beautiful lady sat there, with many servants around her. "Welcome, Prince Ahmed," she said with a warm smile. "How do you know my name?" Ahmed asked in surprise. "I am Peri Banou, a fairy," she answered. "My power carried your arrow so far. I wanted you to find me. Will you stay with me and be my husband?" Ahmed looked at her kind face, and his sadness disappeared. "Yes, with all my heart," he said. So the prince and the fairy married, and they lived happily together in her wonderful palace.

## Level B2

Long ago, a wise and powerful sultan lived in a splendid palace with his three sons, Houssain, Ali and Ahmed. In the same palace lived his niece, Nouronnihar, the daughter of his late brother. The sultan had raised her alongside his own children, and she had become a woman of great beauty and intelligence. When the time came to find her a husband, the sultan discovered a difficulty he had not expected. Each of his three sons was deeply in love with her, and each hoped to marry her himself.

The sultan loved his sons equally and was afraid of creating bitterness between them. For several days he considered the matter, and at last he called them into his private rooms. "If I choose one of you, the other two will feel wronged," he told them. "So I will let fortune decide. Each of you must travel to a distant country and bring back the most remarkable rarity you can find. Whoever returns with the most wonderful object will have Nouronnihar as his wife." The princes accepted the challenge gladly, and the sultan gave each of them a generous purse of gold.

Disguised as merchants, the three brothers left the city together on the same morning. By evening they had reached an inn that stood where the great road split into three separate paths. Over supper, they agreed to travel for one year and then meet again at this very inn. At dawn, they embraced, mounted their horses, and rode off in three different directions.

Houssain, the eldest, made his way to Bisnagar, a wealthy city whose markets seemed to stretch on forever. One morning he heard a trader calling out a price for a small, rather worn carpet, and the sum was astonishingly high. Houssain asked why it was worth so much, and the trader explained that it could carry its owner instantly anywhere. Doubtful but curious, Houssain asked for a demonstration. The two of them sat down together, and Houssain silently wished to be back in his room at the inn. In the blink of an eye, they were there. Without further argument, Houssain paid the full price, convinced that no rarity on earth could be finer.

Ali, the second brother, traveled to a famous city in a neighboring kingdom. In one of its bazaars he was offered an ivory tube about a foot long, with a glass at each end. The merchant told him that by looking through it a person could see anyone or anything he wished, however far away. Ali raised it to his eye and thought of his father. At once, he saw the sultan sitting on his throne, surrounded by his ministers and in perfect health. Convinced that he held the greatest treasure in the world, Ali paid for the tube without hesitation.

Ahmed, the youngest, journeyed to a distant city famous for its gardens and its skilled craftsmen. There a man showed him an artificial apple, so cleverly made that it looked almost real. Its scent, the man claimed, could cure any illness, even when the patient lay at the very edge of death. To prove his claim, he held the apple beneath the nose of a man who was gravely ill. Within moments, the sick man sat up and declared that he felt entirely well. Ahmed bought the apple immediately and set off for the inn.

When the year was over, the three brothers were reunited, and each proudly displayed his treasure. Ali suggested that they use his tube to see how things stood at home. Looking through it, he went pale, for he saw Nouronnihar lying in her bed, surrounded by anxious doctors. She was so ill that nobody expected her to live through the day. Houssain spread his carpet on the floor at once, and the three of them sat on it. He wished them into Nouronnihar's room, and a moment later they were standing beside her bed. Ahmed held the apple close to her face, and as she breathed in its scent, color returned to her cheeks. Soon she was sitting up, as healthy as ever.

The sultan was overjoyed, but the question of her marriage was now harder than ever. "Without Ali's tube, you would never have known of her danger," he said. "Without Houssain's carpet, you could not have arrived in time, and without Ahmed's apple, she would not have survived. Each gift was needed, so none of them can be called the best." Instead, he announced a contest of archery. The prince who shot an arrow farthest would win Nouronnihar's hand.

The whole court gathered on a broad plain outside the city to watch. Houssain shot first, and his arrow flew an impressive distance. Ali's arrow went farther still, landing well beyond his brother's. Then Ahmed drew his bow and released his arrow, which rose high into the air and vanished from sight. Servants searched the plain until evening, but it could not be found anywhere. Since Ahmed's arrow could not be measured, the sultan declared Ali the winner. The wedding was celebrated with great splendor soon afterwards. Houssain was too heartbroken to attend. He gave up his rank and went away to live as a holy man, far from the noise of the world.

Ahmed did not attend the wedding either. Convinced that his arrow had not simply disappeared, he went alone to search for it. He walked on and on across the plain, far beyond the place where the other arrows had fallen. At last, hours later, he found it lying flat at the foot of some steep rocks. No human archer could have sent an arrow so far, he realized, and he began to suspect something mysterious. Searching among the rocks, he found an iron door, which opened easily when he pushed it. A gently sloping passage led him down into the earth.

At the end of the passage lay a magnificent hall, glowing with light and richly decorated. On a raised seat sat a lady of extraordinary beauty, attended by many servants. She rose to greet him and called him by his name, which astonished him. She explained that she was a fairy called Peri Banou, and that she had known about the contest from the beginning. It was her power that had carried his arrow so far, because she had wished to bring him to her home. Then she told him that she would gladly become his wife, if he wanted her. Ahmed, who had lost Nouronnihar only days before, found that his sorrow had completely gone. He accepted with joy, and the prince and the fairy were married that same evening amid music and feasting.

## Level C1

Long ago there reigned a sultan whose wisdom was praised throughout his lands, and who shared his palace with three sons, Houssain, Ali and Ahmed. Under the same roof lived Nouronnihar, the orphaned daughter of his younger brother, whom the sultan had raised from infancy as tenderly as any child of his own. She had grown into a young woman whose beauty was matched by a lively wit, and the old man took great pride in her. Yet when he turned his mind to finding her a husband, he discovered, to his considerable dismay, that each of his three sons had quietly lost his heart to her.

The sultan was too fond of his sons to favor one over the others, and too shrewd not to see the resentment that such a choice would breed. After many days of reflection, he summoned all three to his private chamber. "Were I to choose among you, two of you would carry a grievance to the end of your days," he said. "Let fortune settle it instead. Go out into the world, each of you separately, and bring me back the most extraordinary rarity you can discover. Whichever of you returns with the most marvelous object shall have Nouronnihar for his bride." The princes, each privately confident of victory, accepted without hesitation, and their father furnished each of them with a generous purse for the journey.

Dressed as ordinary merchants so as to travel unremarked, the three brothers set out together and, by nightfall, had reached a large inn standing where the highway divided into three. There, over a modest supper, they made a pact. Each would travel for a single year, after which they would meet again under this same roof, so that all three might return to their father together. At dawn, they embraced, mounted, and rode away along three different roads, each soon lost to the others' sight.

Houssain, the eldest, traveled to Bisnagar, a city of such wealth that its bazaars seemed to stretch from one horizon to the other. For days, he wandered among the stalls, admiring bolts of shimmering silk, heaps of precious stones and lamps of beaten brass, yet nothing struck him as worthy of his purpose. Then one morning he heard a crier announce a price for a small and distinctly shabby carpet, and the figure was so outrageous that he stopped to stare. When he asked, half amused, what could justify such a sum, the man calmly explained that the carpet could carry its owner in an instant to any place he pictured. Houssain demanded proof. The two of them sat down together, Houssain fixed his thoughts on his room at the distant inn, and before he could draw a second breath they were sitting on its floor. He paid the price on the spot, persuaded that no treasure on earth could rival the one now rolled up beneath his arm.

Ali, the second son, made his way to a celebrated city in a neighboring kingdom, where merchants from every corner of the world came to trade. In one of its crowded arcades a man held up an ivory tube about a foot in length, with a little glass set into each end. Through it, the man declared, one could behold any person or place one desired, however many mountains and seas lay between. Ali, skeptical yet intrigued, put the tube to his eye and thought of his father. At once the sultan appeared before him, seated upon his throne amid his counselors, in perfect health. Scarcely able to believe his good fortune, Ali counted out the gold and hurried away with what he felt certain was the finest prize of all.

Ahmed, the youngest, journeyed to a far city renowned for its orchards and its ingenious craftsmen. Wandering through its market, he came upon a man offering an artificial apple, so skillfully fashioned that only a close look revealed it was no fruit of any tree. Its scent, the man insisted, would cure any illness at all, however serious, even in a patient whom the doctors had abandoned. When Ahmed raised an eyebrow, the man led him to a house nearby, where a sick man lay too weak to lift his head, and held the apple beneath his nose. Within moments the invalid sat up, declared himself quite restored, and called for his breakfast. Ahmed needed no further persuasion, and he, too, set off for the inn with his treasure.

When the year had run its course, the three brothers met as they had promised, and each, with barely concealed pride, laid his marvel before the others. It was Ali who proposed that they look through his tube to see how matters stood at home. He had scarcely raised it to his eye when the color drained from his face, for there lay Nouronnihar motionless in her bed, while the court physicians shook their heads around her. By every sign, she had only hours to live. Houssain unrolled his carpet without a word, and the three of them took their places upon it. He fixed his mind on her chamber, and in the next instant they found themselves standing at her bedside, to the utter bewilderment of the doctors. Ahmed bent over her and held the apple close, and as its scent reached her, the faintest flush crept back into her cheeks. Within the hour she was sitting up, as well as she had ever been.

The sultan's joy was boundless, but it did nothing to ease his dilemma; if anything, it deepened it. "Consider what has happened," he said, turning to his sons. "Had Ali's tube not shown you her danger, you would never have known of it, and had Houssain's carpet not brought you, you would have arrived too late. Had Ahmed's apple not been here, she would not be alive. Each of these marvels was indispensable, and I cannot in fairness call any one of them the greatest." There remained, he concluded, only one way to decide the matter. Each prince would shoot a single arrow, and whoever sent his arrow farthest would win Nouronnihar's hand.

On the appointed day the whole court assembled on a broad plain beyond the city walls. Houssain shot first, and his arrow traveled a very respectable distance. Ali's flew farther, coming to rest well beyond his brother's. Last of all, Ahmed drew his bow to its fullest stretch and let fly, and his arrow climbed so high and so far that it dwindled to a speck and vanished. Servants scoured the plain until the light failed, but not a trace of it could be found. Since an arrow that could not be found could not be measured, the sultan, with some reluctance, pronounced Ali the victor. The wedding was celebrated soon afterwards with all the magnificence the court could summon. Houssain, too heavy of heart to witness it, renounced his rank and his inheritance and withdrew to live as a holy man, far from palaces and all their quarrels.

Ahmed, for his part, was absent from the festivities as well, though for a very different reason. He could not shake off the conviction that his arrow had not simply vanished, and so, alone and on foot, he went in search of it. He walked for hours across the plain, long past the spot where his brothers' arrows had fallen, until the city lay far behind him. At last, at the foot of a range of steep and barren rocks, he found it lying flat upon the ground, as though it had been laid there by a careful hand. No mortal archer, he reflected, could have sent it so far, and the thought that some hidden power was at work grew stronger with every step. Searching among the boulders, he came upon an iron door set into the rock itself, and when he pushed it, it swung open without a sound. Beyond it, a gently sloping passage descended into the earth.

The passage brought him out into a hall more splendid than any in his father's palace, its walls glowing with a soft and steady radiance. Upon a raised seat at its far end sat a lady of such beauty that he stopped short, and around her stood a crowd of attendants. To his astonishment, she rose, greeted him warmly by name, and invited him to sit beside her. She was, she told him, a fairy called Peri Banou, and she had followed the whole affair of the three brothers and their rivalry from its very beginning. It was she who had carried his arrow beyond the reach of any searching eye, because she had long wished to draw him to her home. Should he wish it, she added with disarming frankness, she would gladly become his wife. Ahmed, who only days before had believed himself the most unfortunate of men, found his disappointment melting away like frost in sunlight. He accepted gladly, and before the day was over the prince and the fairy were married in her shining hall, amid music and the good wishes of her household.
