# Baucis and Philemon

Summary: A poor old couple open their door to two travelers nobody else will help.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): pitcher, milk, travelers

## Level A1

An old man lived in Phrygia. His name was Philemon. He lived with his wife, Baucis. They were poor, but they were kind. Their small house stood on a hill. There was a village near the hill. The people there were not kind. They never helped travelers.

One evening, two travelers came to the village. Nobody gave them food. So the travelers walked up to Philemon's house. One traveler was young, and one was older. "Come in and eat with us," said Philemon. "Thank you!" said the young traveler. His name was Quicksilver.

Baucis put food on the table. They had bread and a pitcher of milk. Quicksilver drank all his milk. "Can I have more?" he asked. The pitcher had very little milk in it. But Baucis took the pitcher. Milk came out of it again and again! The pitcher was full every time. The old bread was like new bread, too.

"What is this?" Baucis asked Philemon. The travelers stayed the night.

In the morning, they all went outside. Philemon and Baucis looked down. But the village was not there! Now there was a big lake. The houses and people were under the water. Fish swam in it. The older traveler spoke. "Those people were not kind," he said. "We are gods, and my name is Jupiter."

Philemon and Baucis turned around. Their small house was not there. Now a white palace stood there. "You were kind to us," said Jupiter. "What do you want?" he asked. "Let us live and die together," said Philemon. Jupiter said yes. Then the two gods were gone.

They lived there for many years. Their pitcher was always full of milk. They gave milk and food to every traveler. They grew very old. One morning, people looked for them. But Philemon and Baucis were not there. Two old trees stood by the door. They were very close together. People said, "Those trees are Philemon and Baucis."

## Level A2

Long ago, an old man named Philemon lived in Phrygia. He and his wife, Baucis, had a small house on a hill. They were poor, and they worked hard every day. But they were always kind, and they loved each other very much. Near the hill, there was a village with fine houses. The people of the village had a lot of money and food. But they never helped poor people or travelers. Their children laughed at travelers, and their dogs ran after them.

One evening, Philemon sat at the door of his house. Suddenly, Philemon heard dogs and children in the village. Then he saw two travelers on the road. Children and dogs ran after them, but the travelers walked on. Nobody in the village gave them any food. "Poor men!" said Philemon to Baucis. He went down the road to meet them.

One was young, with a happy face. He moved very fast, and his feet almost seemed to fly. The other traveler was older, and he had a deep, strong voice. "Welcome, my friends," said Philemon. He took them up to his house. The young traveler thanked him and said, "People call me Quicksilver."

Baucis put everything they had on the table. There was some bread, a little honey and some grapes. On the table stood an old pitcher with some milk in it. Quicksilver drank his milk quickly and asked for more. Baucis was worried, because there was almost no milk left. But she took the pitcher, and milk came out again! She gave milk to the older traveler, and the pitcher stayed full. The old bread was suddenly like new bread. "There is something special about this pitcher," Baucis said to Philemon.

The travelers stayed the night in the little house. In the morning, Philemon and Baucis walked out with them. They looked down at the village, but the village was gone! Now there was a wide, blue lake in its place. The houses and all the people were under the water. Fish swam in the clear water. "Those people were never kind to travelers," said the older traveler. "We are gods, and my name is Jupiter," he said.

Philemon and Baucis turned around. Their small house was gone too. Now a beautiful white palace stood on the hill. "You were kind to us, so ask me for anything," said Jupiter. Philemon talked with Baucis for a moment. "Let us live together and die at the same hour," said Philemon. Neither of them wanted to live alone. Jupiter said yes, and a moment later the two gods were gone.

Philemon and Baucis lived there for many happy years. The pitcher always had milk in it. They gave food and milk to every traveler at their door. At last, they were very old. One morning, people looked for them, but nobody could find them. In front of the door, there were two old trees. The trees grew very close together, like two old friends. People said, "Those trees are Philemon and Baucis."

## Level B1

Long ago, an old man named Philemon lived on a quiet hill in Phrygia. He lived in a small cottage with his wife, Baucis. They were poor, and they worked hard from morning until night. But they never felt really poor, because they had each other. Their door was always open to anyone who needed food or a place to rest.

Down below the hill, there was a village with fine houses and large gardens. The people there had plenty of everything, but their hearts were hard. They did not like strangers, and they never helped travelers. When a traveler came along the road, the village children shouted rude words at him. Their dogs barked and ran at his heels, and their parents only laughed.

One evening, Philemon was sitting by his door and watching the sun go down. Inside the cottage, Baucis was getting their supper ready. Suddenly they heard a terrible noise from the village below. Dogs were barking, and children were shouting. Philemon stood up and saw two travelers on the road. The children and dogs were following them, but the two men walked on calmly. "Come, Baucis," said Philemon. "We must go and meet these poor men."

The two travelers were very different from each other. The younger one was thin and quick, and he had a bright, clever face. He moved so lightly that his feet hardly seemed to touch the road. He carried an odd walking stick, and it almost seemed to move by itself. The older traveler was tall and calm, and he had a deep, serious voice. "Good evening, friends," said Philemon. "You are very welcome here." The younger traveler laughed and thanked him. His name, he said, was Quicksilver. Philemon told them about the unkind village. The older traveler looked down at it with a serious face.

Baucis felt sorry, because she had very little food for her guests. She put everything on the table: brown bread, cheese, a little honey and some grapes. In the middle stood an old brown pitcher with a little milk in it. Quicksilver drank his milk at once and asked for more. Baucis was worried, because she knew the pitcher was nearly empty. Still, she lifted it and held it over his cup. To her surprise, fresh milk came out until the cup was full. The older traveler asked for milk too, and the pitcher filled his cup as well. The hard old bread now tasted soft and new, and the grapes looked bigger and sweeter. Baucis looked at Philemon, and Philemon looked at Baucis. Neither of them understood what was happening. "This is a very strange pitcher," Baucis said quietly to her husband.

That night, the two guests stayed in the cottage. Early the next morning, the old couple walked with them to the top of the hill. When they looked down, they could not believe their eyes. The village was gone, and in its place lay a wide, quiet lake. The houses, the gardens and all the unkind people had disappeared under the water. Now only fish swam there, in the bright morning light. "Those people had no kindness in their hearts," said the older traveler. "So their village is gone, and the lake will stay in its place." Then he told them the truth. He was the god Jupiter, and his young friend Quicksilver was a god too.

Philemon and Baucis turned around to look at their home. Their little cottage was gone as well! In its place stood a beautiful palace of white marble. "You shared everything you had with us," said Jupiter. "Ask for one wish, and I will give it to you." Philemon and Baucis spoke quietly together for a moment. "We have lived together for many years," said Philemon. "Let us die at the same hour, so neither of us will ever be alone." "I give you your wish," said Jupiter. A moment later, the two gods were gone.

Philemon and Baucis lived in the palace for many more years. They welcomed every traveler, and the pitcher on their table was never empty. At last, they became very old. One morning, their guests looked for them everywhere, but they could not find them. In front of the door stood two new trees, an oak and a linden. Their branches reached toward each other and held on like hands. When the wind moved their leaves, they seemed to whisper, "Welcome, dear traveler, welcome."

## Level B2

Long ago, in Phrygia, an old man named Philemon and his wife, Baucis, lived in a tiny cottage on a hill. They had very little money, and every day they worked hard in their small garden. Yet they were never unhappy, because they loved each other deeply and shared whatever they had. If a tired stranger passed their door, he always found a seat, a piece of bread and a friendly word.

Below the hill lay a green valley, and in the valley stood a rich village. Its people lived in fine houses and ate good food every day, but their hearts were as hard as stone. They hated strangers and treated poor travelers with open scorn. Whenever someone tired and dusty came along the road, the village children ran after him, shouting rude names. The village dogs were sent barking at his heels, and the grown-ups only laughed at the sight. Philemon often shook his head when he heard the noise, because he could not understand such unkindness.

One summer evening, Philemon was sitting outside his door, watching the sun sink behind the hills. Baucis was inside the cottage, getting their simple supper ready. Suddenly a terrible noise came up from the village: dogs were barking and children were shouting wildly. Philemon stood up and saw two travelers climbing slowly up the road toward the hill. A crowd of children and dogs was following them, but the two men walked on without looking back. "Baucis, come quickly," called Philemon. "There are two poor travelers on the road, and nobody in the village will help them."

As the strangers came nearer, Philemon noticed how different they were. The younger one was slim and full of life, and he moved so lightly that he almost seemed to fly. In his hand he carried a curious staff, and it seemed to help him along like a living thing. His companion was much older and taller, with a calm, grand face and a deep, serious voice. "Good evening, my friends," said Philemon warmly. "You must be tired and hungry after such a long road, so please come and rest in our home." The young traveler thanked him with a cheerful laugh and said that his name was Quicksilver. While they walked up to the cottage, Philemon apologized for the people of the village. The older traveler listened in silence, and then he looked down at the valley with a stern, thoughtful face.

Inside the cottage, Baucis was a little ashamed, because she had so little to offer. She set out half a loaf of brown bread, some cheese, a dish of honey and a bunch of grapes. In the center of the table stood an old earthen pitcher, and there was only a little milk inside it. Quicksilver emptied his cup in one go and asked politely for more. Baucis's heart sank, because she had poured almost all the milk already. Even so, she lifted the pitcher and tipped it over his cup, expecting only a few drops. To her amazement, a stream of fresh, creamy milk poured out until the cup was full to the brim.

When the older traveler asked for milk, the pitcher filled his cup in the same way. The stale bread tasted as if it had just come out of the oven, and the honey was golden and sweet. Even the grapes had grown bigger, and they shone like jewels. Baucis peeped into the pitcher and found it full again, although nobody had added a single drop. "What a strange pitcher this is!" she whispered to her husband. Quicksilver only smiled, and Philemon began to suspect that their guests were no ordinary men.

That night, the two travelers slept in the little cottage. Early the next morning, Philemon and Baucis walked with them to the top of the hill to show them the road. When the old couple looked down into the valley, they could hardly believe their eyes. The village had vanished completely, and a wide, peaceful lake lay where it had once stood. The houses, the gardens and all the unkind villagers had disappeared beneath the shining water. Fish swam calmly through the place where the busy streets had been. "The people of that village had forgotten how to be kind to a stranger," said the older traveler seriously. "So the lake has taken their village, and it will stay there." Then he revealed the truth: he was the great god Jupiter, and Quicksilver was a god as well.

Philemon and Baucis turned around, and they gasped with surprise. Their humble cottage had gone, and a magnificent palace of white marble stood in its place. "You opened your door to us when the whole village shut theirs," said Jupiter. "Ask me for any gift you wish, and it will be yours." The old couple talked quietly together for a moment, and then Philemon spoke for them both. "We have spent our whole lives side by side," he said. "Let us leave this world at the same hour, so that neither of us has to live alone." "Your wish is granted," said Jupiter kindly, and in the next moment the two gods were gone.

Philemon and Baucis lived in the marble palace for many more years. They welcomed every traveler who passed, and the old pitcher on their table never ran dry. At last, they grew very, very old. One morning, their guests searched the whole palace for them, but the old couple were nowhere to be found. Instead, two trees that nobody had ever seen before stood in front of the door: an oak and a linden. Their branches grew so close together that they looked like two old friends holding hands. Whenever the breeze stirred their leaves, they seemed to whisper, "Welcome, dear traveler, welcome."

## Level C1

Long ago, in the hill country of Phrygia, an old man named Philemon and his wife, Baucis, lived in a cottage so small that two guests made it feel crowded. They owned almost nothing beyond a little garden, a few tools and an old earthen pitcher, and they worked from dawn to dusk simply to fill their plates. Yet poverty had never soured them; if anything, the long years had made them gentler, and whatever they had they shared without counting the cost. A tired stranger who stopped at their gate could always be sure of a seat in the shade, a crust of bread and a kind word.

Below their hill stretched a broad green valley, and in the middle of it lay a prosperous village. Its people lived in handsome houses, kept well-stocked kitchens and wanted for nothing, but somewhere along the way they had lost the habit of kindness. Strangers, to them, were a nuisance at best, and a poor stranger was simply a target for their contempt. Whenever a dusty traveler came along the road, the children ran after him hurling insults, while the village dogs snapped and barked at his heels. Their parents, far from scolding them, stood in their doorways and laughed, as though the whole sorry scene were an evening's entertainment. Philemon heard that noise more often than he liked, and each time it left him shaking his head, unable to understand how anyone could treat a guest so coldly.

One summer evening, as the sun was sinking behind the western hills, Philemon sat on the bench outside his door, enjoying the cool air. Baucis was busy indoors, getting their modest supper ready. All at once a great din rose from the valley below: dogs barking furiously, children shrieking, and, beneath it all, the mocking laughter of grown men and women. Shading his eyes, Philemon made out two figures climbing steadily up the road, with a noisy crowd of children and dogs trailing behind them. The travelers paid the crowd no attention at all, which struck Philemon as rather remarkable. "Baucis!" he called, rising to his feet. "Two poor wanderers are coming up the road, and it seems that nobody in the village has offered them so much as a cup of water."

As the strangers drew nearer, Philemon could not help noticing how unlike each other they were. The younger was slender and quick, with a bright, mischievous face, and he moved so lightly that his feet barely seemed to brush the ground. In his hand he held a curious staff that appeared to carry him forward almost of its own accord, as if it were alive. His companion was older and much taller, and there was something in his calm, majestic bearing that made Philemon feel he ought to bow. "Good evening, friends," said Philemon, with all the warmth he could gather. "You have had a long road and a poor welcome, so come up and rest under our roof; what little we have is yours." The young traveler thanked him with a merry laugh, remarking that he went by the name of Quicksilver. On the way up to the cottage, Philemon apologized, rather awkwardly, for the behavior of his neighbors in the valley. The older traveler said nothing, but he turned and gazed down at the village for a long moment, and his face grew stern and thoughtful.

Inside, Baucis greeted the guests with a smile, though privately she was ashamed of how bare her table looked. She brought out everything in the house: half a loaf of brown bread, a modest wedge of cheese, a small dish of honey and a single bunch of purple grapes. In the center of the table stood the old earthen pitcher, holding barely enough milk for two cups. Quicksilver drained his cup in a single gulp, declared it the finest milk he had ever tasted, and asked, with perfect politeness, for a little more. Baucis's heart sank, for she had already poured out nearly all there was, and she dreaded the moment when the pitcher would come up empty. Nevertheless, rather than refuse a guest, she lifted it and tilted it over his cup, bracing herself for a few miserable drops. To her astonishment, a rich, creamy stream poured out and kept pouring, until the cup brimmed over. When the older traveler held out his cup, exactly the same thing happened, and the pitcher seemed none the lighter for it.

Meanwhile, the stale bread had turned soft and fragrant, as if it had come straight from the oven, and the honey glowed like liquid gold. Even the grapes had swelled to twice their size, each one bursting with sweetness. Unable to resist, Baucis peeped into the pitcher and found it brimming once more, although no one had added a single drop. "There is something very strange about this pitcher," she whispered to her husband, while Quicksilver hid a smile behind his cup. Philemon, for his part, had begun to suspect that the guests at his humble table were no ordinary men.

The travelers spent the night in the little cottage, and early the next morning the old couple walked with them to the top of the hill to set them on their way. Turning to look down into the valley, Philemon and Baucis stopped short, scarcely able to believe what lay before them. The village was gone; where its houses and gardens had stood only the evening before, a wide, still lake now glittered in the morning sun. Every house and every garden, together with the people who had jeered at strangers, had vanished beneath its calm surface without a trace. Fish glided peacefully through the clear water, over the very place where the busy streets had run.

"Those people forgot that a stranger at the door deserves bread and shelter," said the older traveler in a solemn, quiet voice. "Their village is gone, and the lake will remain in its place for as long as the hills stand." Then, at last, he told them plainly who their guests had been: he was the mighty Jupiter, and the cheerful Quicksilver was a god as well.

The old couple turned back toward their home, and Baucis gave a little cry of wonder. Their humble cottage had vanished too, and in its place rose a splendid palace of gleaming white marble, its doors standing open to the morning. "When a whole village shut its doors against us, you opened yours and gave us everything you had," said Jupiter. "Name any gift you wish, and it shall be yours." Philemon and Baucis drew aside and spoke together in low voices, and when they came back it was Philemon who answered for them both. "We have shared our whole lives, our work and our joy alike," he said. "Our only wish is to leave this world in the same hour, so that neither of us is ever left to grieve alone." "It shall be as you ask," said Jupiter gently, and before the old couple could thank him, both gods had disappeared.

Philemon and Baucis lived on in the marble palace for many more years, and it never occurred to them to keep their good fortune to themselves. Every traveler who climbed the hill found open doors and a table laid for supper. The old pitcher, true to its strange nature, never once ran dry. In time, however, the couple grew very old indeed, so frail that they could only sit together in the sun and watch the lake. One morning their guests searched every hall and garden of the palace, calling their names, but Philemon and Baucis were nowhere to be found. Instead, beside the door stood two trees that no one remembered seeing before: a sturdy oak and a graceful linden. Their branches were woven together so closely that they looked like an old husband and wife holding hands, unwilling to let go. And whenever the evening breeze stirred their leaves, the two trees seemed to murmur softly to every passer-by, "Welcome, dear traveler, welcome."
