# Sadko and the Sea King

Summary: A poor musician from Novgorod plays for the Sea King and goes on a long journey.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): harp, sea king, palace

## Level A1

Sadko lived in the city of Novgorod. He had very little money. But he had a harp. He played it well.

One evening, Sadko sat by Lake Ilmen. He played his harp for the water. Then the water began to move. A big man came up out of it. It was the Sea King! "I love your music," said the Sea King. "Three fish of gold live here. Go and tell the men at the market."

Sadko went to the market. But the men there did not believe him. "Show us one fish," said the men. "Then all our shops are yours!" So they all went to the water. Sadko put a net into the water. Three fish of gold were in the net! Now all the shops were his.

Sadko had a lot of money now. He bought many ships. His ships took him far across the sea. He never gave anything to the Sea King.

On the way home, his ships stopped. They did not move at all. "The Sea King wants me," said Sadko. He took his harp. Then he went down into the sea.

Sadko found a great palace in the sea. "Play for me!" said the Sea King. Sadko played, and the Sea King danced. He danced for three days and nights. Up above, the sea moved and moved.

Then an old man came to Sadko. "Stop playing now," said the old man. "The ships up there need help. The King wants to give you a wife. Do not choose a pretty girl. Choose the last girl in the line."

Sadko stopped playing. "Stay with me," said the Sea King. "Choose a wife from these girls." Many girls stood in a long line. Sadko looked at every girl. Then he chose the last one.

That night, Sadko closed his eyes. In the morning, he opened them. He was by the water near Novgorod! His ships came home on the Volkhov River. Sadko was home again.

## Level A2

Long ago, Sadko lived in the city of Novgorod. He was poor, but he had a harp. He played it at parties, and people loved his music. Then the parties stopped, and nobody asked him to play.

Sadko felt very sad, so he walked to Lake Ilmen. He sat by the water and played for a whole day. Suddenly the water began to move. A very big man came up out of it. It was the Sea King, the ruler of all the seas. "Your music made me very happy," said the Sea King. "Now I want to give you something back. Three fish of gold live in this lake. Go to the market and tell the men there about them."

The next day, Sadko went to the market. "There are fish of gold in Lake Ilmen," he told the men. They laughed at him. "It is not true," they said. "Catch one, and you can have all our shops!" So they all went to the water together. Sadko put a net into the water. He brought out three fish of gold. The men were very surprised, but they gave Sadko all their shops.

Now Sadko had more money than anyone in Novgorod. He built a beautiful house and bought many ships. He traveled across the sea and sold his goods in other countries. But in all those years, he gave the Sea King nothing.

On the way home from one long trip, his ships stopped. The wind was strong, but they did not move at all. "The Sea King wants me," said Sadko. "I forgot him, so now I must go down to him." He took his harp and went over the side of his ship.

Far down in the sea, Sadko found a palace of white stone. "At last!" said the Sea King. "Now play for me." Sadko played, and the Sea King began to dance. He danced for three days and three nights. High above, the ships moved up and down on the sea.

Then an old man with white hair came to Sadko. "Stop playing now," said the old man. "The ships up there need help. The King wants to give you a wife. Do not choose a beautiful girl. Choose the last girl in the line."

Sadko stopped playing, and the sea became still again. "Stay here with me," said the Sea King. "Look at these girls and choose a wife." One beautiful girl after another walked past Sadko. He smiled at each of them, but he said nothing. At the end of the line came a small, simple girl. "I choose her," said Sadko.

The Sea King was pleased, and there was a big party. That night, Sadko closed his eyes and slept. When he opened them, he was by a small river near Novgorod. The girl was not there, because she was the river. Then Sadko looked up and saw his own ships. They came home along the Volkhov, and all his men were well. Sadko never went out to sea again.

## Level B1

Long ago, in the busy city of Novgorod, there lived a young musician called Sadko. He had no land and no money, but he could play the harp beautifully. Rich families often invited him to their feasts, and his songs made everyone happy.

Then, for some reason, the invitations stopped. One day passed, then another, and nobody called for Sadko. Feeling lonely, he walked out of the city to the shore of Lake Ilmen. He sat on a stone and played his harp from morning until evening. On the third day, while he was playing, the lake suddenly began to rise and turn. A huge figure came up out of the water, with water running from his long hair. It was the Sea King, who ruled over every sea and lake in the world.

"Your music has made me very happy," said the Sea King. "Now I will help you in return. Three fish with fins of gold live in this lake. Tell the merchants of Novgorod about them, and make a bet with them." Then he went back down into the water, and the lake was calm again.

The next day, Sadko went to a feast where the richest merchants were sitting. "There are fish with golden fins in Lake Ilmen," he told them. The merchants laughed loudly. "No such fish exist," they said. "We will bet all our shops and goods against everything you have." So they went to the lake together, and Sadko threw a net into the water. When he pulled it out, a fish with golden fins was shining inside. He threw the net two more times and caught two more golden fish. The merchants had to keep their word, and all their shops became his.

Sadko was now the richest man in Novgorod. He built a fine house and bought thirty ships. He filled them with goods and sailed far across the sea to trade in other lands. For twelve years he traveled and grew richer and richer. But in all that time, he never gave anything back to the Sea King.

On the long way home, a strange thing happened. The wind was blowing hard, but the thirty ships stood still on the sea. "The Sea King wants something from us," Sadko said to his men. They threw a barrel of gold and silver into the waves. Still the ships did not move. "It is not gold that he wants," said Sadko. "It is one of us." Each man threw a small piece of wood with his name on it into the water. The other pieces floated, but Sadko's piece went straight down. "Then it is me," he said calmly. He took his harp and said goodbye to his men. Then he stepped down onto a wide board that lay on the waves. At once the ships began to sail away, and Sadko fell into a deep sleep.

When he woke, he was at the bottom of the sea. In front of him stood a palace of white stone. The Sea King was sitting inside on a high seat. "At last you have come," he said. "Play for me, musician!" Sadko began to play, and the Sea King began to dance. He danced for a whole day, then another, then a third. Far above them, the sea was rolling, and the waves were rising high. Many ships were in trouble on the stormy water.

Then Sadko felt a hand on his shoulder. An old man with a long white beard was standing beside him. "Stop playing," the old man said quietly. "Break the strings of your harp and tell the King that you cannot play anymore. Soon he will offer you a wife. Do not choose one of the beautiful ones who come first. Choose the last girl in the line, the quiet one who walks behind the others."

Sadko broke the strings, and the sea above them became calm again. "Stay with me," said the Sea King. "I will give you a wife from my kingdom. Look at these girls and choose." Three hundred beautiful girls walked past Sadko, one after another. He smiled at each of them, but he did not say a word. At the very end came a quiet, simple girl who looked at the ground. "I choose her," said Sadko.

The Sea King was pleased, and there was a great wedding feast in the palace. That night Sadko lay down to sleep. When he opened his eyes, he was lying beside a small river near Novgorod. His bride was gone, and he understood that she was the river itself. Just then, he looked up and saw his own thirty ships. They were sailing home along the Volkhov, and every man on board was safe. Sadko went home, and he never sailed out onto the sea again.

## Level B2

In the old trading city of Novgorod there once lived a young man named Sadko, who owned nothing but a harp. He had no fields, no shop and no savings, yet when his fingers touched the strings, people forgot their troubles. For years the wealthy families of the city invited him to their feasts, where he played until the candles burned low.

Then, without any warning, the invitations stopped coming. A day went by, then a second and a third, and still nobody sent for Sadko. Hurt and restless, he walked out beyond the city walls to the shore of Lake Ilmen. There he sat down on a flat white stone and played to the empty water from morning until dusk. He returned the next day, and the day after that, because the lake seemed to be the only listener he had left.

On the third evening, while he was still playing, the surface of the lake began to swirl. The water rose into a great green hill, and out of it stepped an enormous figure with a crown of shells. It was the Sea King, the lord of every sea, lake and river in the world. "I have heard you play for three days," he said in a deep, rolling voice. "No one has ever given me such a gift, so I will give you one in return. In this lake there are three fish whose fins are made of pure gold. Go to the merchants of Novgorod and wager that such fish exist. They will laugh, but you will win." With that, the Sea King sank back beneath the surface, and the lake lay smooth and silent once more.

The following day Sadko went to a feast where the proudest merchants of the city had gathered. When the talk turned to wonders, he said quietly that there were fish with golden fins in Lake Ilmen. The merchants roared with laughter and offered to bet their shops and all their goods against everything he possessed. Sadko accepted, and the whole company followed him down to the shore. He cast a silk net into the lake, and when it was hauled in, a fish with gleaming golden fins lay inside. He cast it twice more and caught two more, exactly as the Sea King had promised. The merchants could hardly believe what they had seen, but a bet was a bet, and they handed over their shops.

Almost overnight, the poor musician became the richest merchant in Novgorod. He built himself a splendid house, filled it with fine things, and fitted out thirty ships. Loaded with furs and other goods, his ships sailed far across the sea to distant markets. For twelve years Sadko traded in foreign lands, and his fortune kept growing. In all that time, however, he never once sent a gift to the Sea King, who had given him everything.

As the fleet was finally heading home, something strange happened in the open sea. A strong wind filled the sails, yet all thirty ships stopped as if they had dropped anchor. "The Sea King is demanding his share," Sadko told his crew. They lowered a barrel of silver into the waves, then a barrel of gold, but the ships stayed where they were. "It is not treasure that he wants," Sadko said slowly. "He wants one of us." Each man wrote his name on a little piece of wood and dropped it over the side. Every piece bobbed on the water except Sadko's, which went straight to the bottom. Sadko accepted his fate without a word of complaint. He took his harp, bid farewell to his crew, and climbed down onto a broad plank floating beside the ship. The moment he let go, the ships moved off, and Sadko drifted into a deep sleep.

He woke on the seabed, before a palace of white stone whose halls glowed with a soft green light. The Sea King sat inside on a high throne and greeted him like an old friend. "So you have come at last," he said. "Now play for me, musician, and do not stop." Sadko played, and the king leapt up and began to dance. He danced for one day, then a second, then a third, and his dancing shook the whole sea. High above them, the waves rose like hills, and ships all over the world were tossed about in the storm.

As Sadko played on, he felt a light touch on his shoulder. An old man with a long white beard was standing at his side. "You must stop," the old man whispered. "The sailors up there are in danger. Break the strings of your harp and tell the king that you can play no more. Soon he will offer you a bride. Let the beautiful ones pass, and choose the last girl in the line, the quiet one who walks at the very end." Then, before Sadko could thank him, the old man was gone.

Sadko snapped the strings, and slowly the sea above them grew calm. The Sea King was disappointed, but he did not want to lose such a guest. "Stay with me forever," he said, "and I will give you a wife from my own kingdom." Three hundred lovely maidens walked past, then three hundred more, and then three hundred more. Sadko smiled politely at each of them but said nothing. At the very end of the line came a modest girl with dark eyes, who kept her gaze on the sand. "This is the one I choose," said Sadko.

The Sea King was delighted with the choice, and a splendid wedding feast was held in the palace. That night Sadko lay down and fell into a deep sleep. When he opened his eyes, he was lying on the grassy bank of a small river close to Novgorod. His bride had vanished, and he realized that she had been the river all along. At that moment, Sadko saw thirty familiar sails coming up the Volkhov. His ships had come home, and every member of his crew was alive and well. Sadko went back to his house in Novgorod, and he never sailed out onto the open sea again.

## Level C1

In the old trading city of Novgorod, where merchants grew fat on furs and wax and church bells rang out over the Volkhov, there lived a young man named Sadko. His whole fortune was a harp, yet when his fingers ran across the strings, even the sternest guests at a feast leaned back and forgot their cares. For years the great houses of the city sent for him whenever they celebrated, and he played until long after the candles had guttered.

Then, as quietly as it had begun, the summoning stopped. One day passed without a messenger at his door, then a second, then a third, and Sadko began to wonder whether the city had simply grown tired of him. Stung in his pride rather than his purse, he walked out through the gates to the shore of Lake Ilmen, where nobody would see his disappointment. There he settled on a flat white stone and played to the gray water from first light until the sun went down, asking nothing of it but that it listen. He came back the next morning, and the morning after that, since an empty lake, he reasoned, was at least an honest audience.

On the third evening, as the last notes drifted over the reeds, the lake began to stir. The water heaved upward into a shining green mound, and from it rose a figure so vast that his shoulders blotted out the setting sun. His beard was tangled with weed, and his crown was made of pearl and shell. It was the Sea King, lord of every sea, lake and river beneath the sky. "Three days you have played for me, and not once did you ask for payment," he said, his voice rolling like distant surf. "Such a gift deserves another. In this lake swim three fish whose fins are of pure gold, though no man of Novgorod has ever seen them. Go to the merchants, wager them that the fish exist, and leave the rest to me." Then he sank back into the depths, and the lake lay as smooth as polished glass.

The next day Sadko made his way to a feast where the proudest merchants of the city sat eating and boasting of their wealth. When the talk turned, as it always did, to wonders, he remarked that Lake Ilmen held fish with fins of gold. The hall shook with laughter, and before long the merchants were staking their shops and every bale of their goods against whatever the penniless musician possessed. Sadko accepted with a bow, and the whole merry company trooped down to the shore to watch him make a fool of himself. He cast a silk net into the lake, and when it was hauled dripping onto the stones, a fish with fins of gleaming gold lay twisting in its folds. Twice more he cast, and twice more the net came back with a golden prize, precisely as the Sea King had foretold. The merchants stared in silence, but a wager made before witnesses could not be undone, and one by one they handed over the keys to their shops.

So it was that the poorest musician in Novgorod became, almost overnight, its richest merchant. He raised a splendid house with carved gates, filled it with silks and silver, and fitted out thirty ships with painted prows. Laden with furs and wax, the fleet sailed to distant harbors, and for twelve long years Sadko bought and sold in foreign markets, his fortune swelling with every voyage. Yet in all that time, amid all that prosperity, he never once thought to send a single coin to the one who had made it possible.

It was on the long voyage home that the sea reminded him. A brisk wind was filling every sail, and yet all thirty ships came to a sudden halt in open water, as though held fast by an invisible hand. "The Sea King has come to collect what is owed," Sadko told his crew, and none of them argued. They lowered a barrel of silver over the side, and when that failed, a barrel of gold, but the ships stood as still as islands. "It is not treasure he is asking for," Sadko said at last. "It is one of us." Each man wrote his name on a small piece of wood and tossed it into the waves. Every piece bobbed on the surface except one, which plunged straight down like a stone. It was Sadko's. He did not protest, for he knew perfectly well whose debt was being called in. Taking only his harp, he embraced his crew, climbed down onto a broad oak plank that lay on the water, and let the ship go. No sooner had he let go of the ship's side than the fleet surged forward, and Sadko, rocking gently on his plank, sank into a sleep as deep as the sea itself.

He awoke on the seabed, before a palace of white stone whose halls glowed with a soft, underwater green. The Sea King lounged on a throne of coral, and he greeted his guest with all the warmth of a creditor whose patience had finally been rewarded. "Twelve years I have waited for this," he declared. "Play, musician, and do not stop until I tell you." Sadko played, and the king sprang up and began to dance, slowly at first and then with a wild, rolling joy. He danced through one day, then a second, then a third, and with every stamp of his feet the whole sea trembled. Far above, the waves reared up like mountains, and ships across the wide world were flung about in a storm that no sailor could explain.

In the middle of his playing, Sadko felt a light touch on his shoulder and turned to find an old man with a long white beard beside him. "Enough," the old man murmured. "Up there, honest sailors are struggling to save their ships while the king amuses himself. Break the strings of your harp and tell him you can play no longer. He will want to keep you, and he will offer you a bride. Let the beauties go by, however fair they may seem, and choose the last girl in the line, the one who walks behind all the others with her eyes lowered." Before Sadko could ask his name, the old man was nowhere to be seen.

Sadko snapped the strings with his thumb, and little by little the sea above them settled into calm. The Sea King frowned at the broken instrument, but he had no wish to lose so fine a musician, and his frown soon became a coaxing smile. "Stay with me always," he urged, "and you shall have a wife from my own kingdom." Three hundred maidens glided past, each lovelier than the last, then three hundred more, and then three hundred again, until Sadko's head swam with silver and pearl. He smiled courteously at every one of them and said nothing at all. At the very end of the procession came a slender girl in plain clothes, with dark eyes and a quiet manner, who kept her gaze fixed on the sand. "This one," said Sadko, "if she will have me."

The Sea King laughed with delight at so modest a choice, and a wedding feast was held that shook the coral halls with music and dancing. When night came, Sadko lay down and closed his eyes, and the murmur of the sea carried him off into sleep. He opened them to birdsong and morning sunlight, lying in the long grass beside a narrow river not far from the walls of Novgorod. His bride was nowhere to be found. As he watched the clear water hurrying past, he understood that she had been the river all along, and that she had carried him home. At that very moment he caught sight of thirty familiar sails coming up the Volkhov, every ship whole and every man of his crew alive. Sadko walked back through the city gates to his house, and for the rest of his days he never again sailed out onto the open sea.
