# In the Abyss

Summary: A daring scientist goes down to the bottom of the ocean in a steel sphere and finds a hidden world.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): sphere, ocean, lights

## Level A1

The Ptarmigan was a ship on the ocean. Elstead was on the ship with two friends. Their names were Weybridge and Steevens. Elstead wanted to see the ocean bottom.

So Elstead made a strong sphere. It had small windows and a light inside. A rope held a heavy weight under it. The weight could take the sphere down. A small clock could cut the rope. Then the sphere could come back up.

"This is a bad idea," said Steevens. "I must see it," said Elstead. Early one morning, Elstead got into the sphere. His friends put it into the water.

Weybridge and Steevens waited. Hours went by. The sphere did not come back. "We lost our friend," said Steevens. Night came. Then Weybridge saw a light in the water. It was the sphere! Elstead was well. He told his friends his story.

The sphere went down into black water. But soon Elstead saw many small lights. The lights were fish! He could not stop looking at them.

At last, the sphere stopped at the bottom. Then some figures came to it. They had very big eyes. They walked like people. But they lived in the ocean. They only wanted to know about Elstead.

The ocean people took the sphere far away. Soon Elstead saw a city of lights! Many people came out of the houses. They stood around the sphere. They put their heads down. Then they made music together. To them, the sphere was very special. It came from the world above.

Then the clock cut the rope. The weight stayed down. The sphere went up and up.

Later, Elstead wanted to see the city again. He went down in a new sphere. His friends waited for many days. But the sphere never came back up. No one ever saw Elstead again.

## Level A2

The Ptarmigan was a ship far out on the ocean. Three friends were on board: Elstead, Weybridge and Steevens. Elstead wanted to go five miles down, to the ocean bottom.

For this, Elstead built a strong sphere of steel. It had small windows of thick glass and a light inside. Under the sphere, a rope held a heavy weight. The weight could pull the sphere down. A clock inside the sphere could cut the rope after some time. Without the weight, the sphere could go up to the top again.

Steevens said, "I do not like this plan. The water is too deep, and it is too dark down there." "I must see it with my own eyes," Elstead answered. Early one morning, Elstead got into the sphere. His friends closed the door behind him. They put the sphere into the water, and it went down fast.

Weybridge and Steevens watched the water and waited. The morning went by, and then the afternoon. The sphere did not come up. Steevens said, "Something is wrong. I think we lost him." Night came, and the ocean was black. Then Weybridge saw a light under the water. Soon the sphere rose out of the ocean. The friends opened the door, and Elstead came out. He was cold, but he was well.

That night, Elstead told his friends his story. As the sphere went down, the water turned green and then black. But soon Elstead saw small lights in the dark water. The lights were fish! Each fish had its own lights, like a little ship at night.

At last, the sphere came to rest on the bottom. Then Elstead saw some figures, and they came toward him. They were like people, but they were not people. They had big round eyes, and they walked on two feet. They came close and looked at the sphere for a long time. They only wanted to learn about this new thing.

Then the ocean people took hold of the rope. They pulled the sphere and its weight along the ground. After a long time, Elstead saw a city of lights. Every house in it gave light, and there were streets between them. Hundreds of ocean people came out to see the sphere. They stood around it and put their heads down. Then they began to sing a long, slow song. To them, the sphere was a gift from the world above.

In the middle of the song, the clock cut the rope. The weight stayed down there. The sphere went up very fast through the black water. At last, Elstead came out into the night air.

Elstead wanted to go back to the city of lights. So he built a new sphere, bigger and better than before. One clear morning, it went down into the ocean. His friends waited for many days and nights. But the sphere never came back. No one ever saw Elstead again.

## Level B1

The ship Ptarmigan lay still on the calm blue ocean, far from any land. On her deck stood three men who were good friends: Elstead, Weybridge and Steevens. Elstead was a man of science, and he had a bold plan. He wanted to go down five miles, all the way to the bottom of the ocean. Nobody had ever seen that place, and Elstead wanted to be the first.

For his journey, Elstead built a hollow sphere of steel. Its walls were thick enough to stand the great weight of the water above. Small windows of very thick glass let him look out on every side. Inside, an electric light was ready to shine into the darkness. Under the sphere, a strong rope held a heavy weight of lead. The weight would pull the sphere straight down to the bottom. There was also a clock inside the sphere, and Elstead set it for a certain time. At that time, the clock would cut the rope and leave the weight behind. Then the sphere, which was lighter than water, would float up again.

Steevens was not happy about any of this. "The pressure down there is terrible," he said. "One small crack in that glass will be the end of you." Elstead only smiled at his friend. "I have checked everything twice," he replied. "Besides, I must see the bottom of the ocean with my own eyes."

Early the next morning, Elstead climbed into the sphere. His friends closed the heavy door and screwed it tight. Then the sailors swung the sphere over the side of the ship. It dropped into the water, and in a moment it was gone.

Weybridge and Steevens stood by the rail and watched the empty waves. The morning passed, then the long afternoon. The sphere should have come up hours ago, but there was no sign of it. "Something has gone wrong," Steevens said quietly. "I am afraid we will never see Elstead again." The sun went down, and the ocean grew dark. Suddenly Weybridge saw a light deep under the water. It grew brighter and brighter, and then the sphere jumped up out of the sea. The sailors brought it to the ship and opened the door. Elstead was cold and very tired, but he was alive and full of news.

That night, while the ship rocked gently, Elstead told his friends everything. As the sphere went down, the water around him changed from blue to green. Then it turned black, and the cold came through the steel walls. Soon he began to see little lights moving in the darkness. They were fish, and their bodies were shining like small lamps. Some of them swam close to the glass and stared at him with round eyes. Elstead watched them for a long time, and he forgot to be afraid.

At last the sphere landed softly on the bottom of the ocean. Elstead turned on his electric light and looked out. The ground was soft and black, and nothing seemed to move. Then he noticed some shapes that were coming toward the sphere. They walked on two legs, like men, but they were not men. They had large heads and huge eyes, which could see in the dark. These ocean people came close and studied the sphere very carefully. One of them pressed its face to the window and looked at Elstead. They did not seem angry or afraid, only very curious.

After a while, the ocean people took hold of the rope. They began to pull the sphere and its weight across the bottom. Elstead could do nothing, so he simply sat and watched. Far ahead, a soft light was growing in the darkness. It was a city, and every building in it was shining. There were streets and tall walls, and a great building stood in the middle. Hundreds of ocean people came out to meet the sphere. They gathered around it and bowed their heads. Then they began to sing a slow, strange song together. Elstead understood that they thought the sphere was a holy thing. To them, it was something wonderful that had come down from the world above.

In the middle of their song, the clock inside the sphere finally cut the rope. The weight stayed on the bottom, and the sphere raced upward. The city of lights grew smaller below him and then disappeared. The sphere rushed up past the shining fish and broke through into the night.

Elstead could not forget the ocean people and their beautiful city. He wanted to go back and learn more about them. So he built a new and better sphere for a second journey. One clear morning, it went down into the ocean again. His friends waited for many days, but the sphere never came back. No one ever saw Elstead again.

## Level B2

The Ptarmigan lay almost motionless on a calm ocean, hundreds of miles from the nearest land. Three friends stood together on her deck that morning: Elstead, Weybridge and Steevens. Elstead was a scientist with a reputation for daring ideas, and this one was his boldest yet. He intended to go down five miles, to the very bottom of the ocean, where no human being had ever been.

For the journey he had designed a hollow sphere of steel, built to resist the crushing weight of the water. Its walls were as thick as a man's hand, and its small round windows were made of extremely thick glass. An electric light was fixed inside, so that Elstead could look out into the eternal darkness below. Beneath the sphere, a strong rope held a heavy weight of lead, which would drag it swiftly down. The clever part was a clock mounted inside the sphere. After a fixed time, the clock would cut the rope, and the weight would be left behind on the bottom. Since the sphere was lighter than water, it would then float straight back up to the surface.

Steevens had never liked the plan, and he said so once more. "Think of the pressure," he warned. "If even one of those windows cracks, the sea will be inside before you can cry out." "I have tested the glass a dozen times," Elstead answered calmly. "Besides, nobody knows what lives down there, and I intend to be the first to find out."

Early the next morning, Elstead climbed through the narrow door, and his friends screwed it firmly shut behind him. The sailors swung the sphere over the side of the ship and released it. It hit the water, rolled once, and then vanished beneath the gentle waves.

Weybridge and Steevens had calculated that the whole trip would take a few hours at most. The morning passed slowly, then the long afternoon, and still the empty ocean gave them nothing. "Something has gone wrong," Steevens said at last, staring at the water. "I'm afraid our friend is lost." The sun sank, and the sea turned dark, and the sailors began to speak in low voices. Then, close to midnight, Weybridge pointed and gave a shout. Far down in the black water, a pale light was rising toward them. A moment later, the sphere burst out of the sea and rocked on the surface. It was hauled on board and the door was unscrewed. Elstead was cold, pale and exhausted, but he was alive, and his eyes were shining with excitement.

Later that night, he told them what had happened. As the sphere sank, the water outside had changed from blue to green, and then to total black. Before long, however, he had noticed small points of light drifting in the darkness. They were fish, and their bodies glowed like tiny lanterns of green and blue. Some swam right up to the window and peered at him, while others turned and darted away. He had been so fascinated by them that he almost forgot where he was.

Finally, the sphere settled on the ocean floor, sinking slightly into the soft black mud. Elstead switched on his light and gazed out at a silent, empty plain. Then something moved at the edge of the light, and several figures approached the sphere. They walked upright on two legs, almost like men, yet they were clearly not human. Their heads were large, and their enormous eyes had been formed for a world without sunlight. They gathered around the strange object from above, studying it with obvious curiosity. One of them came very close and pressed its face against the glass to examine Elstead. It did not seem hostile, only deeply puzzled, as if it were meeting a creature from another world.

After a time, the creatures took hold of the rope and began to pull. Elstead, who could do nothing to stop them, felt the sphere and its weight sliding slowly along the bottom. Ahead, a faint glow appeared in the darkness and grew stronger as they moved toward it. It was a city, and every building in it shone with a soft light of its own. There were streets, walls and towers, and in the center stood a great building like a temple. Hundreds of the ocean people came out to meet the sphere, and the procession moved through the streets toward the temple. There they stood around the sphere in a wide circle and bowed their heads. Then they began a long, slow chant, rising and falling in the cold water. Elstead realized, with growing wonder, that they believed the sphere was a sacred thing. Perhaps, he thought, they had always looked upward and wondered what lay beyond their dark sky. Now something had come down to them from that unknown world, and they welcomed it with song.

In the middle of the ceremony, the clock finally did its work and cut the rope. The weight stayed on the ocean floor, and the sphere flew upward, leaving the astonished crowd behind. The glowing city shrank beneath him until it was a single spark, and then it was gone. The sphere raced up past the shining fish and at last broke into the cool night air.

Weybridge and Steevens listened to all this in amazement, hardly knowing what to say. Elstead, however, knew what he had seen, and he could think of nothing else. He was determined to return to the city of lights and learn more about its people. Within months, he had built a second and better sphere. One clear morning it went down into the ocean again, while his friends watched from the deck. They waited for many days, but the sphere never came back to the surface, and Elstead was never seen again.

## Level C1

The Ptarmigan rode at anchor on a sea so calm that it might have been a sheet of polished metal, hundreds of miles from the nearest coast. On her deck that morning stood three men bound by long friendship and, for the moment, by a single shared anxiety: Elstead, Weybridge and Steevens. Of the three, it was Elstead who had brought them there, for he had resolved to do what no human being had ever attempted. He meant to descend five miles to the very floor of the ocean and look with his own eyes upon a world that had lain in darkness since time began.

The vessel for this extraordinary journey was a hollow sphere of steel, its walls as thick as a man's hand. Its few small windows were made of glass so dense that it seemed almost solid. Inside, an electric lamp waited to throw its beam into a night that no sun had ever touched. Beneath the sphere hung a massive weight of lead, attached by a stout rope, and it was this weight that would carry the whole contrivance swiftly downward. The return was entrusted to a piece of clockwork mounted within, which, at an appointed hour, would sever the rope and abandon the lead upon the bottom. Relieved of its burden, the sphere, being lighter than the water it displaced, would then rise of its own accord to the surface.

Steevens, who had never cared for the scheme, made one last attempt to dissuade his friend. "You are trusting your life to a few inches of glass," he said, "and to a clock that has never been tested in such depths. Should anything give way down there, none of us could reach you." Elstead heard him out with the patience of a man whose mind had long been made up. "Every part has been tried and tried again," he replied, "and in any case, the question is not whether it is safe. The question is what lives down there, and I mean to be the first man who knows the answer."

At dawn, he squeezed himself through the narrow door, and his companions screwed it home with a care that betrayed their unease. The sailors swung the sphere outboard and released it; it struck the water, turned over once as though reluctant, and slid out of sight beneath the gentle swell. Weybridge and Steevens had reckoned, with some precision, how long the descent and the return ought to take, and for the first hours they watched in tolerable composure. But noon came and went, and then the slow, glaring afternoon, and the empty surface told them nothing at all.

By sunset the talk among the crew had sunk to murmurs, and nobody cared to say aloud what everyone was thinking. "It is no use pretending," Steevens said at last, without taking his eyes from the water. "Whatever has happened down there, I fear we have lost him." Darkness settled over the sea, and the hours crawled toward midnight, when Weybridge suddenly seized his arm and pointed. Far below, so faint at first that it might have been a trick of tired eyes, a pale light was climbing steadily toward them. It swelled and brightened until, with a rush of foam, the sphere broke the surface and lay rocking under the stars. Within minutes, it had been drawn alongside and the door unscrewed, and out crawled Elstead, chilled through and gray with exhaustion, yet alive, and burning to speak.

What he told them that night, while the ship rocked gently beneath them, sounded less like a scientific report than a dream. The descent, he said, had begun in a blue twilight that deepened through green into a blackness more complete than anything on land. Yet the blackness had not been empty for long, since presently there appeared small points of light, drifting, darting and hanging motionless in the void. They were fish, carrying their own illumination along their sides like the lit portholes of some miniature ship, and some came right up to the glass to inspect the intruder. One, longer than his arm, hung before the window for a long moment, its rows of tiny lights pulsing softly, before it turned and slipped away into the dark. So absorbed was Elstead in watching them that he scarcely noticed how far he had fallen until the sphere settled, almost gently, into the soft black ooze of the ocean floor.

When he switched on his lamp, its beam revealed a silent plain stretching away in every direction, and for a time nothing stirred. Then, at the very edge of the light, shapes began to gather, and he realized with a start that they were walking toward him on two legs. They were not men, though in their upright bearing they were strangely like men; their heads were large, and their great eyes were plainly made for a world without sunlight. They circled the sphere slowly, considering it from every side, and at last one of them came forward and laid its face against the window to study the creature within. There was nothing threatening in that long, solemn gaze, only an immense and patient curiosity, the look of a mind confronting something wholly outside its experience.

Presently, as if a decision had been reached, the creatures seized the rope and began to haul, and Elstead felt the sphere, weight and all, scraping slowly across the ooze. He could do nothing but sit and watch, and so he watched as a faint glow kindled far ahead and grew, little by little, into the shape of a city. Every building in it shone with a soft light of its own, and between them ran streets, walls and towers, all centered upon an edifice that could only be a temple. Out of the doorways poured hundreds of the ocean people, and the sphere was drawn through the streets at the head of a slow and stately procession. Before the temple they formed a wide ring around it, bowed their heads together, and raised a long chant that swelled and faded through the cold water.

It dawned on Elstead, with a wonder that never afterward left him, that he was being worshiped, or rather that the sphere was. For countless ages, he supposed, these people had gazed upward and wondered what lay beyond the black water, and now a shining thing had descended from that unknown heaven. What they meant to do with their marvelous visitor he never learned, for in the middle of the ceremony the clockwork, punctual to the last, cut the rope. The lead remained on the floor of the abyss, and the sphere leapt upward, tearing itself out of that reverent circle before a single hand could stop it. The city dwindled beneath him into a cluster of sparks, then a single glimmer, and then nothing, and he rose past the drifting fish toward the cool night and his friends.

Weybridge and Steevens heard the whole account in silence, and whatever doubts they may have harbored, they kept to themselves. Elstead, for his part, was a changed man, unable to speak of anything but the city of lights and the patient, wondering faces that had peered in at him. He had glimpsed a civilization, he insisted, and it would be nothing short of cowardice to leave it unexplored. Within months, he had built a second sphere, larger and better than the first, and one clear morning his friends watched it slip once more beneath the surface. They waited for many days and many nights, scanning the water for that pale, rising light, but it never came, and Elstead was never seen again.
