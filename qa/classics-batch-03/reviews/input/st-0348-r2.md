# Five Little Peppers and How They Grew

Summary: Five poor but cheerful children and their hardworking mother make a happy life in a little brown house.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): stove, cake, measles

## Level A1

Mrs. Pepper lived in a little old house. She had five children. Ben and Polly were the oldest. Joel and Davie were next. Little Phronsie was the youngest. The children called their mother Mamsie. Their father died when Phronsie was a baby.

The family had very little money. Mamsie worked for other people all day. Ben worked too, and Polly helped at home. But they had a lot of fun.

Mamsie's birthday was near. "Let's make a cake for her," said Polly. "We have only the old stove," said Ben. "We can try," said Polly. The next day, Mamsie went out to work. Then Polly and Ben made a cake. The cake was very good.

At night, Mamsie came home. The cake was on the table. "Look, Mamsie, it is for you!" said Phronsie. Mamsie looked and looked at the cake. "Thank you, my dear children," said Mamsie.

Then the measles came to the little house. Joel, Davie and Phronsie got the measles first. Mamsie took care of them day and night. Then Polly got the measles too. After that, Polly's eyes were not strong. She could not read or work for weeks. Slowly, all the children got well again.

One day, the children met Jasper. Jasper came from a big city. His family had a lot of money. He came to their house many times. Soon all the children loved him.

Jasper's father was Mr. King. Mr. King was an old man. He did not like children. But little Phronsie was kind to him. She sat with him and talked to him. Soon Mr. King loved her very much.

Mr. King had a big city house. He asked all the Peppers to come there. In the big house, Polly learned music. Then Mr. King learned something new. Mamsie was from his family! "This is your home now," said Mr. King. Now the Peppers had a big new home.

## Level A2

Near a small town stood a little brown house. Mrs. Pepper lived there with her five children. The children called her Mamsie. Their father died when Phronsie was a baby. Ben was the oldest, and Polly came next. After them came Joel, Davie and little Phronsie.

The Peppers had very little money. Mamsie made and fixed clothes for people in the town. Ben worked hard for the farmers near the town. Polly kept the house clean and looked after the little ones. They often had very little food. Still, their home was always full of fun and games.

Soon it was almost Mamsie's birthday. Polly wanted to make her a real cake. But the old stove had holes in it, and it often smoked. "That stove is too old," said Ben. "We must try," said Polly. On the big day, Mamsie went out to work early. Then the children quickly made the cake. Polly watched the stove all the time. Joel and Davie brought wood for the fire. At last the cake came out, and it was very good.

Mamsie came home in the evening. The children stood around the table with big smiles. "Happy birthday, Mamsie!" they all said. Mamsie looked at the cake and then at the old stove. "You made this in that old stove?" she asked. "Yes, we did!" said Polly. Mamsie held all her children in her arms.

Then hard days came. Phronsie got the measles, and then Joel and Davie got them. Mamsie and Polly looked after them day and night. Then Polly got the measles too. Her eyes stayed very weak for a long time. The doctor told her to stay in a dark room for weeks. Polly could not read or help at home. The others did her work and told her stories. In the end, everyone got well again.

One day the children met a boy from the city. His name was Jasper, and he had a big dog. Jasper and his father were in the town for a while. His family had a lot of money. Jasper loved the happy little house. He played with the boys and talked with Polly. Soon he was their best friend.

Jasper's father was an old man named Mr. King. He was often angry, and many people were afraid of him. But little Phronsie was not afraid. She sat on his knee and talked to him. The old man began to smile again. Soon he loved Phronsie like his own child.

Later, Mr. King asked the whole family to his city house. It was very big, and the Peppers stayed there. Polly learned to play the piano. The boys went to a good school. Then Mr. King found out something very surprising. Mamsie was a relative of his family! "This is your home now," Mr. King told Mamsie. So the five little Peppers grew up in a big, happy home.

## Level B1

At the edge of a quiet country town there was a little brown house. Inside it lived Mrs. Pepper and her five children, and they were poor but cheerful. The children called their mother Mamsie, and she was the center of their world. Their father died when Phronsie was still a baby. Ben was the oldest, a steady boy who worked hard. Polly came next, and she was quick, kind and full of ideas. After her came Joel, who was loud and always hungry, and gentle Davie. Little Phronsie was the youngest, and everyone loved her.

Mamsie sewed clothes for people in the town from morning until night. Ben cut wood and helped the farmers whenever they needed an extra pair of hands. Polly cooked, cleaned and looked after the younger ones while Mamsie was working. Their meals were simple, and new shoes were a rare treat. But the little brown house was always full of laughter, stories and games.

Mamsie's birthday was coming soon, and Polly had a big plan. "Let's bake her a real cake," she whispered to Ben. Ben shook his head. "The old stove has holes in it, Polly. It smokes and burns everything." "Then we must be very careful," Polly said. On the morning of the birthday, Mamsie went out to work as usual. As soon as she left, the children got to work too. Polly mixed the cake while Joel and Davie carried in wood. Ben kept the fire low, and Phronsie watched with wide eyes. The stove smoked, and Polly watched the cake every minute. At last, it came out brown and whole, and the children danced around the kitchen.

That evening Mamsie came home tired. The children were waiting by the table with the cake in the middle. "Happy birthday, Mamsie!" they all shouted together. Mamsie stared at the cake, and then at the old stove. "You made this in that stove?" she asked softly. "We did," said Polly proudly. Mamsie hugged each of them, and her eyes were wet with happy tears.

Not long after that, hard days came to the little brown house. Phronsie caught the measles first, and then Joel and Davie caught them too. Mamsie and Polly nursed them day and night, and slowly they got better. Then Polly fell ill with the measles herself. The illness left her eyes very weak. The doctor told her to stay in a dark room, with no reading and no sewing. For busy Polly, sitting still in the dark was the hardest thing of all. The others did her jobs and told her stories to cheer her up. Week by week her eyes grew stronger, and at last she was well again.

Around that time the children met a boy named Jasper. Jasper was rich, and he was staying in the town with his father. He had a big dog and fine clothes, but he was often lonely. In the little brown house he found something he did not have at home. There was noise, laughter and a family that shared everything. Jasper visited again and again, and soon he was the Peppers' best friend.

Jasper's father, Mr. King, was a rich old gentleman with a terrible temper. Most people were afraid of him, and he did not like noisy children. Little Phronsie, however, was not afraid of him at all. She climbed onto his knee and talked to him like an old friend. Slowly the cross old man began to smile again. Soon he loved Phronsie as much as he loved his own son.

Later, Mr. King invited the whole Pepper family to his large house in the city. There Polly learned to play the piano, and the boys went to a good school. Then one day Mr. King discovered something that surprised everyone. Mamsie was a relative of his own family! Mr. King took Mamsie's hand. "You are not guests here anymore. This is your home now." So the five little Peppers grew up in a big, warm house full of love.

## Level B2

On the edge of a sleepy country town stood a small brown house, old and plain but always neat. Everyone called it the Little Brown House, and inside it lived Mrs. Pepper and her five children. Her husband had died when the youngest was only a baby, and since then money had always been short. The children called her Mamsie. To them, she was the wisest and bravest person in the world.

Ben, the oldest, was serious and dependable, and he already did a man's work for the farmers nearby. Polly, who came next, was the heart of the house: quick, cheerful and always ready with a new idea. Then came Joel, loud, restless and always hungry, and Davie, who followed Joel everywhere but was much gentler. Little Phronsie was the baby of the family, and nobody could ever stay cross with her for long.

Mamsie earned what she could by sewing for the people of the town, often working late into the night. Polly kept the house, cooked the simple meals and looked after the little ones while Mamsie was busy. There was never enough money for books, new shoes or schooling, and the children knew it. Still, they rarely complained, because the Little Brown House was full of jokes, games and songs. Polly had one secret wish of her own, which was to learn music someday. But there was no hope of a piano in a house like theirs.

As Mamsie's birthday drew near, Polly decided that this year her mother would have a real cake. Ben frowned. "With what? That old stove has more holes than iron, and it smokes whenever we light it." "Then we will simply have to watch it very closely," Polly answered. On the morning of the birthday, Mamsie left for her day's work as usual, suspecting nothing. The moment the door closed, the house turned into a busy little bakery. Polly mixed the batter, Joel and Davie carried in wood, and Ben kept the fire steady. Phronsie was given the important job of watching, and she took it very seriously. All day long the children kept glancing at the road, afraid that Mamsie might come home early. The stove smoked and sulked, and Polly hardly took her eyes off it for a minute. At last the cake came out, brown and whole, and the children could hardly believe their luck.

That evening Mamsie came home tired and found all five children standing round the table with the cake in the middle. "Happy birthday, Mamsie!" they cried together. For a moment she could not speak; she simply looked from the cake to the old stove and back again. "You baked this, in that stove?" she asked at last. "Every bit of it," Polly said proudly, and Mamsie gathered them all into her arms. Later they shared the cake around the table, and Mamsie said it was the finest she had ever tasted.

Not long afterward, the measles came to the Little Brown House. Phronsie fell ill first, and then Joel and Davie came down with it too. Mamsie had to spend her days caring for them, which meant less sewing and even less money. Mamsie and Polly nursed them through long days and nights, and one by one they began to recover. Just as everyone was sighing with relief, Polly herself caught the measles. Her case was worse than the others', and the illness left her eyes badly weakened. The kind village doctor said she had to stay in a darkened room until her eyes healed. There could be no reading and no sewing, not even for a minute. For busy Polly, who could never sit still, this was harder than the illness itself. The others took over her jobs, and the boys invented stories and games to keep her spirits up. Slowly her eyes grew stronger, and in the end she could see as clearly as before.

It was around this time that the Peppers met Jasper. He was a well-dressed boy from the city who was staying in the town with his father. He had a big dog that went everywhere with him. For all his money, Jasper was lonely, and the noisy, loving Pepper household seemed wonderful to him. He loved Ben's quiet strength, Joel's jokes and Polly's endless plans. Even his dog seemed to feel at home in the little kitchen. Jasper began to visit the Little Brown House whenever he could. Before long he was the closest friend the children had ever had.

Jasper's father, Mr. King, was a very different matter. He was a rich old gentleman with a sharp tongue and a famous temper, and most people kept out of his way. At first, he grumbled that the Peppers were far too poor and far too noisy to be friends with his son. Little Phronsie, however, had never learned to be afraid of anyone. She climbed onto the old man's knee and chatted to him as if they were old friends. To everyone's surprise, Mr. King softened, and soon he could not refuse her anything.

In time Mr. King invited the whole Pepper family to his large house in the city. There Polly, who loved music, finally learned to play the piano, and the boys went to a good school. In the evenings the whole family gathered together, and the big house was no longer quiet. Then Mr. King made a discovery that astonished everyone: Mamsie was a relative of his own family. Mr. King took her hand. "You are not guests in this house anymore. This is your home, for as long as you want it." And so the five little Peppers, who had once had so little, grew up surrounded by comfort, learning and love.

## Level C1

At the far edge of a drowsy country town, where the road gave up its air of importance and wandered off into the fields, stood a small brown house. It was old and plain, and its roof had seen better days, but the townspeople called it the Little Brown House with a certain fondness. Inside lived Mrs. Pepper, a widow, and her five children, whom she was raising on courage, hard work and very little else. Her husband had died while the youngest was still a baby, and since then every coin in the house had been earned by Mamsie's own needle.

To the children she was simply Mamsie, and in their eyes she could mend anything, from a torn coat to a heavy heart. Ben, the eldest, was a serious, steady boy who had long ago taken on a man's share of the work, chopping wood and helping the farmers whenever they would have him. Polly, next in line, was the bright engine of the household: quick to flare up, quicker to laugh, and forever brimming with schemes. Behind her came Joel, loud, restless and endlessly hungry, and Davie, who trailed after Joel like a gentle shadow. Last of all was little Phronsie, the baby, whose solemn face could melt even Joel's worst temper.

Money was a constant, quiet worry. Mamsie sewed for half the town, bending over other people's clothes long after the candle should have been put out. Polly cooked, swept and minded the little ones, turning the plainest meals into a kind of feast through sheer cheerfulness. Books, schooling and new shoes seemed to belong to some other, luckier family. Yet nobody passing the Little Brown House in the evening would have guessed how poor it was, for it rang with laughter, songs and the endless arguments of children at play. Polly nursed one secret longing above all others: to learn music, though a piano was as far beyond the Peppers' reach as the moon.

It was Polly who decided that Mamsie's birthday deserved a real cake. Ben, practical as ever, nodded toward the stove in the corner, a battered old thing with holes in its sides that smoked at the slightest excuse. "Polly, that stove can barely boil water. How are you going to bake a cake in it?" "Carefully," said Polly, and that was the end of the discussion.

On the morning of the birthday, Mamsie set off for her day's work as usual, quite unaware of the plot being hatched behind her. The door had hardly closed when the kitchen burst into action. Polly measured and stirred with the seriousness of a general, while Joel and Davie hauled in wood. Ben coaxed the fire to burn neither too high nor too low, as if he were taming a moody animal. Phronsie, given the honorable post of watcher, never once took her eyes off the oven door. All day the children kept one anxious eye on the road, half convinced that Mamsie would choose this, of all days, to come home early. The old stove sulked and smoked as though it resented the whole enterprise, and Polly hovered over it for hours, scarcely daring to breathe. When the cake finally emerged, brown and whole and smelling of everything good, the children stood around it in reverent silence.

Mamsie came home that evening worn out from the long day, and stopped short in the doorway. Five faces shone at her over the table, and in the middle of it sat the cake. "Happy birthday, Mamsie!" they shouted, all at once and not quite together. She looked at the cake, then at the smoking stove, then at the cake again, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. "In that stove?" Mamsie asked at last, her voice not entirely steady. "Every crumb," said Polly, glowing, and Mamsie opened her arms wide enough to hold all five of them at once. Later, when the cake had been shared out to the last crumb, Mamsie declared that no cake in the world had ever tasted half so fine.

Hard times, however, have a way of following happy ones. That season the measles found its way to the Little Brown House, and Phronsie was the first to fall ill, followed closely by Joel and Davie. Mamsie could take in less sewing while they were ill, and the little store of money shrank alarmingly. Mamsie and Polly nursed them through the long, restless days and nights, until one by one the patients grew cross and demanding, which everyone knew was a sign of recovery. Then, just when the house had begun to breathe again, Polly caught the measles herself. Hers was the worst case of all, and when the fever passed it left her eyes so weak that the village doctor looked very serious indeed. She must stay in a darkened room, he said, with no reading, no sewing and no work of any kind, until her eyes had healed. For a girl who had never sat still in her life, it was a harder sentence than the illness itself. The others rallied round her, taking over her chores without complaint, while the boys made up stories and silly games to coax a laugh out of her in the dark. Slowly, week by week, her eyes grew stronger, until at last she could step back into the sunlight and see the world as clearly as before.

It was not long after this that a newcomer entered their lives. Jasper was a well-dressed boy from the city, staying in the town with his father, and he went nowhere without his big, devoted dog. For all his fine clothes and money, he was a lonely boy, and the crowded, noisy warmth of the Pepper household struck him as the most wonderful thing he had ever seen. He admired Ben's quiet strength, laughed at Joel's jokes and listened to Polly's plans as if they were matters of state. Even his dog, it seemed, preferred the cramped little kitchen to any grand room in the city. He came once, then again, and then as often as he could, until the children could hardly remember a time before Jasper.

His father was quite another story. Mr. King was a wealthy old gentleman whose temper was famous, and servants, shopkeepers and even his own relations had learned to tread softly around him. At first, he grumbled loudly that his son had fallen in with a crowd of poor and noisy children. Little Phronsie, however, had never learned to tread softly around anyone. She climbed onto his knee as calmly as if it were a kitchen chair and chatted to him about whatever came into her head. To the astonishment of everyone, the fierce old man softened under her small hand, and before long there was nothing he would refuse her.

In time Mr. King invited the whole Pepper family to his large house in the city, a place of wide halls and tall windows that seemed to the children like a palace. There Polly, whose love of music had always been a secret ache, at last sat down before a real piano and learned to play, while the boys went to a good school. In the evenings the whole family gathered in the great rooms, and for the first time in years the great house was filled with noise. Then came the discovery that turned a kindness into a homecoming: Mamsie, it turned out, was a relative of the King family. Mr. King took both her hands in his. "You are not guests in this house, my dear. You never were, and it is your home now for as long as you choose." And so the five little Peppers, who had begun with a broken stove and a cake made out of hope, grew up in a house full of comfort, learning and love.
