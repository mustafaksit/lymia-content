# Gulliver in the Land of Giants

Summary: A traveler from England lands in a country of giants, where a kind girl looks after him.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): giant, giants, box

## Level A1

Gulliver was a man from England. He often went to far places by ship. Once, his ship stopped near a new land. Gulliver and some men looked for water. Gulliver walked away on his own. Then the men saw a giant! They ran back to the ship. The ship left without Gulliver.

Everything in this land was very big. The plants were as high as a house. Soon a giant found Gulliver. Gulliver was as big as the giant's hand. The giant took him home.

The giant had a little girl. Her name was Glumdalclitch. She was nine years old, but very big. She loved the little man at once. She made a small bed for him. Gulliver learned her language from her. Soon the two friends talked every day.

Her father wanted money. He took Gulliver to many places. People paid money to see the little man. Gulliver had to talk and walk all day. He got weak and did not eat much.

Then the queen of the land saw Gulliver. She liked him and bought him. Glumdalclitch came with him to help. The queen gave Gulliver a big box. It was like a little room. It had a bed and a table.

The king liked Gulliver too. Gulliver told him all about England. The king asked many questions. He did not like everything about England.

Gulliver lived there for two years. Then the king took everyone to the sea. Gulliver went too, in his box. A boy put it down by the water. Then a very big bird came down. It took the box up into the air! Far away, it let the box go. The box fell into the water.

Gulliver called for help. At last, men from a ship found him. They opened the box and helped him out. These men were small, like Gulliver. But Gulliver talked to them very loudly! He still thought of the giants. The ship took him home to England.

## Level A2

Gulliver was a doctor from England, and he loved the sea. He often worked at sea and visited far countries. On one long trip, his ship came to a new land. Gulliver and a few men went on the land to find water.

Gulliver walked away to look around. When he came back, the men were in their small boat. A giant was behind them in the water! The men got away, and their ship left. Gulliver was alone, so he ran into a field. The plants there were higher than a house.

Workers came into the field, and one almost stepped on him. Gulliver called out, and the giant picked him up. He gave Gulliver to the farmer. The man looked at him for a long time. Then he took the little man home.

The farmer's daughter was nine years old. Her name was Glumdalclitch, and she was very kind. She made Gulliver a bed and new clothes. Every day she taught him words in her language. Gulliver loved her and called her his little teacher.

Soon the farmer had an idea. He took Gulliver to the market in the next town. People paid money to see the little man walk and talk. After this, the farmer took him to the big city. Gulliver had to do his show many times a day. Soon he could not eat or sleep well.

Then the queen asked to see him. She liked him, so she bought him for a lot of money. "Please let Glumdalclitch stay with me," Gulliver said. The queen agreed, so the girl came too. A worker built Gulliver a box with a bed and a table. It was his own little room.

Life with the queen was not always easy. A very small man worked for the queen. He did not like Gulliver and often made fun of him.

The king often talked with Gulliver about England. Gulliver told him about its history and its laws. The king asked a lot of questions. In the end he did not like everything. "Your people fight too much," the king said.

Gulliver stayed for two years, but he wanted to go home. One summer the king and queen went to the sea. Gulliver went with them in a travel box. A boy carried the box down to the water. He put it down and walked away. Suddenly a very big bird came down and took the box. It went high into the air and far over the sea. Then it let the box go. The box fell into the water, but it did not go down.

After a few hours, a ship came near. The men on the ship pulled the box out of the water. They opened it, and Gulliver came out. He talked to them very loudly, and they did not understand why. In his head, he was still among the giants. The ship took him home to England and to his family.

## Level B1

Gulliver was a ship's doctor from England who could never stay at home for long. Only two months after his last long journey, he went to sea again. For weeks the ship sailed east, until a terrible storm pushed it far off course. When the wind finally dropped, the sailors saw an unknown coast in front of them. They had almost no fresh water left, so the captain sent a small boat to land.

Gulliver went with the sailors, but he soon walked off alone to explore. When he returned to the beach, he saw something that made his heart stop. The sailors were rowing back to the ship as fast as possible. Behind them, a giant was walking through the sea after their boat. The men reached the ship in time, and it sailed away without Gulliver.

Gulliver ran inland and hid in a field of corn that was taller than a church. Soon a group of giant workers came to cut the corn. One of them nearly stepped on Gulliver, so he shouted as loudly as he could. The giant picked him up carefully between his finger and thumb. Then he carried the strange little man to his master, the farmer.

The farmer was surprised and curious, and he decided to take Gulliver home. His wife cried out at the sight of him, but she soon got used to him. Their daughter, Glumdalclitch, was nine years old and very clever with her hands. She made Gulliver a bed inside her doll's cradle and sewed shirts for him. Every day she pointed at things and taught him the words of her language. Gulliver called her his little nurse, and they became true friends.

Before long, a neighbor told the farmer that he could make money from Gulliver. So the farmer took him to the market in the nearest town. Gulliver stood on a table, walked up and down, and waved his tiny hat. Crowds paid to see him, and the farmer decided to travel to the capital city. There Gulliver had to perform ten times a day, and he grew thin and weak. Glumdalclitch was worried, because she could see that her friend was suffering.

News of the little man reached the palace, and the queen wanted to see him. She liked him so much that she offered the farmer a large bag of gold. The farmer accepted, because he believed Gulliver did not have long to live. Gulliver asked the queen for one favor: to let Glumdalclitch stay with him. The queen agreed, and the girl became his nurse and teacher at court. A carpenter built Gulliver a wooden box, which was like a small bedroom.

Life at court was mostly pleasant, but it had its dangers. The queen's dwarf was jealous of Gulliver, who was even smaller than he was. Once, as a joke, the dwarf dropped him into a large bowl of cream. Gulliver swam until someone pulled him out, and the dwarf got into trouble. Another time, wasps as big as birds flew around his breakfast. Gulliver bravely drove them away, and the whole court praised him.

The king was a wise man, and he often invited Gulliver to talk. Gulliver proudly described England, its parliament, its courts and its long history. The king listened carefully and wrote down many questions. The next day, he asked about money, wars and the honesty of judges. At last, he picked Gulliver up and stroked him gently. "I am afraid many of your people love power more than goodness," he said. Gulliver felt hurt, but he could not find a good answer.

After two years, Gulliver still missed his own country and his family. One summer the king and queen traveled to the south coast, and Gulliver went with them. Glumdalclitch was ill, so a young page carried his travel box down to the beach. The page put the box on the rocks and went off to look for birds' eggs. Gulliver was sleeping inside when the box suddenly rose into the air. An eagle was holding it by the iron ring on its top. The eagle flew high over the sea, but other eagles were chasing it. Suddenly it let go, and the box fell into the waves.

Luckily, the box was strong, and it stayed on top of the water. Hours later, a passing ship noticed the box and came closer. The sailors tied a rope to the ring and pulled it to their ship. One of them cut a hole in the roof, and Gulliver climbed out at last. The sailors looked as tiny as dolls to him, so he spoke to them very loudly. The captain thought he was mad, until Gulliver showed him things from the giants. Months later, Gulliver arrived home in England. For many days, he still felt like a tiny man among giants.

## Level B2

Gulliver, a ship's doctor from England, had been home for only two months when the sea called him back. Early one summer he sailed on a merchant ship bound for India. The journey went well until a fierce storm drove the vessel far to the north and east, into waters no map described. When the weather cleared, the crew sighted an unknown coast. Their fresh water was running low, so the captain sent a boat ashore.

Gulliver landed with the sailors but soon wandered off on his own, curious to see what kind of country it was. He found nothing but bare rocks, and he was walking slowly back to the shore when he noticed something alarming. The sailors were already in their boat, rowing toward the ship as if their lives depended on it. Behind them strode a giant, wading through the sea with steps as long as a ship. The men reached the ship just in time, and it sailed away, leaving Gulliver alone on the beach. Gulliver later learned that this country of giants was called Brobdingnag.

Frightened, he ran inland and hid among stalks of corn that rose higher than church towers. Before long, a group of enormous workers arrived with tools to harvest the crop, moving closer with every stroke. When one of them was about to step on him, Gulliver shouted at the top of his voice. The giant stopped, looked down, and picked him up between a finger and a thumb, the way a person lifts a beetle. Gulliver bowed, spoke politely in several languages and pointed at his mouth to show that he was hungry. Amused, the worker carried him to his master, a wealthy farmer, who studied the tiny visitor with great interest.

At dinner that evening, Gulliver stood on the table among plates as wide as ponds. The farmer's wife cried out at first, but soon she was feeding him small pieces of bread and meat. It was their nine-year-old daughter, Glumdalclitch, who truly took care of him. She turned her doll's cradle into his bed and sewed him shirts from the finest cloth she could find. She also became his teacher, pointing at objects and repeating their names until he could speak her language. Gulliver called her his little nurse, and he never forgot how kind she was to him.

Unfortunately, a neighbor suggested that the strange creature could make the family rich. The farmer took Gulliver to the market in the nearest town and showed him in an inn for money. Gulliver marched up and down on a table, drank from a thimble, and waved a tiny sword for the crowds. Glumdalclitch, who stood beside him, gave him instructions and kept the excited children at a safe distance. The shows were so successful that the farmer decided to travel to the capital and exhibit him there. In the great city, Gulliver performed ten times a day, and the endless work left him exhausted and thin. His young friend watched him grow weaker and secretly wept for him.

The farmer believed Gulliver would not live long, so he was delighted when a message arrived from the palace. The queen had heard about the little man, and when she saw him, she was completely charmed. She paid the farmer a thousand pieces of gold, and Gulliver politely asked for one thing in return. He wanted Glumdalclitch to stay with him, because she had always treated him with such care. The queen gladly agreed, and the girl became his nurse and tutor at court. The royal carpenter was ordered to build him a wooden box, sixteen feet square, which served as his bedroom.

Court life brought honor, but it also brought small dangers that seemed very large to Gulliver. The queen's dwarf, who had never before met anyone smaller than himself, was bitterly jealous. One day at dinner he picked Gulliver up and dropped him into a silver bowl of cream. Gulliver swam until he was rescued, and the dwarf was punished for his rude joke. On another morning, a swarm of wasps the size of birds buzzed around his breakfast cake. Gulliver bravely chased them off with his knife, and the ladies of the court admired his courage.

The king, a thoughtful and learned man, enjoyed long conversations with his small guest. Gulliver proudly described England, its parliament and courts of law, its trade, and a century of its history. The king listened in silence and made careful notes, and over the next few days he asked dozens of questions. How were judges chosen, and were they always honest? Why did a nation spend so much money on wars? Finally, the king lifted Gulliver into his hand and stroked him gently, like a pet. "From what you have told me," he said, "many of your leaders seem to care more for power than for virtue." Gulliver's pride was hurt, yet he could find no convincing reply.

Two years passed, and although Gulliver was treated well, he longed for his family and for people his own size. In the third summer he went with the king and queen on a journey to the south coast. Glumdalclitch fell ill on the way, so a young page carried Gulliver's travel box down to the shore. The page set the box on the rocks and went off to look for birds' eggs, while Gulliver dozed inside. He woke with a violent jerk and realized that the box was rising swiftly into the air. An eagle had seized it by the iron ring on its roof and was carrying it out over the ocean. Soon other eagles attacked the bird, and suddenly the box was falling. It struck the sea with a mighty splash, sank for a moment, and then bobbed back to the surface.

For several hours Gulliver drifted, waving a handkerchief through a small opening and calling for help. At last a passing English ship spotted the floating box and sent sailors to fasten a rope to its ring. A carpenter sawed a hole in the roof, and Gulliver climbed out into the daylight. He spoke so loudly that the sailors stared at him, and to his eyes they looked as small as dolls. The captain feared the poor man had lost his reason, until Gulliver showed him a gold ring from the queen. When he finally reached home, he bent down to greet his family, as though they were the tiny ones. It was a long time before the world around him looked the right size again.

## Level C1

Gulliver had barely spent two months by his own fireside before the old restlessness returned, and he signed on as ship's doctor aboard a merchant vessel bound for India. For a time the voyage was uneventful, but beyond the Cape a storm of unusual violence seized the ship and drove her, day after day, into seas no chart could name. When the wind at last relented, a strange, mountainous coast lay before them, and with fresh water nearly gone, the captain had little choice but to send a boat ashore.

Gulliver, who never could resist a new country, went with the shore party and then strayed from it, climbing a rocky slope in the hope of finding a stream or a village. He found neither, only bare stone and the silence of an empty land, and he was making his way back down to the beach when he stopped short in disbelief. The boat was already pulling hard for the ship, and close behind it, striding through the surf as a man might cross a puddle, came a giant. Whether the creature gave up the chase or simply lost interest, Gulliver never learned; he saw only that the boat escaped, and that the ship sailed without him. It was his first glimpse of the people of Brobdingnag, as he would later learn to call their country.

Panic sent him running inland until he plunged into a field of corn whose stalks rose above him like the pillars of a cathedral. He had not hidden there long before the ground began to tremble, and a team of enormous laborers appeared, sweeping their blades through the crop with a noise like distant thunder. As one of them raised a foot that threatened to end his travels altogether, Gulliver let out the loudest cry of his life. The giant paused, peered down, and with the cautious curiosity of someone examining an unfamiliar insect, picked him up between finger and thumb. Gulliver, determined to appear harmless, bowed, joined his hands in a gesture of appeal, and addressed his captor in every language he knew, none of which produced the slightest effect. Intrigued, the laborer carried his find to his master, a prosperous farmer, who studied the little man for a long while before deciding to take him home.

There, at a dinner table that seemed to Gulliver as broad as a parade ground, the household gathered to inspect its new curiosity. The farmer's wife shrieked at first, as she might have at a spider, yet within the hour she was offering him crumbs of bread and drops of broth with evident tenderness. It was the youngest of the family, however, who claimed him as her own: a girl of nine named Glumdalclitch, small for her age, though still a towering figure to him. She fitted out her doll's cradle as his bed, stitched shirts for him from the softest cloth in the house, and appointed herself his teacher. Object by object, she named the world for him, until the strange syllables of her tongue began to make sense. Gulliver, for his part, called her his little nurse, and it is fair to say that in that vast country she was the one creature he trusted without reserve.

Such happiness was not destined to last, for a neighbor who had seen the little man whispered to the farmer that a fortune stood upon his kitchen table. On the next market day Gulliver was carried to the nearest town and displayed in an upstairs room of an inn, packed with paying customers. He marched, saluted, drank from a thimble, and flourished a sword no longer than a needle, while Glumdalclitch, perched on a stool beside him, called out each performance in turn. Encouraged by the takings, the farmer resolved to try his luck in the capital, and on that long journey, and afterwards in the city, Gulliver performed ten times a day. The work wore him down until he grew pale and thin, and the girl who had made his cradle watched him fade with a grief she was too young to hide.

Rescue, of a sort, arrived in the shape of a summons from the palace, where the queen, having heard rumors of the miniature man, wished to judge them for herself. She was enchanted within minutes, and when she asked whether he would like to live at court, Gulliver bowed and accepted, provided his little nurse might stay with him. A thousand pieces of gold changed hands, the farmer departed well satisfied, and Glumdalclitch was installed at court as Gulliver's guardian and tutor. The royal carpenter was set to work on a box sixteen feet square, furnished with a bed, chairs and a cabinet, which became at once his bedroom and his traveling home.

Court life brought him honor and comfort, but it also brought hazards that a person of ordinary size would scarcely have noticed. The queen's dwarf, who until then had enjoyed the distinction of being the smallest person in the kingdom, could not forgive Gulliver for taking it from him. Seizing his chance one evening at supper, the dwarf dropped his rival into a silver bowl of cream, where Gulliver swam gamely until a servant fished him out. The prank cost its author, to Gulliver's quiet satisfaction, his place at the queen's side. One summer morning a cloud of wasps as large as partridges descended on his breakfast. Gulliver, drawing his little knife, drove them off with such spirit that the ladies of the court talked of nothing else for a week.

It was the king, however, whose company Gulliver came to value most, for the monarch was a man of learning and of an unusually patient temper. Over several audiences Gulliver painted a glowing portrait of England: its parliament and its courts, its trade and its learning, and the proud story of the past hundred years. The king listened without interrupting, took careful notes, and then, over the days that followed, returned with a series of questions that grew steadily more uncomfortable. How were judges chosen, and did they ever favor the rich? Why should a nation at peace need so large an army? At last the king lifted his guest into the palm of his hand and, stroking him as one might stroke a kitten, delivered a judgment that Gulliver never quite forgave. "I am inclined to think," he said gently, "that from your own account, a good many of your countrymen care far more for power than for virtue." Gulliver's patriotic pride was sorely stung, though it did not escape him, in later years, that he had found no answer the king could not dismiss.

Two years slipped by, and although he wanted for nothing, Gulliver ached for his family and for people who did not have to stoop to speak to him. In the third summer he accompanied the king and queen on a royal tour of the southern coast, traveling as always in his box. Glumdalclitch fell ill on the journey and was confined to her bed, so a young page was charged with carrying Gulliver down to the shore to breathe the sea air. The boy set the box on the rocks and wandered off in search of birds' eggs, leaving its occupant dozing behind a half-closed window. Gulliver woke to a violent lurch and the unmistakable sensation of rising; something, he realized, had seized the iron ring on his roof. It was an eagle, as he later guessed, and when rival birds gave chase, it let go of its prize far out above the open sea. The box plunged, struck the water with a force that shook him from head to foot, and then, to his astonishment, rose again and floated.

He drifted for hours, waving a handkerchief tied to a stick through a hole in the ceiling and calling out until his voice failed. Toward evening, he felt the box being drawn along, and soon after he heard, in plain English, a sailor's voice asking whether anyone was inside. The ship's carpenter cut an opening in the roof, a ladder came down, and Gulliver emerged into the daylight among men who seemed to him no larger than dolls. He addressed them at the top of his voice, as he had grown used to doing, and his rescuers exchanged anxious glances, convinced that the ordeal had unsettled his mind. Only when he showed the captain a gold ring from the queen and a few other curiosities from that land did the skeptics begin, however reluctantly, to believe him.

Months later, when he walked up the road to his own front door, Gulliver stooped low to embrace his family, half afraid that he might tread on them. His wife and children stared at him in bewilderment, and for many weeks afterwards he found it impossible to shake off the feeling that he was a giant among little people.
