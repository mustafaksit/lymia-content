# The Flying Stars

Summary: At a Christmas party, three famous diamonds vanish during a children's play, and a quiet priest finds the thief.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): diamonds, pantomime, clown

## Level A1

It was Christmas in Putney. Ruby lived there with her father. Their house was big and full of people. Father Brown came too. He was a small, kind priest.

Sir Leopold had a lot of money. He gave Ruby a small box. In the box were three white diamonds. People called them the Flying Stars.

A young man named Crook was there too. Crook loved Ruby, and Ruby liked him. But her father did not like Crook. Crook said bad things about men with money.

Late in the day, a tall man came. He came with a friend of the family. No one in the house knew him well. "Let us play a pantomime!" he said. The tall man was the clown. Sir Leopold and Crook had parts too.

The clown ran and fell and ran again. The children loved him. But no one watched his hands.

After the play, Sir Leopold opened the box. The diamonds were not there! All the people looked at Crook. "Crook took them!" said Ruby's father. "No, I did not!" said Crook.

Father Brown thought for a long time. Then he looked for the clown. But the clown was not in the house. Father Brown went outside into the night. The clown sat high up in a tree. "I know you, Flambeau," said Father Brown.

Flambeau took other people's things for many years. He wanted the Flying Stars. So he came as a friend. He started the pantomime and played the clown. A clown can run into people. No one asks why. So in the game, he took the diamonds.

"You are still young," said Father Brown. "This life only goes down and down. Stop now, and be a good man." Flambeau said nothing for a long time. Then he let the diamonds fall. Flambeau went away into the night.

Father Brown went back with the diamonds. Crook was a good man after all. Ruby took his hand.

## Level A2

It was Christmas Day in Putney. Ruby lived there in a large house with her father. That day the house was full of friends and family. Father Brown, a little priest with a kind face, was there too.

Sir Leopold arrived late, and everyone came to meet him. He was a man with a great deal of money. He had a Christmas present for Ruby. It was a small box with three diamonds inside. People called them the Flying Stars. "Many bad men want these stones," said Sir Leopold. "Even Flambeau wants them." Flambeau took other people's things, and the police could never catch him. Sir Leopold closed the box and kept it close to him.

A young man named Crook was also at the party. He loved Ruby, and Ruby loved him. But her father did not like him. Crook often said hard words about people with a lot of money. So Sir Leopold did not like him either.

Late in the day, Ruby's uncle came with a friend. The friend was tall and full of fun. No one in the house knew him well. "Let's play a pantomime for the children!" said the friend. Everyone loved the idea. The friend chose the parts for everyone. He was the clown, and Crook and Ruby had small parts. Sir Leopold played a policeman. He did not want it, but the clown asked again and again.

The pantomime was loud and a lot of fun. The clown ran, fell, danced and ran again. He played games with the poor policeman all the time. The children laughed and laughed. But no one watched the clown's hands.

After the play, Sir Leopold opened his box. There was nothing in it! Everyone looked at Crook. "You took them, Crook!" said Ruby's father. "You hate people with money!" "I did not take them!" said Crook. His face went red, and Ruby held his arm.

Father Brown did not look at Crook. He thought about the clown. "Where is our clown?" he asked. No one knew. So Father Brown went out into the dark garden.

Someone sat high in a tree near the garden wall. It was the clown. "Good evening, Flambeau," said Father Brown. The clown did not move. Father Brown understood everything now. Flambeau wanted the Flying Stars. So he came to the house as the uncle's friend. A clown can run into people, and no one asks why. So during the game, he took the diamonds from Sir Leopold.

Father Brown spoke to him in a low voice. "You are still young, Flambeau," he said. "A good man can stay good for many years. But a bad life never stays the same. It only goes down and down. Leave it now, while you still can." For a long time, Flambeau said nothing. Then the three diamonds fell from the tree. They landed at the priest's feet. Flambeau went over the wall and into the night.

Father Brown carried the Flying Stars back into the house. Now everyone knew the truth about Crook. Ruby smiled and held his hand.

## Level B1

It was Christmas in Putney, and the house of Ruby's father was full of people. Her father, an old soldier, usually liked quiet evenings. Tonight, though, he was happy to have guests. One of them was Father Brown, a small, round priest with a gentle face. He was an old friend of the family, and he liked to sit and watch.

Late in the afternoon, Sir Leopold arrived in a heavy fur coat. He was a very rich man, and he had a Christmas present for Ruby. He opened a small box, and everyone came closer to look. Inside lay three diamonds, as bright as ice. "People call them the Flying Stars," Sir Leopold said proudly. "Thieves all over Europe want them. Even Flambeau, the great French thief, is looking for them, I hear." Then he closed the box and put it back into the pocket of his coat.

Ruby was not only thinking about diamonds. She was also looking at a young man called Crook, who was standing near the fire. Crook wrote for the newspapers and had strong ideas. He often spoke against rich men, and he always wore a bright red tie. Ruby's father did not like him much, and Sir Leopold liked him even less. But Ruby liked him very much.

Soon Ruby's uncle arrived, a loud, cheerful man who loved games. He brought a tall friend with him, and nobody in the house knew this friend. The stranger was charming and quick, and he had an idea at once. "Let's do a pantomime for the children!" he said. The uncle loved the idea, and in a few minutes the friend planned everything. He chose to be the clown himself. Crook got the part of a dancer, and Ruby played a pretty girl. The uncle played a silly old man. Sir Leopold had to be the policeman. He did not want the part, but the clown talked and laughed until he agreed.

It was a wild, happy show. The clown was wonderful. He fell over chairs, ran up and down, and played tricks on the poor policeman. Sir Leopold looked hot and cross, but the children laughed until they cried. Father Brown watched quietly from his corner. Everyone was watching the clown's funny face, and nobody was watching his hands.

After the show, Sir Leopold reached into his coat and opened the little box. His face went white. The box was empty. "The Flying Stars!" he cried. "They are gone!" At once everyone turned to Crook. "It was you," said Ruby's father. "You hate rich men, and you were next to him all evening." "That is not true," said Crook, and his face became as red as his tie. Ruby took his arm and held it tightly.

Father Brown did not join the noise. He was thinking. A pantomime, he thought, is the perfect place for a thief. A clown can run into anyone and put his hands everywhere, and nobody asks why. And this clown was a stranger. "Where is our clown?" asked Father Brown quietly. Nobody knew. So the little priest went out through the back door into the dark, snowy garden.

The moon was shining on the snow. At the end of the garden stood a tall tree. High in its branches sat a figure in a clown's white clothes. "Good evening, Flambeau," said Father Brown. The clown did not move, but the priest went on in his calm voice. He explained everything. Flambeau came to the house as the uncle's friend, and he chose the pantomime on purpose. While everyone was laughing, he took the diamonds from Sir Leopold's pocket.

Then Father Brown spoke about something more important. "You are still young, Flambeau, and there is still good in you. A man can stay at one level of goodness for a long time. But nobody can stay at one level of evil. That road only goes down. Come away from it now, while you still can."

For a long time there was no sound at all. Then something fell from the tree and lay shining in the snow at the priest's feet. It was the Flying Stars. When Father Brown looked up again, the branches were empty. Flambeau was gone over the garden wall and into the night.

Father Brown picked up the three diamonds and carried them back into the warm house. Sir Leopold had his stones again, and nobody spoke against Crook after that. Ruby stood beside the young man in the red tie, and she was smiling.

## Level B2

Christmas had come to Putney, and the old house of Ruby's father, a retired soldier, was full of lamps, holly and noise. The old man usually preferred a quiet fire and a newspaper, but for his only daughter he had opened every door. Among the guests sat Father Brown, a short, round little priest who was an old friend of the family. He said very little, but his gray eyes noticed a great deal.

The most important guest arrived last. Sir Leopold was a rich man with a large, comfortable face, and he came in wrapped in an enormous fur coat. He had brought Ruby a present, and he made everyone wait while he took a small box from an inside pocket. When he opened it, the room went silent. Three diamonds lay on the dark cloth, shining like drops of frozen light. "They are called the Flying Stars," Sir Leopold said, clearly enjoying the moment. "Half the thieves in Europe have tried to get them. I am told that even Flambeau, the famous French thief, has been following them." Then he closed the box and returned it carefully to the pocket of his coat.

Ruby thanked him politely, but her eyes kept moving towards a young man near the fire, whose name was Crook. He wrote for small newspapers and held strong opinions about the poor. He always wore a bright red tie, as if he wanted everyone to know which side he was on. He made no secret of his dislike for rich men. Ruby's father found him rude, and Sir Leopold found him worse than rude. Ruby, however, found him the most interesting person in the room.

Later in the afternoon, Ruby's uncle burst in, red-faced and cheerful, shaking snow from his hat. He was a big man who loved games, and he had brought a friend with him. The friend was tall, light on his feet and full of jokes, and he seemed to know all about the stage. Nobody in the house had ever seen him before. Within ten minutes he had suggested an old-fashioned pantomime for the children, and the uncle was delighted. The stranger arranged everything himself, and of course he chose to be the clown. Crook was given the part of the young dancer, Ruby the pretty girl, and the uncle the foolish old man. Sir Leopold, to his great annoyance, had to be the policeman. He tried to refuse, but the clown joked and begged until it was easier to agree.

The show was loud, wild and very funny. The clown seemed to be everywhere at once. He tumbled over chairs, slid across the floor and played one trick after another on the unhappy policeman. He pushed Sir Leopold this way and that until the poor man was hot and cross. The children roared with laughter, and even the old soldier smiled. In the corner, Father Brown watched the whole performance with quiet attention. Every eye in the room was on the clown's painted face, and not one of them was on his hands.

When the curtain fell, Sir Leopold went to his coat, took out the little box and opened it. For a moment he simply stared. Then he held it up for everyone to see: it was empty. "The Flying Stars have been stolen!" he cried. Every head turned towards the young man in the red tie. "It was you," said Ruby's father coldly. "You talk all day against rich men, and you have been close to Sir Leopold all evening." Crook went as red as his tie. "I never touched them," he said, and Ruby moved to his side and took his arm.

Father Brown alone did not join in the noise. He was thinking about pantomimes. In a pantomime, he reflected, a clown can crash into anyone and put his hands into any pocket. He can fall over anything, and the audience only laughs. There could be no better cover for a thief. And the clown was a man whom none of them knew. "Excuse me," said the priest mildly, "but where is our clown?" Nobody could say, and Father Brown slipped out through the back door into the garden.

Snow lay over everything, and a large moon made the garden almost as bright as day. At the far end, near the wall, stood a tall tree. Among its bare branches there was a pale shape in a clown's white costume. "Good evening, Flambeau," said Father Brown, looking up. There was no answer, but the priest did not seem to expect one. Calmly, he described the whole plan, as if he were telling a story by the fire. Flambeau had come as the uncle's friend and had chosen the pantomime on purpose. As a clown, he could touch Sir Leopold again and again without anyone wondering why. He had taken the stones while the room was roaring with laughter.

Then the little priest's voice changed. "You are still young, Flambeau," he said. "You still have courage, a sense of humor and some honor. Do not believe that you can stay where you are. Men may keep a certain level of goodness all their lives. But no man has ever been able to keep a certain level of evil. That road goes down and down, and it ends somewhere very dark and very lonely. Turn back now, while the way is still open."

For a long while the only sound was the wind in the branches. Then something small dropped from the tree and lay glittering in the snow at the priest's feet. Father Brown bent and picked up the three diamonds. When he looked up, the branches were empty, and a single white figure was already disappearing over the garden wall.

The priest walked slowly back to the warm, bright house with the Flying Stars in his hand. Sir Leopold had his treasure again, and no one could say another word against Crook. Ruby, standing beside the young man in the red tie, looked happier than any diamond could have made her.

## Level C1

Many years later, when he had become an honest man, Flambeau liked to say that the most beautiful crime of his life had also been his last. It had been committed, quite appropriately, on Christmas Day. He was an artist, he would explain, and he had always tried to suit his crimes to the season and the scenery, as a painter chooses his colors. For a Christmas crime he needed a comfortable English house full of children and old jokes, and he found exactly that in Putney.

The house belonged to Ruby's father, a retired soldier with a stiff back and a soft heart. He generally preferred his newspaper to company, but for his only daughter he had thrown every door wide open. The rooms smelled of holly and roasting chestnuts, and somewhere a piano was being played badly and cheerfully. In a corner of the drawing room, almost lost among the cushions, sat Father Brown, a small, round priest with a face like a dumpling. He was an old friend of the family, and he seemed to do nothing all afternoon except blink.

The guest everyone was waiting for arrived last, as important guests do. Sir Leopold was a man of large fortune and larger self-satisfaction. He came in so deeply wrapped in fur that he looked less like a man than like a very expensive bear. He had brought a Christmas present for Ruby, and he made the most of it, unbuttoning his great coat with slow ceremony and drawing a small case from an inside pocket. When he opened it, even the children stopped talking. On the dark velvet lay three diamonds, each of them burning with a cold, restless fire. "The Flying Stars," Sir Leopold announced. "They have been stolen so often that they have earned the name, and half the thieves of Europe would give their right hands for them. The papers tell me that Flambeau himself is in England, no doubt with the same idea." He laughed comfortably, shut the case, and returned it to the pocket of the fur coat, which he then hung over a chair as if nothing in the world could touch it.

Ruby thanked him with perfect manners, but her attention kept drifting to the fireplace, where a lean young man with untidy hair was standing. This was Crook, who wrote fierce articles for small radical papers and wore a scarlet tie as other men wear a flag. He believed the rich were a disease from which the country would one day recover. He did not trouble to hide his opinion of Sir Leopold, and Sir Leopold returned the feeling in full. Ruby's father treated the young man as a passing spell of bad weather, best ignored; Ruby herself, as anyone with eyes could see, did not want him to pass at all.

Towards evening the front door flew open and Ruby's uncle came stamping in, red-cheeked and roaring with good humor, a big man who had never outgrown his love of games. With him came a friend, a tall, supple fellow with laughing eyes, who seemed to know every stage in Europe and every trick that had ever been played on one. No one in the house had met him before, yet within a quarter of an hour he had charmed them all. Within half an hour he had persuaded them to perform an old-fashioned pantomime for the children.

It was the stranger who arranged everything, and he did it with dazzling speed. He himself would be the clown, naturally. Crook was made the nimble young dancer, Ruby the pretty girl he loved, and the uncle the foolish old man who is always being fooled. There remained the policeman, the figure whom every pantomime exists to make ridiculous. The clown insisted, with a hundred jokes and much flattery, that only Sir Leopold had the dignity for the part. Sir Leopold protested, grumbled and finally gave in.

What followed was, by every account, the best pantomime ever seen in Putney. The clown was a genius of disorder. He rolled under tables, slid down banisters and fell over the piano stool, rising again in a single movement. He gave the poor policeman no peace at all, spinning him round, knocking his helmet over his eyes and bustling him from one end of the room to the other. The children shrieked with delight, the uncle laughed until he had to sit down, and even the old soldier was seen to wipe his eyes. Only Father Brown, in his corner, watched with an expression of mild and growing puzzlement, as though he were reading a book in which one sentence did not quite belong.

When the performance ended and the actors were taking their bows, Sir Leopold went to his chair, felt inside the fur coat, and drew out the little case. He opened it, closed it, opened it again, and then held it up with a face the color of cold porridge. It was empty. In the silence that followed, every head in the room turned, as if pulled by a single string, towards the scarlet tie by the fireplace. "I think, sir," said Ruby's father in his parade-ground voice, "that you had better explain yourself. You have spent the whole evening telling us that rich men deserve to lose their property, and you have spent the whole pantomime within reach of this gentleman." Crook turned as red as his tie. With more dignity than anyone expected, he said that he had never touched the stones; and Ruby, without a word, crossed the room and stood beside him.

Father Brown alone seemed uninterested in the young man. He was thinking about pantomimes, and about how wonderfully suited they are to a certain kind of crime. In ordinary life, a man who bumps into you, clutches your coat and tumbles over your furniture invites suspicion; in a pantomime, he invites applause. A clown may put his hands anywhere and the audience will only laugh the louder. And this particular clown had chosen the play and chosen the parts. Above all, he had arranged that the owner of the diamonds should spend the evening being pushed about the room by the clown himself. "Forgive me," said the priest in his mild voice, to nobody in particular, "but has anyone seen our clown?" Nobody had, and while they were still looking at one another, Father Brown slipped quietly out by the garden door.

Outside, the snow lay thick and unbroken under an enormous moon, and the garden was as clear as a stage. At the far end, by the high wall, stood an old tree, and among its black branches crouched a pale shape in loose white clothes, as still as an owl. "Good evening, Flambeau," said Father Brown, without raising his voice. The figure did not stir. The priest, as if talking to an old acquaintance about the weather, went calmly through the whole affair. He described the arrival with the cheerful uncle, the pantomime proposed in the first hour, and the careful casting of Sir Leopold as the policeman. Last of all came the three stones, lifted from the fur coat in the middle of the loudest laugh of the evening.

Then his tone changed, and he spoke more earnestly than anyone in that house had ever heard him speak. "You are young still, Flambeau," he said, "and you have courage and wit and a kind of honor, which is more than most men in your trade possess. But do not imagine that you can keep them. A man may keep a steady level of goodness for the whole of his life; no man in history has ever been able to keep a steady level of evil. That road leads only downward, and every step makes the next one easier. I have seen where it ends, and it ends in a very small and very lonely place. Come down from it while there is still a way down."

For a long time nothing answered him but the faint creak of the branches in the frost. Then something fell, glittering, through the moonlight, and three small stars lay burning in the snow at the priest's feet. Father Brown stooped and gathered them up, and when he straightened again the tree was empty, and a white figure was dropping lightly over the garden wall into the dark lane beyond.

The little priest walked back across the snow towards the bright windows. Inside, the family was still arguing over Crook, and Sir Leopold was still mourning his loss. Then Father Brown laid the Flying Stars on the table with an explanation that nobody could quite follow. The young man in the scarlet tie was cleared at once, and Ruby, standing at his side, looked as though she had received a far better present that evening than any diamond. As for the clown, he was never seen in Putney again.
