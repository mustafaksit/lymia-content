# The Ebony Horse

Summary: A young prince rides a flying ebony horse and searches far lands for the princess he loves.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): horse, ebony, sage, princess

## Level A1

The king of Persia had a big party. Three sages came with presents. The last sage had an ebony horse. "This horse can fly," said the sage. "What do you want?" asked the king. "I want your daughter," said the sage.

But the young prince said no. "Try the horse first," said the sage. The young man got on the horse. He turned a small part on its side. Up and up went the horse! The sage did not show the way down.

The horse went over many lands. Then the young man found a second part. He turned it, and the horse went down. It was night. The horse came down on a big house. This house was in the city of Sana.

In one room, he found a young girl. She was the princess of Sana. "Where are you from?" asked the princess. They talked all night. By morning, they were in love.

Her father found them and was very angry. The young man ran to his horse. He went home to Persia. His father held him close. But he always thought about the princess. So he went back to Sana for her. She got on the horse with him.

He left her in a small house. It was near his city. "Wait here for me," said the prince. But the sage came to the house. "The prince sent me," said the sage. The princess believed him. The sage took her far away.

In another land, a king saw them. He took the princess from the sage. This king wanted to marry her. So every day, the princess acted mad.

The prince looked for her in many lands. At last, he came to this king. He came as a doctor. "Bring the horse outside," said the prince. He sat on the horse with the princess. He turned the small part. Up they went, all the way home! Soon the prince and the princess married.

## Level A2

Long ago, the king of Persia had a great party. Three sages came from far countries with presents. The last sage brought a horse of black ebony. "This horse can fly to any place," said the sage. He got on the horse and flew around the city. "Ask me for anything," said the king. "Then give me your daughter as my wife," said the sage.

The young prince did not like this plan. "Father, you cannot give my sister to this old man!" he said. "First, try my horse," said the sage. He showed the young man a small key on one side. The young man got on and turned it. At once, the horse went high into the air. The sage did not tell him how to come down.

In Persia, his father waited all day. But the young man did not come back. So the king kept the sage in a dark room.

High in the air, the young man looked at the horse again. He found a second key on the other side. He turned it, and the horse began to go down. It was night when it stopped on top of a large house. This house was in the city of Sana.

In one room, he found the princess of Sana. She opened her eyes and saw a young man there. "Where do you come from?" asked the princess. He told her his story, and they talked until morning. Soon they were in love.

In the morning, her father found them together. He was very angry and called his men. The young man ran to his horse and flew home. His father was very happy and let the sage go free.

But the prince could not forget the princess of Sana. After some days, he flew back to her city. "Come with me to Persia," said the prince. The princess got on the horse behind him. Near his father's city, there was a garden with a small house. "Wait here while I talk to my father," said the prince.

But the old sage was in the garden that day. "The prince sent me to bring you to him," said the sage. The princess believed him and got on the horse. The sage flew away with her to a far country. There, a king saw them and took the princess home. He put the sage in a dark room.

This king wanted to marry the princess. But she loved only the young prince. So she acted mad every day. Many doctors came, but no one could help her.

The prince looked for her in many towns. At last, he heard news about a mad princess. He dressed as a doctor and went to that king. "Bring her and the horse into the open field," said the prince. There, he sat on the horse with the princess. Then he turned the key, and they flew up. They flew home to Persia, and soon they married.

## Level B1

Long ago, the king of Persia held a great festival every new year. One year, three sages arrived, men who knew many secrets of the world. The first sage gave the king a peacock of gold that sang every hour. The second gave him a brass trumpet that warned the city when enemies came near. The third sage, a very old man, led in a horse of shining black ebony.

"This horse can carry a rider through the air to any place," he said. To show its power, he climbed onto its back and rose high above the crowd. The king was delighted. "Name your reward, and I will give it to you," he promised. "Then give me your daughter as my wife," the sage answered.

The princess heard these words and ran to her room in tears. Her brother, the young prince, was furious. "Father, you cannot give my sister to this old stranger!" he cried. The king did not want to break his promise. "First, my son will try the horse," he said. The sage hid his anger. He showed the prince a small peg on the horse's right shoulder. He told the prince how to go up, but he said nothing about coming down. The prince turned the peg, and the horse flew up into the sky like a bird.

The king waited for his son until night, but the prince did not return. Full of sadness and anger, the king locked the sage in a prison under the palace. "You will stay there until my son comes home," he said.

Far above the earth, the prince understood the sage's trick. At last, he found a second peg on its left shoulder. When he turned it, the horse began to sink slowly toward the ground. It was already dark when it landed on the flat roof of a great palace. This was the palace of the king of Sana.

The prince crept down some stairs and passed a few sleeping guards. In a beautiful room, a young woman was sleeping on a bed of silk. She was the princess of Sana, and she was lovelier than the moon. "Who are you, and how did you come here?" she asked. The prince told her everything, and they talked until the stars faded. By morning, they were deeply in love.

In the morning, a servant saw the stranger and ran to the king of Sana. "Who is this man in my daughter's room?" he demanded. "I am the son of the king of Persia, and I love your daughter," he replied. The king called his soldiers, but the prince hurried up to the roof. He sat on the ebony horse, turned the peg, and rose above their heads. Then he flew all the way home to Persia.

His father wept with joy when he saw him. Then the king set the sage free. But the prince could not stop thinking about the princess of Sana. Soon he flew back to her palace by night. "Come with me to Persia, and you will be my wife," he said. She agreed and climbed onto the horse behind him.

Near his father's city, there was a royal garden with a little summer house. The prince left the princess there to rest. "Wait for me here while I prepare a welcome for you," he said. But that day the sage was in the garden, gathering plants for his medicines. He saw the horse and the princess, and he made a wicked plan. "The prince sent me to bring you to the city," he lied. The princess believed him and got on the horse. At once, the sage flew away with her, far from Persia.

They came down in a distant kingdom, where the king was out riding with his men. The king saw the frightened girl and asked who she was. "This old man stole me from my prince," she said. The king believed her, took her to his palace, and threw the sage into prison. But soon this king also wanted to marry her. The princess had a clever idea, and she pretended to be mad. She shouted strange words and did not let anyone come near her. The king called many doctors, but none of them could cure her.

Meanwhile, the prince was searching for her in every land. One day, in a busy market, he heard people talking about a mad princess. He dressed as a doctor and went to that kingdom. "I can cure her, but I need the ebony horse," he told the king. He asked the king to bring her and the horse into an open field. There, he sat the princess on the horse and climbed up behind her. Then he turned the peg, and they rose into the sky. They flew home to Persia, and soon they had a joyful wedding.

## Level B2

Long ago, the king of Persia held a great festival every new year, and visitors came from every province to honor him. One year, three sages arrived at the palace, each carrying a wonderful gift for the king. The first brought a golden peacock. Every hour, it spread its tail and cried out. The second brought a brass trumpet that would sound by itself whenever an enemy approached the city gates. The third, a very old man, led in a horse carved from black ebony, so smooth and shining that it seemed alive.

"And what can a wooden horse do?" asked the king, smiling politely. "Your Majesty, this horse can carry its rider through the air to any place on earth," replied the sage. To prove it, he mounted the horse, touched something on its shoulder, and rose above the heads of the astonished crowd. After circling the towers twice, he brought it gently back to the ground. The king was so delighted that he promised the sage any reward he wished. Without hesitation, the old man asked for the hand of the king's daughter.

When the princess was told of this, she shut herself in her room and wept. Her brother, the prince, went straight to their father. "If you give my sister to that old man, you will break her heart," he said. The king was troubled, because a king's promise cannot easily be taken back. At last, he said that the prince should test the horse first, to see whether it was really worth such a price. The sage showed the prince a small peg on the horse's right shoulder. Turning it, he explained, would make the horse rise. However, he said nothing at all about how to bring it down again. The moment the prince turned the peg, the horse leapt into the sky and was soon lost from sight.

The king waited until evening, and then all through the night, but his son did not come back. Convinced that the sage had tricked him, he had the old man thrown into prison. "You will stay there until my son returns," the king declared.

Meanwhile, the prince was being carried higher and higher above unknown lands. He realized that the sage had sent him away on purpose, so he began to examine every part of the horse. Finally, on its left shoulder, he found a second peg, and when he turned it, the horse began to sink gently. Night had fallen by the time it landed on the flat roof of a magnificent palace. He later learned that this was the palace of the king of Sana.

Moving quietly, the prince went down a staircase and slipped past the guards, who were fast asleep. In a room lit by a single lamp, he found a young woman sleeping, more beautiful than anyone he had ever seen. She was the princess of Sana. When she woke and saw a stranger beside her, she was surprised. Yet she was not afraid, for his face was kind. He told her who he was and how the horse had brought him there, and they talked until dawn. By the time the sky grew light, they had fallen in love.

In the morning, one of the servants found the young man and ran to tell the king of Sana. The king rushed in and demanded to know who had dared to enter his daughter's room. The prince answered that he was the son of the king of Persia and that he wished to marry the princess. When the king laughed at him, the prince offered to face the whole army of Sana by himself. The king agreed, and his soldiers were gathered in the courtyard below the roof. But the prince simply climbed onto his horse, turned the peg and rose high above their heads. He flew straight home to Persia without stopping.

His father wept with joy to see him safe. In his happiness, he set the sage free. Yet the prince could not stop thinking about the princess of Sana. Before long, he flew back to her palace at night, and she agreed to leave with him. She climbed onto the horse behind him, and together they crossed mountains and seas until the towers of his father's city appeared.

Outside the city stood a royal garden with a small summer palace. The prince left the princess there to rest, because he wanted to ride ahead and arrange a proper welcome for her. By bad luck, the sage was walking in that very garden, collecting herbs for his medicines. When he saw the princess and the ebony horse, he knew exactly what to do. He told her that the prince had sent him to bring her into the city, and she believed him. As soon as she was seated behind him, he turned the peg and carried her far away from Persia.

They came down at last in a distant kingdom, in a meadow where its king happened to be riding with his men. The king asked who they were, and the princess explained that the old man had stolen her from the prince she loved. The king believed her, ordered the sage to be locked in prison, and took the princess to his palace. The ebony horse was carried there too and kept in the royal treasury. Before long, however, the king decided that he wanted to marry the princess himself.

The princess knew that she could not refuse him openly, so she thought of a clever plan. She pretended to have lost her mind, shouting strange words and refusing to let anyone come near her. Doctors were brought from every town in the kingdom, but none of them could help her.

All this time, the prince had been searching for her from city to city. One evening, in a crowded market, he heard some merchants talking about a foreign princess who had gone mad. He dressed himself as a doctor and travelled to that kingdom, where the king welcomed him eagerly. After seeing the princess, the prince said that her illness came from the ebony horse. She would be cured, he explained, only in the meadow where she had been found, with the horse beside her. The king agreed, and soon everything was ready. The prince helped the princess onto the horse, climbed up behind her and turned the peg. As they rose into the air, the king and his court could only stare in amazement. They flew home to Persia, where the old king welcomed them with great joy. Soon afterwards, the prince and the princess were married.

## Level C1

In the old days, the kings of Persia marked the turning of the year with a festival that lasted many days. During that time the palace gates stood open to anyone who came with a gift or a request. Among the merchants, nobles and travelers who crowded in one spring were three sages from distant countries, each claiming to bring a marvel no king had ever owned.

The first set before the throne a peacock of pure gold, which spread its glittering tail at the passing of every hour and gave a single clear cry. The second offered a trumpet of brass that, once placed above the city gate, would sound of its own accord whenever an enemy drew near. The third was an old man so bent and thin that he seemed hardly able to stand. Behind him, he led a horse carved from ebony, polished until the courtiers could see their own faces in its sides.

The king admired the first two gifts and rewarded their makers generously, but he could not hide a smile when he turned to the third. "And what does your horse do, apart from standing very still?" he asked. "Your Majesty, it flies," said the sage, "and it will carry its rider to any place under heaven in a single day." Without waiting for permission, he climbed onto its back, touched something hidden in its shoulder, and rose above the gasping crowd. Twice he circled the palace towers before settling again exactly where he had started.

Delighted beyond words, the king swore to grant the old man whatever he desired in return. The sage bowed low and asked, in a voice as dry as dust, for the hand of the king's daughter. The princess, who had been watching from behind a screen, fled to her rooms in tears. It was her brother, the young prince, who went to their father to plead on her behalf. "You cannot sell my sister for a toy, however clever it may be," he said. The king, who had already begun to regret his promise, seized gladly on a way to delay. Let his son first try the horse, he declared; if it truly flew as the sage claimed, they could speak of payment afterwards.

The sage, concealing his anger behind a courteous smile, showed the prince a small peg on the horse's right shoulder and told him that turning it would make the creature rise. Of the means to bring it down again he said nothing at all. Eager and fearless, the prince sprang onto its back and turned the peg. He was carried upwards so swiftly that within moments he had shrunk to a speck against the clouds, and then he vanished altogether.

All that day and the following night the king waited on the palace roof, but no rider came back out of the sky. At last, sick with grief, he understood that he had been deceived, and he ordered the sage to be thrown into prison until the prince came home.

High above the earth, meanwhile, the prince had grasped the trick that had been played on him. Refusing to give way to despair, he began patiently to search every inch of the horse. On its left shoulder his fingers found a second peg, no bigger than a grain of rice. When he turned it, the horse began to sink, gently and steadily, through the cool evening air. Darkness had fallen by the time it touched down on the flat roof of a palace grander than any he had known. Only later did he learn that he had come to Sana.

Leaving the horse in the shadows, he crept down a winding stair, past guards who were snoring at their posts, until he reached a chamber lit by a single silver lamp. There, on a bed hung with silk, lay a young woman so lovely that he forgot his hunger, his weariness and his danger all at once. She was the princess of Sana, and when she opened her eyes and found a stranger watching her, she was startled rather than afraid. There was nothing in his face but wonder. He told her who he was and by what strange road he had come, and they talked in low voices until the windows turned pale with dawn. By then, neither of them could imagine parting from the other.

It was a servant who discovered them and ran to fetch the king of Sana, who arrived in a fury to demand what thief had dared to enter his daughter's room. The prince replied, with perfect calm, that he was no thief but the son of the king of Persia, and that he wished to marry the princess. When the king scoffed, the prince offered to prove his worth by facing the whole army of Sana on his own. Half amused and half curious, the king agreed. Yet once the soldiers had gathered in the great courtyard, the prince simply mounted his ebony horse, turned the peg, and rose far above their upturned faces. An entire army was left staring at an empty sky.

He flew without resting until he reached Persia, where his father, who had given him up for lost, wept for joy at the sight of him. In the gladness of that hour, the old king released the sage from prison. But the celebrations at court could not hold the prince's attention for long, because his thoughts kept drifting back to the princess of Sana. Within a few weeks he had flown back to her by night, and this time she did not hesitate. She climbed up behind him, and together they crossed deserts, mountains and seas until the domes of his father's city glimmered on the horizon.

Wishing to bring her into the city with the honor she deserved, the prince left her to rest in a royal garden outside the walls. A small summer pavilion stood there among the trees, and he rode ahead to prepare a fitting welcome. It was the worst of luck that the sage, still bitter over his humiliation, had wandered into that very garden to gather herbs. Seeing the ebony horse and the beautiful stranger beside it, he guessed at once who she must be, and he approached her with a deep bow. The prince, he told her, had sent him to bring her to the palace; and the princess, having no reason to doubt him, allowed him to help her onto the horse. The moment she was seated, he turned the peg, and before she understood what was happening, the garden, the city and all of Persia had dropped away beneath them.

They came down at last in a distant kingdom, in a meadow where its king happened to be riding with his courtiers. He demanded to know who these strange travelers were. The sage claimed the princess as his wife, but she cried out that he was lying and had stolen her from the man she loved. The king, who could see the truth in her face, had the sage led away to prison and brought the princess, together with the ebony horse, to his palace. She soon found, however, that she had merely exchanged one captor for another, more polite but no less determined, for the king announced that he would marry her himself. Since she dared not refuse him openly, she chose the only defense left to her and pretended to have lost her reason. She raved at anyone who came near and tore up every letter the king sent. Physicians were summoned from every corner of the kingdom, and every one of them went home defeated.

All this while, the prince had been wandering from city to city in search of her, with nothing to guide him but rumor. In the market of a busy port he overheard some merchants discussing a foreign princess who had gone mad in a neighboring kingdom. They spoke, too, of the ebony horse that had been found with her, and he set out at once. Disguised as a physician, he presented himself to the king, examined the princess with great solemnity, and declared that her madness had entered her from the ebony horse. She could be cured, he said, only in the meadow where she had first been found, seated on the horse itself. The king, desperate for a cure, ordered everything to be done exactly as the doctor directed. In the meadow, before the whole court, the prince lifted the princess onto the horse, mounted behind her, and turned the peg. As they rose into the sky, the king understood at last that his remarkable physician had been no physician at all. They flew home to Persia, where the old king welcomed them with boundless joy, and the prince and the princess of Sana were married amid celebrations that lasted for many days.
