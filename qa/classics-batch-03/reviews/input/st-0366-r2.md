# The Shepherdess and the Chimney-Sweep

Summary: Two little china figures in love try to escape a wedding that the old grandfather has arranged.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): china, chimney, cabinet, goat-legs

## Level A1

An old cabinet stood in a big room. A little wooden man was on the cabinet. He had the legs of a goat. He always looked at a small table.

Two little china people stood on it. One was the Shepherdess. The other was the Chimney-Sweep. His clothes were black from the chimneys. He had a ladder. The two loved each other.

The old china grandfather stood near them. He could move his head up and down. "I am her grandfather," he often said.

The man on the cabinet wanted the Shepherdess. "I want to marry her," he said. The grandfather moved his head. It meant yes. "You can marry her tonight," he said.

The Shepherdess cried. She went to the Chimney-Sweep. "Take me out into the world," she said. "Yes, let us go," said the Chimney-Sweep.

That night, they went down from the table. The man on the cabinet saw them. "Stop them!" he called. The grandfather came after them. The two ran into the chimney. They went up and up.

At last, they came to the top. Above them were many stars. The world was very, very big. "It is too big!" said the Shepherdess. "I want my little table again." The Chimney-Sweep talked to her. But she only cried more. So he took her back down.

But the grandfather was not on the table. He went after them, and he fell. Now he was in three parts. "This is because of us!" said the Shepherdess. "People can help him," said the Chimney-Sweep. They went back up on the table.

Then the people of the house came. They put the grandfather back together. But now he could not move his head. The man on the cabinet asked again. "Can I have the Shepherdess now?" The grandfather could not say yes. And he did not want to say why. So the two stayed together. They loved each other for a long time.

## Level A2

In an old room stood a very old cabinet. In the middle of it stood a little man. He had a long beard and the legs of a goat. He always looked at a small table.

On that table stood two little china people. One was the Shepherdess, with a red rose on her dress. Next to her stood the Chimney-Sweep. He was as black as night, but he was china too. He had a small ladder. The two stood close together, and they loved each other.

Near them stood the old china grandfather. He was three times bigger than them, and he could nod. "I am the Shepherdess's grandfather," he always said. So he wanted to decide everything for her.

One evening, the man on the cabinet spoke to the grandfather. "I want to marry the Shepherdess," he said. The grandfather nodded his big head. "You can marry her tonight," he answered.

The Shepherdess did not want this husband, and she began to cry. "Take me into the wide world," she said to the Chimney-Sweep. "Do you know how big the world is?" he asked. "We can never come back here." "I know, and I still want to go," said the Shepherdess.

That night, they went down from the table on his ladder. The man on the cabinet saw them. "Look! They want to run away!" he called. The old grandfather began to come after them. The two young people ran into the chimney. It was very dark inside, but the Chimney-Sweep helped her up. Far above them, they could see one bright star.

At last, they came out at the top of the chimney. Above them was the night with all its stars. Under them were the houses of the town. The world was much bigger than the Shepherdess thought. She put her head on the Chimney-Sweep's arm and cried. "The world is too big!" she said. "Please take me back to our little table." The Chimney-Sweep talked to her for a long time. But she only cried more, so he took her back down.

From the dark chimney, they looked out into the room. The old grandfather was on the floor in three pieces. He fell from the table on his way after them. The man on the cabinet stood in his old place.

"Oh no, it is all because of us!" said the Shepherdess. "People can put him back together," said the Chimney-Sweep. "They can fix his back and his head." So the two went back up to their table.

The people of the house put the grandfather back together. He was as good as new, but now he could not nod. "Can I marry the Shepherdess now?" asked the man on the cabinet. The two young people looked at the grandfather and waited. But he could not nod his head. And he did not want to tell anyone why. So the Shepherdess and the Chimney-Sweep stayed together. They loved each other for many, many years.

## Level B1

In a quiet old room stood a cabinet that was almost black with age. Flowers, leaves and little deer heads covered its wood from top to bottom. In the middle of the cabinet stood a carved man with a long beard. He had two small horns on his head and the legs of a goat. The children of the house gave him a long, funny name. Day and night, the goat-legged man looked at the little table under the mirror.

On that table stood a pretty china Shepherdess. She had golden shoes, a golden hat and a red rose on her dress. Next to her stood a little Chimney-Sweep, who was china too. He was as black as coal, and he carried a small ladder. Only his face was clean and pink. They stood so close that they fell in love and promised to marry.

Not far from them stood a much bigger figure, the old china grandfather. He was three times as tall as them, and he could nod his head. He always said that he was the Shepherdess's grandfather, but nobody could prove it. Because of this, he believed that he could decide everything for her.

One evening, the goat-legged man spoke to the old grandfather. "I want the little Shepherdess for my wife," he said. The grandfather thought for a moment, and then he nodded. He turned to the Shepherdess. "This man owns a whole cabinet full of silver," the grandfather told her. "He already has eleven china wives in there. Tonight, when the old cabinet creaks, you will marry him."

The Shepherdess began to cry and looked at the Chimney-Sweep. "I do not want to go into that dark cabinet," she said. "Please take me out into the wide world. We cannot stay here." "Do you know how big the world is?" the Chimney-Sweep asked her. "We can never come back here again." "I know," said the Shepherdess, "and I still want to go."

That night, the two climbed down the leg of the table on his ladder. The goat-legged man saw them at once and jumped high into the air. "They are running away!" he shouted to the grandfather. The old grandfather began to rock toward the edge of the table. The young pair ran and hid for a while in a drawer under the window. When they came out, they saw the grandfather coming after them. "My way goes through the stove and up the chimney," said the Chimney-Sweep. "Are you brave enough to come with me?" "Yes," said the Shepherdess, and they crept into the big iron stove.

From the stove they went up into the chimney, where it was very dark. The Chimney-Sweep lifted her, helped her and showed her where to put her little feet. Far above them, a single star was shining like a little lamp for them.

At last, they reached the top and sat down on the edge, very tired. Over them was the starry night sky, and under them were the roofs of the town. They could see far out into the wide world. The poor Shepherdess leaned on the Chimney-Sweep and cried. She cried so hard that the gold came off her belt. "It is too much for me," she said. "The world is far too big. I want to go back to our little table under the mirror." The Chimney-Sweep talked to her kindly and tried to change her mind. But she only cried harder and held on to him, so at last he agreed.

The way down was hard, but finally they reached the stove again. They looked out into the quiet room. The old grandfather was lying on the floor in three pieces. He fell off the table when he tried to follow them. His back lay in one place, and his head was in a corner. The goat-legged man stood where he always stood, deep in thought.

"Oh, this is terrible!" said the Shepherdess. "Poor grandfather is broken, and it is all our fault." "People can mend him," said the Chimney-Sweep. "They will glue his back and put a good rivet in his neck. Then he will be as good as new." So they climbed back up onto their table.

The family of the house mended the old grandfather. They glued his back and put a rivet in his neck. He was as good as new, but he could not nod any more. "You are very proud now," said the goat-legged man. "Do I get the Shepherdess or not?" The Chimney-Sweep and the Shepherdess looked at the old grandfather with worried eyes. He could not nod, and he was too proud to talk about his rivet. So the two little china people stayed together. They loved each other until the day they broke.

## Level B2

In the parlor of an old house there stood a cabinet so ancient that its wood had turned almost black. Long ago, a craftsman had carved it from top to bottom with roses, tulips and twisting leaves. Here and there, the heads of little deer with branching antlers peeped out between the flowers. Right in the middle stood the strangest carving of all. It was a little man with a long beard, two small horns on his forehead and the legs of a goat. The children of the house gave him a long, grand title full of army ranks. Day and night, the goat-legged man kept his eyes on the little table under the tall mirror.

On that table stood a lovely little Shepherdess made of china. Her shoes were golden, her hat was golden, and a red rose was pinned to the front of her dress. In her hand she held a shepherd's crook. Close beside her stood a little Chimney-Sweep, who was made of the same fine china. He was as black as coal from head to foot, except for his face, which was clean and rosy. At his side he held his little ladder. Because they had always stood side by side, they had grown fond of each other, and now they were engaged. They suited each other well, because both were young and both would break at the slightest knock.

A little way off stood a figure three times their size: the old china grandfather. He could nod his head, and he did it often. He claimed to be the Shepherdess's grandfather, although he had never been able to prove it. All the same, he behaved as though he had the right to rule over her whole life.

One evening, the goat-legged man asked the old grandfather for the Shepherdess's hand. The grandfather considered the matter for a while, and then he nodded his heavy head. "You will have a fine husband," he told the Shepherdess. "I believe he is made of the very best dark wood, and his cabinet is full of silver." "But I don't want to live in that gloomy cabinet!" cried the Shepherdess. "He already has eleven china wives in there, I hear," the grandfather went on. "You can be the twelfth. Tonight, as soon as the old cabinet creaks, the wedding will take place." Then his head nodded forward, and he fell asleep.

The Shepherdess turned to the Chimney-Sweep with tears in her eyes. "Please take me out into the wide world," she begged. "Have you thought about how big the world is?" the Chimney-Sweep asked. "Once we leave, we can never come back." "I have thought about it," she answered.

That very night they set off. With the help of his ladder, they climbed carefully down one leg of the table and reached the floor. But the goat-legged man saw them, and he sprang so high that his beard shook. "They are running away!" he shouted to the old grandfather. Frightened, the two young lovers slipped into the drawer under the window seat. Inside lay a few old playing cards and a little puppet theater. A play was being performed, and the cards were sitting in rows to watch it. It told of two young people who loved each other but were not allowed to marry. The Shepherdess began to cry, because it was exactly like her own story.

"I can't bear it," she whispered. "We must get out of this drawer." When they climbed out, they saw that the old grandfather was awake. He was rocking his whole body back and forth, coming slowly toward the edge of the table. "He is coming after us!" cried the Shepherdess, and she fell to her knees in fear. "My way leads through the stove and up the chimney," said the Chimney-Sweep. "Are you brave enough to come with me?" "Yes, I am," she answered.

They crept into the big iron stove and from there into the chimney. Inside the chimney it was pitch dark. He lifted her, pushed her gently upward and showed her where to put her little china feet. Far above them, a single star shone down the long black chimney, as though it wanted to show them the way.

At last, they reached the top and sat down on the rim of the chimney, completely worn out. Above them stretched the night sky, crowded with stars, and beneath them lay the roofs of the town. From up there they could look far out into the wide world. The Shepherdess had never imagined that the world would be so big. She leaned her head on the Chimney-Sweep's shoulder and cried so hard that the gold came off her sash. "It is too much," she sobbed. "I cannot bear a world this big. If only I were back on my little table under the mirror!"

The Chimney-Sweep spoke to her gently and tried to make her see sense. He reminded her of the old grandfather and the goat-legged man. But she sobbed so bitterly and begged him so often that in the end he gave in. Climbing back down was hard work, but at last they crept into the stove again and peeped out through the door.

There on the floor lay the old china grandfather. He had tried to follow them, had fallen off the table and had broken into three pieces. His back had come off in one piece, and his head had rolled into a corner. The goat-legged man still stood in his usual place, lost in thought.

"How terrible!" said the Shepherdess. "Poor old grandfather is broken, and it is all our fault." "He can be mended," said the Chimney-Sweep. "We only need to glue his back and put a good rivet in his neck. Then he will be as good as new, and he will be able to say plenty of unkind things to us again." "Do you really think so?" she asked. Then they climbed back up onto the table where they had always stood.

The family of the house had the old grandfather mended. His back was glued, and a rivet was put in his neck. He looked as good as new, but he could no longer nod. "You have become very proud since you broke," said the goat-legged man. "Do I get the Shepherdess or not?" The Chimney-Sweep and the Shepherdess looked at the old grandfather, afraid that he would nod. But he could not, and he was far too proud to admit to anyone that he had a rivet in his neck. So the two china lovers stayed together. They blessed the grandfather's rivet, and they loved each other until the day they broke.

## Level C1

Some pieces of furniture seem to remember everything that has ever happened in a room, and the cabinet in the old parlor was certainly one of them. Its wood had darkened almost to black over the years, and a carver whose name nobody could recall had covered it from top to bottom with roses, tulips and curling leaves. Between the flowers, the heads of small deer with branching antlers peeped out, as though a whole forest had been pressed flat into the panels.

At the very center of all this carving stood a figure that caught every eye in the room. He was a little man with a long beard, two small horns sprouting from his forehead and, below the waist, the legs of a goat. His mouth had been carved into a broad grin, though whether the grin was friendly or sly was impossible to say. The children of the house had honored him with an enormous title, crammed with army ranks, which nobody could pronounce without stumbling. Whatever he was called, the goat-legged man had eyes for one place only: the little table beneath the tall mirror on the opposite wall.

On that table stood a Shepherdess of the finest china, as delicate as frost on a window. Her shoes were gilded, her hat was gilded, a red rose was pinned to the front of her dress, and in one small hand she held a shepherd's crook. Close beside her stood a Chimney-Sweep, fashioned from exactly the same china, though nobody would have guessed it at first glance. From head to foot he was as black as coal, except for his face, which had stayed as clean and rosy as a ripe apple. He held his little ladder at his side with an air of quiet pride. The two had been placed side by side on the day they arrived, and standing so close, they had slowly grown fond of each other until they were engaged. It was a sensible match, for both were young, both were made of the same china, and both would shatter at the slightest knock.

A little way off, and three times their size, stood the old china grandfather. His one great talent was that he could nod his head, and he used it often and with considerable dignity, as though every nod settled some important question. He claimed to be the Shepherdess's grandfather, although he had never been able to prove it, and on that claim alone he behaved as if her life were his to arrange.

One evening the goat-legged man, who had been admiring the Shepherdess for longer than anyone knew, asked the old grandfather for her hand. The grandfather considered the request with great seriousness and then, slowly and heavily, he nodded. "You are a lucky girl," he informed the Shepherdess, "for you are getting a husband who is made, I almost believe, of the very finest dark wood. His cabinet is full of silver, and heaven knows what further treasures he keeps tucked away in his secret drawers." "But I don't want to go into that gloomy cabinet!" the Shepherdess protested. "I have heard," said the grandfather, ignoring her completely, "that he already keeps eleven china wives in there, so you will make a pleasant twelfth. Tonight, the moment the old cabinet creaks, the two of you shall be married." Having settled the matter to his own satisfaction, he let his head droop forward and nodded off to sleep.

The Shepherdess turned to the Chimney-Sweep with brimming eyes and begged him to take her out into the wide world, since she could not bear to stay another night. "Have you thought about how big the world is?" the Chimney-Sweep asked her gently. "Once we leave this table, there will be no coming back." "I have thought about it," she replied, and the firmness in her small voice surprised them both.

That very night, using the little ladder as a bridge between the tabletop and the floor, they crept down one carved leg of the table. They had hardly reached the bottom when the goat-legged man spotted them and leapt so high into the air that his beard trembled. "They are running away!" he bellowed across the room to the grandfather. "The Shepherdess is running away!" Frightened, the two lovers slipped into the drawer beneath the window seat, where a few worn packs of playing cards lay beside a little puppet theater. A play happened to be under way, and the cards had arranged themselves in rows to watch it, the queens sitting stiffly in the front row and the jacks standing behind them. It was the story of two young people who loved each other dearly but were forbidden to marry, and the Shepherdess, recognizing her own fate in it, began to weep.

"I can't bear to watch this," she whispered, and they climbed out of the drawer as quietly as they could. Out on the floor, they looked up and saw that the old grandfather had woken and was rocking his whole body back and forth, edging slowly toward the rim of the table. The Shepherdess sank to her knees in fright, certain that he was coming for them. "My road leads through the stove and up the chimney," said the Chimney-Sweep, taking her hand. "Do you have the courage to come with me?" She looked at the black mouth of the stove for a long moment, and then she said that she did.

They crept through the big iron stove and into the chimney beyond it, where the darkness was so thick that the Shepherdess could not see her own gilded shoes. The Chimney-Sweep, however, was at home there, and he lifted her, steadied her and showed her exactly where to place her small china feet. Far above them, a single star shone down the long black shaft, and it seemed to the Shepherdess that it was lighting their way on purpose.

At last, they reached the top and sank down on the rim of the chimney, too exhausted to speak. Above them stretched the night sky, crowded with stars, while beneath them the roofs of the sleeping town rolled away in every direction like a dark and endless sea. From that height they could gaze far out into the wide world, and it was immeasurably larger than anything the Shepherdess had ever pictured on her quiet little table. Overwhelmed, she leaned her head against the Chimney-Sweep's shoulder and wept so hard that the gilding came off her sash. "It is too much," she sobbed. "I cannot bear a world as vast as this. If only I were back on my little table under the mirror, I would never complain again, and I shall never be happy until I am standing there once more."

The Chimney-Sweep spoke to her gently and reasonably, reminding her of the old grandfather and of the goat-legged man who would be waiting for her below. But she sobbed so bitterly and pleaded with him so often that in the end he gave in, though he knew perfectly well that it was foolish. The descent was far harder than the climb, yet at last they crept back into the stove and peeped out through its little door.

The room lay utterly silent. There on the floor, in three pieces, lay the old china grandfather, who had tried to follow them and had toppled off the edge of the table. His back had come away in one piece, and his head had rolled into a corner. The goat-legged man still stood exactly where he had always stood, apparently lost in thought.

"How dreadful!" said the Shepherdess. "Poor old grandfather is broken, and it is all our fault. I shall never get over it." "He can be mended," the Chimney-Sweep assured her. "A little glue on his back and a good strong rivet in his neck, and he will be as good as new, ready to say any number of unkind things to us." "Do you really think so?" she asked. Comforted a little, she climbed with him back up onto the table where they had always stood.

The family did have the old grandfather mended: his back was glued, and a rivet was fixed in his neck, so that he looked as fine as before. There was only one thing he could no longer do, and that was nod. "You have grown very proud since you were broken," remarked the goat-legged man, "though I cannot imagine what you have to be proud of. Well, do I get the Shepherdess or not?" The Chimney-Sweep and the Shepherdess turned such pleading eyes on the old grandfather that it would have softened a heart of stone, for they dreaded that he would nod. But nod he could not, and he would sooner have stood silent forever than confess to a stranger that he had a rivet in his neck. So the two china lovers stayed together, quietly blessing the grandfather's rivet, and they went on loving each other until the day they broke.
