# Sense and Sensibility

Summary: Two very different sisters move to a small cottage and learn hard lessons about love.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): sisters, cottage, letter

## Level A1

The Dashwood family lived at Norland. Elinor was the oldest of three sisters. She kept her feelings to herself. Marianne, the second girl, showed every feeling. She loved music, books and long walks.

Then Mr Dashwood died. His son got the house and the money. He was not kind to the sisters. So the girls and their mother left. Before they left, Elinor met Edward Ferrars. He was kind and good. Elinor began to love him. But Edward did not show his love.

A cousin gave them a cottage at Barton. There they met Colonel Brandon. He was older, and very kind. He loved Marianne, but she did not know. One day Marianne fell on a walk. A young man carried her home. His name was Willoughby. He loved music and books too. Soon Marianne loved him very much.

Willoughby came to the cottage every day. Then one morning he left. He did not explain why.

Later a young woman came to Barton. Her name was Lucy Steele. Lucy spoke to Elinor alone. "Edward and I plan to marry. Please tell no one," she said. Elinor felt great pain, but she said nothing.

In winter the sisters went to the city. Marianne wrote many letters to Willoughby. He did not answer them. At a party, he turned away from her. Then he sent her a cold letter. He planned to marry a woman with money.

Marianne walked for hours in the rain. Soon she was very ill. Colonel Brandon went to bring her mother. Elinor sat by her day and night. At last Marianne got better.

Edward's mother learned about Lucy. She gave her money to Edward's brother. Then Lucy married the brother, not Edward! Now Edward was free. "Please marry me, Elinor," he said. Elinor said yes with a happy face.

Marianne began to see Colonel Brandon's good heart. Later, she married him. She loved him very much.

## Level A2

The Dashwood family lived in a large house at Norland. There were three sisters, and the two oldest were very different. Elinor was nineteen, and she always thought before she spoke. She kept her feelings inside. Marianne was seventeen, and she showed every feeling at once. She loved music, books and long walks.

Then Mr Dashwood died. The house and most of the money went to his son. The son's wife did not want to share anything. So the girls and their mother had to find a new home. Before they left, Edward Ferrars came to stay. He was the brother of the son's wife. He was kind and gentle, and Elinor began to love him. Edward seemed to like her too, but he never said so.

A cousin offered the family a small cottage at Barton. It was far away from Norland, in another part of the country. At Barton, they met Colonel Brandon. He was much older than the girls, and he spoke little. He loved Marianne, but she hardly saw him.

One day Marianne ran down a hill in the rain. She fell and hurt her foot. A young man saw her and carried her home. His name was Willoughby, and he was full of life. He loved the same books and music as Marianne. Soon he visited every day. The two of them were always together.

Then one morning Willoughby came to say goodbye. He had to go away, but he did not explain why. Marianne cried all night.

A few weeks later, a young woman came to Barton. Her name was Lucy Steele. One day she spoke to Elinor alone. "Edward and I plan to marry. We made our promise four years ago. His mother does not know, so please tell no one," said Lucy. Elinor felt deep pain, but she said nothing.

That winter the sisters went to the city too. Marianne wrote letter after letter to Willoughby. He never answered. At last, they saw him at a party. He spoke to Marianne coldly and turned away. The next day he sent her a short, cold letter. He planned to marry a woman with a lot of money. Marianne's heart was broken.

On the way home, the sisters stayed at a friend's house. Marianne walked for hours in the rain. Soon she was very ill. Colonel Brandon went through the night to bring her mother. Elinor stayed by her sister's side day and night. At last Marianne began to get better.

Edward's mother learned about Lucy, and she was very angry. She gave all her money to Edward's younger brother. Edward did not leave Lucy. Colonel Brandon kindly gave him work in a village church. But soon Lucy married the younger brother instead. Now he had the money! So Edward was free at last. He came to the cottage and asked Elinor to marry him. With a happy heart, she said yes.

Marianne grew calmer and wiser. She began to see Colonel Brandon's good and patient heart. Later, she married him. She loved him with her whole heart.

## Level B1

The Dashwood family lived at Norland, in the south of England. It was a large, comfortable house with old trees all around it. Mrs Dashwood had three daughters, and the two oldest could not be more different. Elinor, who was nineteen, had a calm mind and a warm but careful heart. Marianne, two years younger, felt everything strongly and hid nothing. She loved music, poetry and wild country walks, and she hated anything ordinary.

Mr Dashwood also had a grown son from his first marriage. When Mr Dashwood died, Norland and almost all of his money went to this son. Before the end, the son promised his father to help his sisters. But his wife soon talked him out of it, and he gave them almost nothing. Life at Norland became uncomfortable, and Mrs Dashwood decided to leave. During those sad months, the wife's brother, Edward Ferrars, came to stay. He was shy and gentle, and he did not care about money or fame. Elinor grew very fond of him, and he seemed to feel the same. Yet something was clearly worrying him, and he never spoke about the future.

A kind cousin offered the family a small cottage at Barton, in another part of England. The cottage was simple, but the countryside around it was beautiful. At Barton the sisters met their cousin's friend, Colonel Brandon. He was a serious, quiet man of thirty-five. He fell in love with Marianne almost at once. Marianne, however, thought he was far too old for any kind of love.

One afternoon Marianne was running down a hill when the rain began. She slipped, fell and hurt her ankle. At that moment a young man appeared and carried her all the way home. His name was Willoughby, and he was handsome, lively and charming. He shared all of Marianne's tastes in books and music. From then on he came to the cottage every day. They walked, read and sang together, and everyone expected a wedding.

Then, one morning, Willoughby arrived looking pale and unhappy. He told them he had to leave for London at once. He would not say when he was coming back. Marianne spent the whole day in tears, and she did not sleep that night. Elinor was worried, because the two of them never made any promise.

Some weeks later, a young woman called Lucy Steele came to visit. She was pretty and clever, and she soon found a chance to speak to Elinor alone. "I want to tell you a secret," she said softly. "Edward Ferrars and I got engaged four years ago. His mother knows nothing about it, so we must wait." Elinor felt the ground move under her feet. She understood now why Edward always looked so troubled. Still, she kept her promise and told no one, not even Marianne.

That winter the sisters went to London with an older friend. Marianne wrote to Willoughby again and again, but no answer came. At last, they saw him at a crowded party. He bowed coldly to Marianne and quickly turned away to another woman. The next morning a short, polite letter arrived. In it, Willoughby said he was going to marry a rich young lady. He behaved very badly, and Marianne's heart broke.

On the way home, the sisters stopped at a friend's house in the country. Marianne, still unhappy, walked for hours in the wet evening grass. She caught a fever, and soon she was dangerously ill. Colonel Brandon rode through the night to bring Mrs Dashwood to her side. For days Elinor sat by her sister's bed, hoping and praying. At last the fever broke, and Marianne slowly began to recover.

Meanwhile, everyone learned about the secret engagement. Edward's proud mother was furious. She told him to leave Lucy, but Edward was an honest man and refused. So his mother gave all of his money to his younger brother. Colonel Brandon kindly offered Edward work as a minister in a small village church. Then came a surprise: Lucy married the younger brother, who now had the money. Edward was free at last. He hurried to Barton and asked Elinor to be his wife, and she happily said yes.

The illness changed Marianne. She became calmer and wiser, and she learned to value quiet goodness. Slowly she came to see Colonel Brandon's patient and loyal heart. A few years later she married him. In time, she loved him as deeply as she once loved Willoughby.

## Level B2

For many years the Dashwood family had lived happily at Norland, a fine old estate in the south of England. Mrs Dashwood had three daughters, and the two oldest could hardly have been more different. Elinor, at nineteen, possessed a cool head and a warm heart, and she rarely let her feelings show. Marianne, two years younger, was clever and generous, but she believed that true feeling should never be hidden or controlled. She loved music and poetry, and she had no patience for anything she considered dull or ordinary.

The trouble began when Mr Dashwood died. By law, Norland passed to his son from an earlier marriage, a man with a comfortable fortune of his own. Before he died, Mr Dashwood had asked him to take care of the girls and their mother. The son agreed, and he even thought of giving them a generous sum. His wife, however, soon persuaded him that a few small presents would be quite enough. Feeling like unwelcome guests in their own home, Mrs Dashwood and her daughters began to look for somewhere else to live.

During those unhappy months, the wife's brother, Edward Ferrars, came to stay at Norland. He was shy and not especially handsome, but he was sensible, honest and kind. His mother wanted him to become a great man in public life, yet he longed only for a quiet country existence. Elinor and Edward spent many hours in conversation, and a deep attachment grew between them. Still, he often seemed low in spirits, as if some private worry weighed on his mind. When the family finally left, he said nothing about the future.

A generous cousin of Mrs Dashwood offered them a cottage on his land at Barton, far away in the west. It was small and plain, but the hills and valleys around it were lovely. Their cousin welcomed them warmly and invited them to his house almost every evening. There they met his friend Colonel Brandon, a serious, silent man of about thirty-five. The colonel was clearly drawn to Marianne from the moment he heard her play. Marianne, for her part, considered a man of his age far too old to think of love.

One wet afternoon, Marianne was running down a hill when she slipped and twisted her ankle. A young gentleman out with his dogs happened to be passing. At once, he lifted her in his arms and carried her back to the cottage. His name was Willoughby, and he seemed to be everything Marianne had ever dreamed of. He was handsome, lively and passionate, and he loved exactly the same books and songs as she did. From that day on he called at the cottage constantly. The two of them read, sang and walked together, without caring what anyone thought. Everyone assumed that they were secretly engaged.

Then, quite suddenly, everything changed. One morning Willoughby appeared, pale and uneasy, and announced that he was leaving for London at once. He gave no clear reason and made no promise to return. Marianne was heartbroken, and she made no effort to hide her grief. Elinor was troubled too, for she noticed that no engagement had ever actually been announced.

Not long after this, two young women, the Steele sisters, arrived as guests of their cousin. The younger, Lucy Steele, was pretty and sharp, and she made a great show of friendship towards Elinor. One day, walking alone with her, Lucy shared a surprising secret. "I have been engaged to Edward Ferrars for four years," she said. "We met when he was studying with my uncle. His mother would never approve, so we must wait and keep it to ourselves." As proof, she showed Elinor a small portrait of Edward. Elinor was deeply hurt, but she hid her pain behind a calm face. She had given her word, so she kept the secret for months, even from Marianne.

That winter an older friend took the sisters to London. Marianne wrote to Willoughby several times, but no answer came. At last, they met him at a crowded evening party. He greeted Marianne coldly and quickly returned to a fashionable young lady. The next morning a short letter arrived in which he returned all her notes and a lock of her hair. Soon they heard that he was to marry a rich young lady in order to pay his debts. He had behaved very badly, and Marianne was left almost broken by grief.

Around the same time, Lucy's secret came out. Edward's mother was furious and demanded that he give Lucy up at once. Edward refused, because he believed it would be wrong to break his word. To punish him, his mother gave his fortune to his younger brother. Colonel Brandon, who had heard of Edward's trouble, kindly offered him the post of minister in a small country parish. Ironically, it was Elinor who had to deliver the colonel's offer to Edward.

In spring the sisters set off for home, staying on the way at a friend's house in the country. Still unhappy, Marianne took long walks in the wet grass in the evening. She caught a fever, and within days her life seemed to be in danger. Colonel Brandon rode through the night to bring Mrs Dashwood. Elinor never left her sister's side, and at last, to her enormous relief, the fever broke. During her slow recovery, Marianne thought hard about her own conduct and resolved to live more wisely.

Back at Barton, the family heard that "Mr Ferrars" had married Lucy, and Elinor's heart sank. A few days later, however, Edward himself appeared at the cottage door. It turned out that Lucy had married his younger brother, who now had the money. Edward was free, and he asked Elinor to marry him. She burst into tears of joy and accepted him. They settled happily in the little parish house the colonel had offered.

Marianne, meanwhile, came to see the colonel's patience, loyalty and quiet strength. Within two years she married him. She gave him her whole heart, just as she had once given it to Willoughby.

## Level C1

For generations the Dashwood family had been settled at Norland, a fine estate in the south whose woods and lawns seemed to promise that nothing there would ever change. Mrs Dashwood had three daughters, and the two eldest might have been invented to illustrate opposite theories of the heart. Elinor, at nineteen, combined strong feeling with the good sense to govern it, and her mother often relied on her judgment more than on her own. Marianne, two years younger, was every bit as clever, but she held that any emotion worth having deserved to be expressed in full, at once and without apology. She read poetry aloud with burning cheeks and played the piano until the candles burned low. Moderation, in her view, was merely a polite name for coldness.

The comfortable world of Norland ended with the death of Mr Dashwood. The estate, by the terms of an old will, passed to his son from a former marriage, a man already wealthy and not inclined to become poorer. At his father's bedside he had promised to provide for his half-sisters, and for an afternoon he even pictured himself handing them a generous sum. His wife, however, had arguments of her own. By the time she had finished, the generous sum had shrunk to an occasional basket of fruit in season. Treated as visitors in the house they had always called home, Mrs Dashwood and her girls resolved to leave as soon as they could find somewhere to go.

Their departure was delayed, however, by the arrival of the wife's brother, Edward Ferrars. He was neither handsome nor brilliant in company, and his ambitious mother despaired of making him a statesman, a lawyer or anything else that would bring the family glory. What Edward wanted was a quiet life, a few good books and a useful occupation in the country. Elinor, who saw past his awkward manner to the honest mind beneath, came to value him more than she cared to admit. Marianne thought him agreeable but sadly lacking in fire, since he read poetry, in her words, as if it were a list of household accounts. Yet even Elinor noticed that Edward was often low in spirits, as though some private obligation pressed upon him. When the family finally went away, he spoke no word about the future.

A distant cousin of Mrs Dashwood, a hearty, hospitable gentleman, offered them a cottage on his land at Barton, in the green hills of the west country. The cottage was small, and its rooms were plain, but the valley around it was lovely enough to make even Marianne forgive the narrow staircase. Their cousin, who could not bear an empty dinner table, insisted on their company nearly every evening. Among his guests was Colonel Brandon, a serious, reserved man of about thirty-five, whose quiet attention to Marianne's playing did not escape her sister. Marianne herself treated the idea with scorn, declaring that a man of such advanced years, who complained of a pain in his shoulder, could hardly feel anything worth calling love.

The man who did meet her standards appeared, as such men do in stories, at precisely the right moment. Running down a hillside with her younger sister one wet afternoon, Marianne slipped and twisted her ankle. A young gentleman out with his dogs carried her home in his arms. His name was Willoughby, and within a week it seemed that fate had designed him for her alone. He admired the same poets, sang the same songs and shared her contempt for anything conventional. He called at the cottage every day, begged a lock of her hair as a keepsake, and behaved in every respect like a man about to propose. Elinor, watching with growing unease, could find no evidence that any engagement had been formally made, and Marianne thought it beneath her to ask.

Then, one morning, the whole romance collapsed without warning. Willoughby arrived pale and unsettled, announced that his wealthy relation had sent him to the capital on business, and admitted that he had no plans to return to Barton soon. He left within the hour, and Marianne passed the following days in a state of grief that she seemed almost determined to deepen, refusing food, sleep and conversation alike. Elinor, whose own feelings were scarcely calmer, could only remind her mother that nothing had been promised and nothing, therefore, could be demanded.

It was at this moment that the cousin's household acquired two new guests, the Steele sisters, and with them Elinor's private trouble took a sharper form. Lucy Steele, the younger, was pretty, watchful and ingratiating, and she attached herself to Elinor with a determination that was difficult to escape. On a walk one afternoon, after swearing her companion to secrecy, she confided that she had been engaged to Edward Ferrars for four years. They had met while he was a pupil of her uncle, she explained, and they dared not tell his mother, who intended him for a far richer bride. To remove any doubt, she produced a miniature portrait of Edward and mentioned, with careful innocence, a ring he wore that contained a lock of her hair. Elinor heard all this with a composure that cost her more than anyone could guess. She kept her word so faithfully that for four months not even Marianne suspected a thing.

That winter an older friend of the family, a talkative widow with a large heart and a larger house, carried both sisters off to the capital. Marianne sent note after note to Willoughby's lodgings, and each day that passed without a reply sharpened her hope into a kind of panic. When at last they met him at a crowded evening party, he bowed to her with chilling formality and turned back to a fashionable young woman at his side. The next morning brought a letter of perfect politeness and perfect cruelty, in which he returned her notes and her lock of hair and regretted any misunderstanding. Within days the whole town knew that he was to marry a wealthy young heiress whose fortune would rescue him from his debts. He had behaved very badly, and no amount of explanation could make his conduct look otherwise.

Lucy's secret, too, could not be kept forever, and when it reached Edward's mother her fury was magnificent. She ordered him to give Lucy up; Edward, believing that a promise made in youth was still a promise, refused. She therefore transferred his inheritance to his younger brother, a vain young man who spent his days choosing toothpick cases. Colonel Brandon, moved by the injustice, offered Edward a modest living as the minister of a small parish on his own estate. With a refinement of irony that she did not fail to appreciate, it fell to Elinor to convey the offer to Edward herself.

In spring the sisters at last turned towards home, breaking the journey at the country house of friends. There Marianne, still nursing her sorrow, wandered through the long wet grass in the evening and returned with a cold that soon became a dangerous fever. For several days the household feared for her life, and Colonel Brandon rode through the night to bring Mrs Dashwood to her daughter. Elinor sat beside the bed hour after hour, reading every change in her sister's breathing, until at last the fever broke, and a natural sleep took its place.

On that same night, to Elinor's astonishment, Willoughby himself appeared, having heard that Marianne's life was in danger. He wished, he said, to be thought less of a villain; he had truly loved her, but when his relation cut him off, he had chosen money over affection. Elinor listened, pitied him a little, and judged him no less severely, for regret did not undo what he had done. When Marianne, slowly recovering, heard the story, she was able at last to see her past conduct clearly, and she resolved to govern her feelings rather than be ruled by them.

The final surprise belonged to Edward. Hearing from a servant that "Mr Ferrars" had married Lucy, the family assumed the worst, and Elinor endured the news in silence. Days later Edward rode up to the cottage and explained, with a mixture of embarrassment and relief, that Lucy had married his brother instead, the brother who now possessed the fortune. Released from a promise he had long regretted, he asked Elinor to be his wife, and she, who had kept her composure through everything, burst into tears of happiness. They settled in the parsonage on Colonel Brandon's land, where their income was small and their contentment large.

Marianne, who had once declared that no one could love twice, discovered her mistake gradually and without regret. She came to see in the colonel a patience, a loyalty and a quiet tenderness that no amount of poetry could have taught her to value at seventeen. At nineteen, she married him, and the heart she had once given entirely to Willoughby became, in time, as entirely his.
