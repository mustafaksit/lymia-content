# A Case of Identity

Summary: A young woman asks Sherlock Holmes to find the man who vanished on their wedding morning.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): typewriter, letters, disguise

## Level A1

Sherlock Holmes lived in Baker Street. His friend Watson often came to see him. One morning, a young woman visited them. Her name was Mary Sutherland.

"Please help me, Mr. Holmes," said Mary. "I must find a man. His name is Hosmer Angel."

Mary lived with her mother and Mr. Windibank. Mr. Windibank was her mother's new husband. Mary had her own money every year. But she gave it all to her family. Mr. Windibank kept her at home.

Then Mr. Windibank went to France for work. Mary and her mother went to a dance. There Mary met Hosmer Angel. He wore dark glasses. He had a big mustache. His voice was very low. "My eyes are bad," said Hosmer.

Hosmer and Mary often walked together at night. Soon they planned to marry. Hosmer sent her many letters. He wrote every letter on a typewriter. He typed even his name at the end.

"Always be true to me," said Hosmer. "Wait for me, Mary." Mary and her mother got into a cab. Hosmer got into a second cab. At the church, the second cab stopped. But no one was in it. Mary never saw Hosmer again.

Holmes read the letters. "These letters come from a typewriter," said Holmes. Then he wrote to Mr. Windibank. Mr. Windibank answered on a typewriter too.

That evening, Mr. Windibank came to Baker Street. "Look at your letter," said Holmes. "The E is never clear. The R is not right. Hosmer's letters have the same problems." Mr. Windibank's face went white.

"You are Hosmer Angel," said Holmes. "The glasses and the mustache were a disguise. You came to Mary as Hosmer. Now she waits for Hosmer. She does not look at other men. So her money stays with you." Mr. Windibank ran out of the house.

"The law can't stop him," said Holmes. "Do you want to tell Mary?" asked Watson. "No," said Holmes. "She loves Hosmer very much. She can't believe it."

## Level A2

Sherlock Holmes and Watson sat by the fire in Baker Street. A young woman came to see them. Her name was Mary Sutherland. "Mr. Holmes, please help me," she said. "I must find Hosmer Angel. We planned to marry, but he left me on our wedding day."

Mary lived with her mother and her mother's second husband, Mr. Windibank. He was only five years older than Mary. Mary got some money every year from her uncle. She also typed letters for other people. She lived at home and gave all her money to her family. Mr. Windibank never let Mary go out.

One winter, Mr. Windibank went to France for his work. While he was away, Mary and her mother went to a dance. There Mary met Hosmer Angel. He wore dark glasses because his eyes were weak. He had a big mustache and a very low voice.

Soon Hosmer and Mary walked out together in the evenings. Before long, he asked Mary to marry him, and she said yes. He never gave her his home address. He typed all his letters, even his name at the end.

A week before the wedding, Hosmer asked Mary for a promise. "Always be true to me," he said. "Wait for me, always." Mary gave him her promise.

On the wedding day, Mary and her mother got into a cab. Then Hosmer got into a second cab. At the church, the second cab stopped, but no one got out. The driver looked inside, and the cab was empty. "I saw the man get in," said the driver. After that, Mary heard nothing more from Hosmer.

Mary gave Holmes four of Hosmer's letters. "Forget this man, Miss Sutherland," said Holmes. "I can't," said Mary. "I made a promise, and I must wait for him."

"Why does a man type his own name, Watson?" asked Holmes. "He does not want Mary to see his real writing." Then Holmes wrote a letter to Mr. Windibank.

The next evening, Mr. Windibank came to Baker Street. "I got your letter, Mr. Holmes," he said. "But nobody can find Hosmer Angel." "You answered me on a typewriter," said Holmes. "Every typewriter is a little different. On your machine, the E is never clear. The R is always a little wrong too. Hosmer Angel's letters have the same two problems."

Mr. Windibank's face went white. "You are Hosmer Angel," said Holmes. "The glasses, the mustache and the low voice were your disguise. You wanted to keep Mary's money in your house. So you came to her as Hosmer, and she loved him. Then you left her at the church. Now she waits for Hosmer and does not look at other men." "And her mother knew about this?" asked Watson. "Yes, she helped him," said Holmes. "And he got out of the cab by the other door."

"The law can do nothing to me," said Mr. Windibank. "You are right," said Holmes. Mr. Windibank ran out of the house. "Do you want to tell Miss Sutherland?" asked Watson. "No," said Holmes. "She loves Hosmer too much. She can't believe me."

## Level B1

On a gray autumn morning, Sherlock Holmes was standing at the window in Baker Street. Below him, a young woman was walking slowly up and down the street. "She wants to ask for our help, Watson," said Holmes. "But she cannot decide." Soon she knocked at the door. Her name was Mary Sutherland.

Holmes looked at her sleeves and hands for a moment. "You do a lot of typing, I think," he said. Mary was surprised. "Yes, I type letters for other people," she said. "But how did you know?" Holmes only smiled.

Mary lived with her mother and her stepfather, Mr. Windibank. Her mother married him soon after her father's death. He was only five years older than Mary. Mary had a small income from an uncle in New Zealand. Because she lived at home, she gave all the money to her mother and stepfather. Mr. Windibank worked for a London company and often traveled to France. He did not want Mary to have friends, and he kept her at home.

One winter, while Mr. Windibank was in France, Mary and her mother went to a dance. There Mary met a gentle, shy man called Hosmer Angel. He wore tinted glasses because, he said, his eyes were weak. He had a thick mustache and dark whiskers, and he spoke only in a soft whisper. "I was very ill as a child, and my voice never got better," he explained.

Hosmer visited the house, and soon they were walking out together in the evenings. "I am a quiet man, and I don't like crowds," he told her. Within a week, he asked her to marry him. He said that he worked in an office in the city and slept there too. So Mary sent her letters to a post office, and Hosmer collected them. He typed all his own letters to her, even the name at the bottom.

A few days before the wedding, Hosmer asked Mary for a strange promise. He put her hand on a Bible. "Promise me that you will always be true to me," he said. "Whatever happens, you will wait for me." Mary promised, and her mother agreed.

On the wedding morning, Hosmer came to the house with two cabs. He helped Mary and her mother into the first one, and he got into the second. At the church, Mary stepped out and waited for him. The second cab stopped at the door, but nobody came out of it. When the driver looked inside, the seat was empty. "I saw him get in with my own eyes," the driver said. Mary did not hear from Hosmer again after that day.

"Something terrible happened to him," Mary said sadly. "I think he knew that some danger was coming. That is why he asked for my promise." Holmes took four of Hosmer's letters and a description of him. "My advice is to forget him," he said gently. "I can't do that, Mr. Holmes," said Mary. "I will wait for him, as I promised."

After she left, Holmes studied the letters closely. "Look, Watson," he said. "Every letter comes from a typewriter, even the name at the bottom. Why would a man type his own name? Because Mary would know his real handwriting." That evening Holmes wrote two letters. One went to Mr. Windibank's company, and the other went to Mr. Windibank himself.

The next evening, Mr. Windibank arrived. He was a polite young man with a smooth face and sharp gray eyes. "I got your letter, Mr. Holmes," he said. "But you will never find this Hosmer Angel." "Your answer came from a typewriter," said Holmes. "Every typewriter has its own small faults. On your machine, the letter E is never clear, and the R has a broken tail. Hosmer Angel's letters show exactly the same faults."

Mr. Windibank sat down heavily, and his face went white. "You were Hosmer Angel," said Holmes quietly. "You wore tinted glasses, false whiskers and a false mustache, and you spoke in a whisper. You wanted to keep Mary's money in your house. So you made her love you, and then you disappeared at the church. Because of her promise, she will wait for Hosmer for years. And her mother knew everything and helped you." "But how did he leave the cab?" asked Watson. "He went in at one door and out at the other," said Holmes.

"The law cannot touch me," said Mr. Windibank with a cold smile. "That is true," said Holmes. "But you did a cruel thing to a kind young woman." Holmes stood up, and Mr. Windibank ran out of the room and down the stairs. "Will you tell Miss Sutherland?" asked Watson. "No," said Holmes. "She loves Hosmer too much, and she will not believe me."

## Level B2

Sherlock Holmes and Watson were sitting by the fire in Baker Street one autumn morning, talking about ordinary life. "Real life is far stranger than anything a writer could invent," said Holmes. As he spoke, he glanced down at a young woman in a large hat who was hesitating on the opposite pavement. "That is the look of a woman with a problem of the heart," he remarked. A moment later, the bell rang, and Miss Mary Sutherland was shown into the room.

She was a friendly, open-faced young woman, but she was clearly troubled. Holmes studied her for a moment. "With your short sight, isn't it hard to do so much typing?" he asked. Mary stared at him. "How did you know that?" "It is my business to notice things," Holmes replied. "Now, please tell me why you have come."

"I want you to find Mr. Hosmer Angel," she said. "He disappeared on the morning of our wedding." Then she explained her situation. Her father had died some years before, and her mother had married again. Her new husband, Mr. Windibank, was much younger than her mother and only five years older than Mary herself. Mary had a small income of her own from an uncle in New Zealand, and she earned more by typing. While she lived at home, however, she handed it all over to her mother and stepfather. Mr. Windibank, who traveled for a London company, did not like her to have friends of her own. "He always said that a family should be happy within its own circle," she told them.

When he went to France on business, though, Mary and her mother went to a dance, and there she met Hosmer Angel. He was a quiet, shy gentleman who wore tinted glasses to protect his weak eyes. He had bushy whiskers and a mustache, and an illness in childhood had left him able to speak only in a whisper. He called at the house, and Mr. Windibank was clearly displeased. As soon as her stepfather went back to France, however, the two began to walk out together in the evenings. Within a week they were engaged.

Hosmer told her that he worked as a cashier in an office in the city. He never gave her his home address, saying that he slept at the office. So she sent her letters to a post office, where he collected them. His own letters to her were all typewritten, and even the signature at the bottom was typed. Strangely, he insisted that her letters to him should be written by hand, because typed ones felt cold to him.

A few days before the wedding, Hosmer made her place her hand on the Bible and swear to be true to him. "Even if something unexpected comes between us, promise me that you will wait," he urged her. Her mother, who was very fond of Hosmer, said that he was right to ask it. The wedding was arranged for a Friday morning at a quiet church in the city.

On the morning itself, Hosmer arrived with two cabs. He put Mary and her mother into the first and climbed into the second himself. When they reached the church, Mary stepped down and watched the second cab pull up behind them. Nobody got out. The driver climbed down and found the seat empty, though he swore he had seen the gentleman get in. "Since that morning, I have not heard a single word from him," Mary said.

"What do you think happened?" asked Holmes. "I believe he saw some danger coming, and that is why he made me promise," she replied. Her mother refused to discuss it, and her stepfather simply said that she would hear from Hosmer again in time. Holmes asked for four of the letters and a description of the missing man. "Leave the matter with me," he said gently, "and try to forget Mr. Angel." "I cannot," she answered firmly. "I shall be true to him and wait."

When she had gone, Holmes leaned back with his eyes half closed. "The letters are the key, Watson," he said. "Look at the signature. It is typed. A man who types his own name at the end of a love letter must have a good reason." "And what reason could that be?" asked Watson. "He was afraid that she would recognize his handwriting." That afternoon Holmes wrote two letters, one to Mr. Windibank's company and one to Mr. Windibank himself. He asked Mr. Windibank to call at six the next evening.

Exactly at six, Mr. Windibank arrived, a smooth-faced, polite young man with sharp gray eyes. "I received your letter," he said, "but I do not see how you can find this Hosmer Angel." "On the contrary, I believe I have found him," said Holmes quietly. Mr. Windibank's eyes moved quickly to the door. "Your note to me was typed," Holmes went on. "A typewriter has as much character as a man's handwriting, and no two machines write exactly alike. On yours, the letter E is always a little blurred, and the tail of the R is missing. Every one of Hosmer Angel's letters shows the same faults. I also sent a description of Hosmer to your company, leaving out the glasses, the whiskers and the whisper. They replied at once that it matched one of their own travelers: you."

Mr. Windibank sank into a chair, his face pale. "It is not a crime," he muttered. "No, sadly, the law cannot touch you," Holmes said. Then he turned to Watson and explained the whole plan. "Here was a young man living comfortably on his stepdaughter's money. If she married, the money would leave the house with her. So, with her mother's help, he put on tinted glasses, false whiskers and a mustache. He turned his clear voice into a whisper and courted her himself. Then he made her swear to be faithful and vanished, in through one door of the cab and out through the other. After such a promise, she would refuse every other man for years."

"You may be right," said Mr. Windibank, getting up with a sneer, "but you can do nothing about it." Holmes rose from his chair, and Mr. Windibank did not wait for another word. He rushed out of the room and down the stairs. "There goes a cold-hearted man," said Holmes. "And Miss Sutherland?" asked Watson. "Will you tell her?" "She would not believe me," Holmes replied. "She has given her heart to a man who never existed, and no words of mine will take it back."

## Level C1

Sherlock Holmes liked to say that no novelist could invent anything half as strange as the ordinary business of daily life. He was explaining this theory to Watson one gray autumn morning when he broke off and stepped to the window. On the pavement opposite, a young woman in a broad hat stood hesitating, moving towards their door and then back again, like a swimmer afraid of cold water. "When a woman hesitates like that, Watson, the trouble is nearly always one of the heart," Holmes remarked. Then, with sudden resolution, she crossed the street, and a minute later Miss Mary Sutherland was shown upstairs.

She was a good-natured young woman, plainly dressed and plainly anxious, and she sat holding her small bag as though it contained her last hope. "Do you not find," he asked, "that with your short sight it is tiring to do so much typing?" Mary Sutherland stared at him, then laughed in spite of herself; he had read both facts from her sleeves and the marks of her glasses. "I came to ask you about Mr. Hosmer Angel," she said, recovering herself.

Her story, once begun, ran on with very little prompting. Her father, a plumber, had died some years earlier, and her mother had soon married again. Her choice was a Mr. Windibank, nearly fifteen years younger than herself and only five years older than Mary. She had, besides, a modest income of her own, the interest on money left to her by an uncle in New Zealand, and she added to it by typing documents at home. As long as she lived under her mother's roof, however, every penny went into the family purse. Mr. Windibank, who traveled on business for a London firm, held strict views about young women: they ought to stay at home, he said, and find their company within the family.

It was during one of his business trips to France that Mary, with her mother's encouragement, went to a dance, and there she met Mr. Hosmer Angel. He was a gentle, retiring man, quite unlike anyone she had known. He wore tinted glasses against the light, for his eyes were weak, and bushy whiskers and a heavy mustache hid most of his face. An illness in childhood, he explained, had left him able to speak only in a soft, hoarse whisper. Mr. Windibank, on his return, was displeased to hear about the dance, but he soon went back to France, and in his absence the courtship moved quickly. Hosmer would only walk out with her in the evenings, saying that he disliked being noticed, and within a week of their first walk he had asked her to marry him.

He was a cashier, he said, in an office in the city, and since he slept on the premises he gave her no home address at all. Her letters went to a post office, where he called for them, and his letters came back to her in a steady stream, every one of them typewritten. Even the signature at the foot of each page had been typed. Oddly enough, he insisted that she write to him by hand, because a typed letter from her, he said, made him feel that a machine stood between them.

In the last week before the wedding, Hosmer grew strangely solemn. He asked her to lay her hand on the Bible and swear that, whatever happened, she would remain true to him. Her mother, who could not praise him enough, told her he was quite right to ask it, since it proved his devotion. Mr. Windibank was in France again, and the wedding was fixed for a Friday morning at a small church in the city.

On that morning Hosmer called for them with two cabs. He handed Mary and her mother into the first, a light two-wheeled cab, and climbed into the second, a closed four-wheeler, himself. They reached the church first, and Mary stepped down onto the pavement, turned, and watched the four-wheeler draw up behind them. Nobody got out. When the driver climbed down and opened the door, the seat was empty, and the bewildered man could only repeat that he had seen the gentleman step in. That was the last anyone had seen of Hosmer Angel.

"I am sure some danger threatened him," Mary said, her voice unsteady. "Why else would he make me swear such a thing? He must have known that something was coming." Her mother, oddly, had grown angry and forbidden her to mention the matter, while her stepfather had merely assured her that she would hear from Hosmer in due course. Holmes took four of the letters and a description of the bridegroom, then kindly advised her to let Mr. Angel vanish from her life as he had vanished from the cab. "I cannot do that, Mr. Holmes," she said, rising. "I gave my word, and I shall keep it. I shall wait for him."

When the door had closed behind her, Holmes spread the letters across his knee. "Have you noticed the signature, Watson?" he asked. "It is typed, like the rest," said Watson. "Precisely, and why should a lover type his own name at the end of a love letter, unless he fears that his handwriting might be recognized?" "Recognized by whom?" asked Watson. "That," said Holmes, reaching for his pen, "is what two letters of mine will tell us." One of them went to Mr. Windibank's firm in the city, and the other to Mr. Windibank, inviting him to call at Baker Street at six o'clock the following evening.

Watson arrived a little before the hour and found his friend dozing in his armchair, the room heavy with the sharp smell of the chemical experiments he loved. At six exactly, Mr. Windibank came in: a sturdy, clean-shaven man of about thirty, polite and smooth, with a remarkably sharp pair of gray eyes. He took the chair Holmes offered and explained, with patient regret, that his stepdaughter was excitable and that nobody could find a man who did not wish to be found. "On the contrary," Holmes said pleasantly, "I have every reason to think that I shall find him." Something flickered across the visitor's face, and his glance went to the door.

"You answered my note on a typewriter," Holmes continued, "and that was careless of you. Every machine has its own little habits, as personal as a signature: a letter worn down here, another slightly out of line there. On yours, the E is always faintly blurred and the tail of the R has broken off. There are other marks as well, but those two are enough, for they appear in every one of Hosmer Angel's letters. Your employers, moreover, kindly answered my inquiry. I sent them a description of Mr. Angel with the glasses, the whiskers and the whisper left out, and they recognized one of their own travelers at once."

Mr. Windibank sat very still; then he sank back in his chair, and the color drained from his face. "It was only a joke at first," he muttered, "and in any case it is not a crime." "Unfortunately, you are right," said Holmes, "and the law cannot touch you, though I have seldom met a more heartless piece of selfishness." Turning to Watson, he laid out the whole scheme as calmly as a lecturer. "Here was a man living comfortably on his stepdaughter's income, which would leave the house on the day she married. Since he could not keep suitors away forever, he decided, with his wife's knowledge and help, to become the suitor himself. Tinted glasses hid his eyes, false whiskers hid his face, and a whisper disguised his voice, while her short sight did the rest. Once she was engaged, he needed only to vanish in a way she would never forget, and a sworn promise would keep her waiting, and unmarried, for ten years at least. The cab was simple enough: he stepped in at one door and quietly out at the other."

Mr. Windibank rose, buttoning his coat, and some of his confidence had returned. "You may be very clever, Mr. Holmes, but you have no power over me," he said with a thin smile. Holmes rose too, and something in his expression convinced the visitor that the conversation was over. He was through the door and clattering down the stairs before another word was spoken. "That fellow will go from one cold scheme to the next until one of them ruins him," he said.

"And Miss Sutherland?" Watson asked. "Surely you will tell her?" Holmes shook his head. "She would never believe me. She has given her loyalty to a man who never existed, and to take it from her now would be to take the one bright thing in her life. Let her keep her faith a little longer; time will teach her what I cannot."
