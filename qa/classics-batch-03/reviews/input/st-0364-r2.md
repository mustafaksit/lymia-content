# King Thrushbeard

Summary: A proud princess mocks every man who wants to marry her, until her angry father makes a choice.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): princess, beggar, pots, pride

## Level A1

Once there was a young princess. She had too much pride. No man was good enough for her.

Her father had a big party. Many men came to marry her. The princess laughed at every man. "You are too short!" she said to one. Then she saw a good young king. "Look at his chin!" said the princess. "It is like a bird's mouth. I call him King Thrushbeard!"

Her father did not like this. "Now you must marry a beggar," he said. Soon a beggar came to the door. He sang for them. "Now marry this man," said her father. They married that same day.

They walked for a long time. At last, they came to a big city. The princess asked, "Who owns this city?" "King Thrushbeard," said the beggar. "Why did I laugh at him?" she said.

Then they came to a very small house. "This is our home," said the beggar. The princess had to do all the work. But she did not know how.

The princess sold pots at the market. Then a man came by on a horse. His horse ran into the pots. All the pots broke. The princess ran home and told the beggar. "Work in the castle kitchen," he said. She took food home in little pots.

One evening the castle had a wedding. The princess looked in at the door. She thought about her old pride. Then King Thrushbeard came in. She knew his face from the party. "Come and dance with me," he said. She said no, but he took her inside. Her pots fell, and the food fell out. All the people laughed. The princess ran away. King Thrushbeard ran after her.

"Do not be afraid," said King Thrushbeard. "I am the beggar from the small house. I love you. I wanted to make your pride small." "I was wrong," said the princess. "I am not good enough for you." "The bad days are over," said King Thrushbeard. "Now we can have our wedding." Her father and all his people came. It was a very good day for all.

## Level A2

Once there was a beautiful princess with far too much pride. To her, no man was good enough.

One day her father had a great party. Men came from near and far to marry her. The princess had something unkind to say about each man. "This one is too short," she said. Then she came to a good young king. "Look at his chin!" she laughed. "It is like the mouth of a little bird. I call him King Thrushbeard!"

Her father was very angry with her. "You must marry the first beggar at my door," he said. A few days later, a poor man sang under the window. Her father called him inside, and the beggar sang for them. "I like your song," said her father. "You can have my daughter as your wife." The princess was afraid, but her father did not change his mind. So she married the beggar that same day.

The beggar and the princess walked a long way. They came to a big forest and then to a large city. The princess asked, "Who owns all this?" "King Thrushbeard," said the beggar. "It is not yours, because you said no to him." "Why did I say no to King Thrushbeard?" said the princess.

At last, they came to a very small house. "This is our home now," said the beggar. "Make a fire and make our food." The princess did not know how. Then he gave her other work, but she did it badly.

"You can sell pots at the market," said the beggar. He bought some pots, and she sat with them in the market. But then a man came very fast on a horse. His horse ran right through her pots. Every pot broke into small pieces. The princess ran home and told the beggar. "You cannot do this work," he said. "Go and work in the castle kitchen." So the princess did the hardest jobs in the kitchen. She carried two small pots with her. She took food home in them.

One evening there was a big wedding at the castle. The princess stood at the door of the great room. She thought about her old pride, and she was sorry.

Then the young king of the castle came in. He took her hand. "Come and dance with me," he said. The princess said no, because she knew his face. It was King Thrushbeard! She laughed at him at her father's party. But he took her into the room. Her pots fell, and the food went all over the floor. Everyone laughed at her. The princess ran out of the door and down the stairs. But King Thrushbeard caught her there and brought her back.

"Do not be afraid," said King Thrushbeard. "I am the beggar from the little house. I did it all because I love you. Your pride was too big, and I wanted to make it small." "I was very wrong," said the princess. "I am not good enough to be your wife." "The bad days are over," said King Thrushbeard. "Now we can have our wedding." Her father came with all his people. It was a very happy wedding.

## Level B1

There was once a king with a daughter who was very beautiful. But she was so proud that no man was good enough for her.

One day the king gave a great feast for all the men hoping to marry her. The princess found something wrong with each of them. One was too short for her, and another had a face that was too red. At last, she came to a good young king with a slightly crooked chin. "Look at that chin!" she laughed. "It is just like a thrush's beak. I will call him King Thrushbeard!" From that day on, everyone used that name for him.

The old king was angry with his daughter. "You will marry the first beggar who comes to my door," he said.

A few days later, a poor singer stood under the castle window and sang for coins. The king heard him and told his servants to bring him in. The beggar sang for the king and the princess. "Your song pleased me so much that I will give you my daughter," said the king. The princess was frightened, but her father did not change his mind. So she married the beggar that same day. "A beggar's wife cannot stay in my castle," said her father.

Then the beggar and the princess came to a large forest. "Who owns this beautiful forest?" asked the princess. "King Thrushbeard owns it," answered the beggar. "It could be yours too. You only had to say yes to him." "Why didn't I marry King Thrushbeard?" said the princess. Then they crossed a green meadow and reached a great city. Each time she asked about the owner, she heard the same answer. "Am I not good enough for you?" asked the beggar.

At last, they reached a tiny house with a low door. "This is our home," said the beggar. "There are no servants, so you must do the work yourself." But the princess knew nothing about fires or cooking, so the beggar had to help her. They lived like this for a few days, and then all their food was gone. She tried to make baskets and to spin thread, but she could do neither.

"I will buy some pots," said the beggar, "and you can sell them at the market." Many people bought pots from the pretty young woman. They lived on this money for a while, and then the beggar bought new pots. This time the princess sat at the corner of the market with her pots around her. Suddenly a man came riding fast through the market. His horse ran straight into the pots, and they all broke. The princess began to cry, because she did not know what to do. She ran home and told her husband everything. "Who sits at the corner of a market with pots?" he said. "Stop crying. I went to the king's castle today, and they need a kitchen maid. They will take you."

So the princess became a kitchen maid, and she did the dirtiest jobs. She carried two small pots in her pockets. Every evening she took food home in them.

Some time later, a great wedding was going to take place at the castle. The princess went up and stood by the door of the great hall to watch. The candles were burning, and the guests were wearing beautiful clothes. She thought about her own life, and she felt sorry for her old pride. The servants gave her bits of food, and she put them into her pots.

Then the young king of the castle came in, wearing silk and gold. He saw the princess at the door, took her hand and asked her to dance. She said no, and she felt afraid. She knew his face: it was King Thrushbeard. She once laughed at him at her father's feast. But he pulled her into the hall. The string around her pockets broke, and the pots fell to the floor. Soup ran across the floor, and bits of food went everywhere. Everyone in the hall laughed, and the princess wished she could disappear. She ran out, but on the stairs a man caught her and brought her back. It was King Thrushbeard.

"Do not be afraid," he said kindly. "I was the beggar who lived with you in the little house. I did it all because I love you. You laughed at me, and I wanted to make your pride smaller." The princess cried. "I was very wrong," she said. "I am not good enough to be your wife." "The bad days are over," said King Thrushbeard. "Now we will have our wedding." Maids came and dressed her in the finest clothes. Her father arrived with his whole court to wish her joy.

## Level B2

A king once had a daughter whose beauty was known far and wide. Yet she was so proud that no man who asked for her hand was ever good enough.

So the king gave a great feast and invited all the young men who wished to marry her, from near and far. They were placed in a long row according to rank, with the kings at the front. The princess was led along the row, and she found fault with every single one. "Too round," she said of the first. "He looks like a ball." "Too long and thin," she said of the second. "He sways like a tree in the wind." The third was too short for her taste, and the fourth had a face as red as a rooster. At last, she reached a good young king whose chin was a little crooked. "Just look at him!" she cried, laughing. "His chin is like a thrush's beak. I shall call him King Thrushbeard!" From that day on, he was known by that name.

The old king was furious when he saw how his daughter mocked all his guests. "I swear that you will marry the first beggar who comes to my door," he told her.

A few days later, a wandering singer stood beneath the castle window and sang, hoping for a few coins. When the king heard him, he ordered the man to be brought inside. The beggar sang for the king and the princess, and then he asked for a small gift. "Your song has pleased me so much that I will give you my daughter as your wife," said the king. The princess was frightened, but her father would not change his mind. "I gave my word, and I intend to keep it," he said. And so she was married to the beggar that very day. "A beggar's wife does not belong in a king's castle," her father declared. "You must go with your husband now."

The beggar led her away on foot, and before long they came to a great forest. "Who owns this lovely forest?" asked the princess. "King Thrushbeard owns it," replied the beggar. "If you had married him, it would all be yours." "Poor foolish me," she sighed. "If only I had married King Thrushbeard!" Then they crossed a wide green meadow and came to a splendid city. Each time she asked about the owner, she received the same reply. At last the beggar grew annoyed. "Why do you keep wishing for another husband?" he asked. "Am I not good enough for you?"

Finally, they reached a tiny house, and the princess had to bend her head to get through the low door. "Who does this poor little place belong to?" she asked. "It is ours," said the beggar. "This is where we will live." "There are no servants," he told her. "You must do the work yourself. Light the fire, put some water on and cook our supper." The princess knew nothing about fires or cooking, so the beggar had to lend a hand. They lived like this for a few days, until all their food had been eaten. He fetched thin willow sticks for her to weave into baskets, but the work was far too hard for her soft hands. Then he set her to spinning thread, but she was no better at that.

"I will buy some pots," said the beggar, "and you can sell them at the market." People were happy to buy pots from such a pretty young woman, and some paid without even taking their pots. The couple lived on this money for a while, and when it was spent, the beggar bought a new load of pots. This time the princess sat down at the corner of the market and set out her pots around her. Suddenly a man on horseback came galloping through the market, and his horse ran straight into the pots. In a moment every single pot lay in pieces. The princess burst into tears and had no idea what to do. She ran home and told her husband what had happened. "Who sits at the corner of a market with pots?" he said. "Stop crying. I have been to the king's castle to ask whether they need a kitchen maid, and they have agreed to take you."

So the princess became a kitchen maid. She helped the cook and did the dirtiest jobs in the kitchen. She tied two small pots inside her pockets, and every evening she filled them with leftover food and carried it home. That was what she and her husband lived on.

Some time later, it was announced that a great wedding would take place at the castle. The princess went upstairs and stood by the door of the great hall to watch. The candles were lit, and the guests came in one after another, each more splendid than the last. As she watched, she thought sadly about her own life, and she regretted the pride that had brought her so low. Now and then the servants handed her bits of food, and she put them carefully into her pots.

Then the young king of the castle entered, dressed in silk and velvet, with gold chains around his neck. When he saw the princess standing at the door, he took her by the hand and asked her to dance. She refused in fright, because she saw that he was King Thrushbeard, the man she had mocked at her father's feast. But he pulled her into the hall. At that moment the string that held her pockets broke, and the pots fell to the floor. Soup ran across the floor, and bits of food went everywhere. Everyone in the hall burst out laughing, and the princess was so ashamed that she wished the ground would swallow her. She ran out of the door, but on the stairs a man caught up with her and brought her back. It was King Thrushbeard.

"Do not be afraid," he said kindly. "The beggar who lived with you in that little house was me. I did all this because I love you. You were too proud, and you laughed at me in front of everyone. I wanted to soften that proud heart of yours." The princess wept bitterly. "I have done a great wrong," she said, "and I am not worthy to be your wife." "Comfort yourself," said King Thrushbeard. "The hard days are over, and now we shall celebrate our wedding." Maids came and dressed her in the finest clothes. Her father arrived with his whole court, and they all wished her happiness in her marriage.

## Level C1

There was once a king whose daughter was more beautiful than any girl in the land, and who knew it rather too well. So proud and haughty was she that no man who asked for her hand was ever good enough, and each suitor left with his ears burning from her mockery.

Hoping to find her a husband at last, the king held a great feast and invited every young man who wished to marry her, whether he came from near or far. The guests were arranged in a long row according to their rank, with the kings at the front, then the dukes, princes and counts, and finally the barons. The princess was led slowly down the line, and she had a sharp word ready for every face she passed. "Look at that one, as round as a pumpkin," she said of the first, and the second, who was tall and thin, she likened to a young tree swaying in the wind. The third was too short and stout to please her, the fourth too pale, as white as a freshly painted wall, and the fifth too red, like a rooster in the farmyard. The sixth did not stand straight enough, and she declared that he looked like green wood left to dry behind the stove. In this way she found something to laugh at in every single suitor, but she saved her sharpest joke for a good young king who stood near the head of the row. His chin, as it happened, was very slightly crooked. "Oh, just look at him!" she cried, laughing aloud. "His chin is exactly like a thrush's beak. I shall call him King Thrushbeard!" The name stuck, and from that day on he was known by no other.

The old king, watching his daughter make fools of his guests one after another, grew red with anger. "Since you will have none of them," he told her, "I swear that you shall marry the first beggar who comes knocking at my door."

A few days later a wandering singer, poor and travel-stained, stood beneath the castle window and began to sing, hoping that someone inside might throw him a coin or two. When the king heard the song, he ordered the man to be brought in at once. Dressed in his dusty rags, the beggar sang for the king and the princess, and when he had finished, he bowed and asked for a small gift. "Your song has pleased me so much," said the king, "that I will give you my daughter for your wife." The princess was horrified and begged him to relent, but her father would not hear of it. "I swore an oath," he said, "and I mean to keep it." A priest was sent for, and before the day was out the proud princess had become the beggar's wife. "A beggar's wife has no place in a king's castle," her father told her coldly. "Go now, and follow your husband wherever he leads."

The beggar led her out of the castle on foot, and before long they entered a vast forest whose trees rose high above their heads. "Who owns this beautiful forest?" asked the princess. "It belongs to King Thrushbeard," the beggar replied. "It might all have been yours, had you accepted him." "Poor foolish creature that I am," she sighed, "if only I had taken King Thrushbeard!" They crossed a wide green meadow, and then they came to a great, splendid city, and each time she asked who the owner was, she received exactly the same answer. At length the beggar lost patience with her. "It does not please me at all to hear you wishing for another husband," he said. "Am I not good enough for you?"

Their journey ended at a tiny house so low that the princess had to stoop to pass through its door. "Whose miserable little hut is this?" she asked. "Ours," said the beggar. "This is where we shall live from now on." She looked about her for the servants, but he only shook his head. "There are no servants here," he told her. "Whatever needs doing, you will do yourself. Light the fire, put the water on and cook our supper, for I am tired." Yet the king's daughter had never lit a fire or cooked a meal in her life, and the beggar had to lend a hand himself before anything came of it. They managed in this way for a few days, until the little food they had was gone. "Wife," said the beggar, "this cannot go on; you must earn something." He went out and cut some willow sticks, and he set her to weaving baskets, but the stiff sticks were far too tough for her soft hands. Next he gave her thread to spin, and she fared no better at that.

"You are not cut out for this kind of work," said the beggar. "I will buy a stock of pots and dishes, and you can sit in the market and sell them." To her surprise, things went well at first. People were glad to buy from so pretty a young woman, and some of them paid her and walked off without even taking what they had bought. For a while the couple lived on what she earned, and when the money ran out, the beggar bought a fresh load of pots. This time she chose a place at the corner of the market and set out her wares all around her. Then, quite without warning, a man on horseback came galloping through the market, and his horse plunged straight into the pots. In an instant every one of them lay shattered on the ground. The princess burst into tears and had no idea what to do. She ran home to her husband and told him of her misfortune. "Whoever heard of sitting at the corner of a market with pots?" he said. "Stop crying; I can see you are fit for no ordinary work. I have been up to the king's castle and asked whether they need a kitchen maid, and they have promised to take you on."

So the princess became a kitchen maid, at everyone's call and given the dirtiest tasks. She fastened a small pot inside each of her pockets, and every evening she filled them with leftover food and carried it home. On that, she and her husband lived.

Some time later, word spread that a great wedding was to be held at the castle. The poor woman crept upstairs and stood in the doorway of the great hall, wanting to see the splendor for herself. The candles were lit, and the guests swept in one after another, each more richly dressed than the last. As she watched, she thought sadly about her own lot, and she bitterly regretted the pride and arrogance that had brought her so low. Now and then a servant tossed her a morsel from the dishes being carried past, and she tucked it carefully into her pots to take home.

Then the young king of the castle came in, clothed in silk and velvet, with heavy gold chains around his neck. Catching sight of the lovely woman in the doorway, he took her by the hand and asked her to dance. She drew back in fright, for she recognized him at once: it was King Thrushbeard, the very suitor she had mocked so cruelly at her father's feast. Her refusal made no difference; he drew her into the hall. At that moment the string that held up her pockets snapped, and down fell the pots, so that soup spread across the floor and scraps of food scattered in every direction. A roar of laughter went up from the whole company, and she was so ashamed that she longed for the floor to open and hide her. She fled through the door, but on the stairs a man caught up with her and led her back. When she looked at him, she saw that it was King Thrushbeard once more.

"Do not be afraid," he said gently. "The beggar who shared that wretched little hut with you and I are one and the same. I put on those rags because I loved you. You were too proud, and you mocked me in front of everyone, so I wanted to humble that haughty heart of yours." At this she wept bitterly. "I have done a great wrong," she said, "and I am not worthy to be your wife." "Take comfort," he told her. "The hard days are behind us, and now we shall celebrate our wedding." The maids came and dressed her in the most splendid clothes, and her father arrived with his whole court to wish her joy in her marriage. Only then did true happiness begin for the princess and King Thrushbeard.
