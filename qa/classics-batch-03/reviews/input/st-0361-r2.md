# Arachne the Weaver

Summary: A young weaver in Lydia boasts that her work is better than a goddess's.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): loom, spider, contest, thread

## Level A1

A young woman named Arachne lived in Lydia. Her home was in a small town. Her family did not have much money. But Arachne could do one thing very well. She could weave. She made pictures with thread on her loom. Her work looked like real life.

People came from far away to see her. "Your work is very good," they said. "Athena showed you all this." Athena was a goddess. Arachne did not like their words. "No one showed me," said Arachne. "I am better than Athena. Let her come here. We can have a contest."

Athena heard about these words. So she came down to Lydia. But she came as an old woman. "Listen to me, child," said the old woman. "Be the best weaver among people. But do not try to beat a goddess. Say sorry to Athena now. She can still be kind to you."

Arachne did not change her mind. "Go away, old woman," said Arachne. "Why does Athena not come here?" "She is here," said the old woman. Then the old woman changed. Now she was Athena, the goddess. All the other people put their heads down. Arachne's face changed color for a moment. But she still wanted the contest.

So Athena and Arachne sat at two looms. They worked very hard. Athena showed the gods on her loom. They looked strong and great. Arachne showed the gods too. In her picture, the gods did bad things.

Then Athena looked at Arachne's work. She looked very hard. But she could find no problem in it. This made Athena very angry. "You think you are better," said Athena. "Now you must learn a lesson."

Then Athena used her power on Arachne. Arachne's hair fell away. Her head became very small. Her body became small too. Her fingers became long legs. Arachne was now a spider.

But the spider still had her thread. She still made good work with it. And spiders still weave today.

## Level A2

Long ago, a young woman named Arachne lived in Lydia. Her home was a small town, and her family was poor. But Arachne had one great skill. She could weave better than anyone in the country. On her loom, she made pictures with colored thread. People wanted to touch the pictures because they looked so real.

People came from far away to watch her. "Your hands move so easily," they said. "Athena must be your teacher." Athena was the goddess of this kind of work. Arachne did not like these words. She wanted people to talk only about her. "Nobody taught me," said Arachne. "I am better than Athena. Let her come and have a contest with me."

Athena heard about this, and she was not pleased. She came down to Lydia in a different form. Now she looked like an old woman with white hair. "Listen to an old woman, child," she said to Arachne. "Old people know many things. Be the best among people. But do not put yourself above Athena. Say sorry to her now. She can still be kind to you."

Arachne looked at the old woman with cold eyes. "You are old, and your mind is not clear," said Arachne. "Talk to your own daughters, not to me. Why does Athena not come here herself?" "She is here," said the old woman. Then the old woman changed back into her true form. She was Athena herself. The other people there put their heads down. Only Arachne stood up straight. Her face turned red for a moment. But she did not change her mind about the contest.

So Athena and Arachne set up two looms side by side. They both worked quickly, and their threads moved like light. Athena made a picture of the twelve great gods. They sat in their high seats, strong and calm. She also showed her contest with the sea god for a city. At the sides, she made four small pictures of people. These people fought against the gods and lost. It was a clear message for Arachne.

Arachne made her own picture of the gods. But in her picture, they lied to people and did wrong things. Her work was full of life. Every thread was in the right place.

Athena studied Arachne's work for a long time. She could find no problem in it. This made Athena very angry. Arachne's work was too good, and she showed no respect. Athena pulled the cloth off the loom and tore it apart.

Then Athena used her power on Arachne. "You love your thread so much," said Athena. "So you must make thread all your life, you and your children." Arachne's hair fell away. Her head and body became very small. Her long fingers became legs. Arachne was now a spider.

The spider still had her old skill. She still made fine thread from her own body. And from that day on, spiders weave, just like Arachne.

## Level B1

Long ago, in the country of Lydia, there lived a young woman named Arachne. She came from a small town, and her family had very little money. Her father earned his living by coloring wool a deep purple. Her mother had died, so Arachne grew up in a simple, quiet home. Yet everyone in Lydia knew her name, because nobody could weave like Arachne.

On her loom, she made pictures with colored thread. The pictures looked so real that people almost expected the figures in them to move. Visitors came from far away just to watch her hands at work. Even the spirits of the hills and rivers left their homes to see her. They loved the way she turned rough wool into soft, fine thread.

"Your skill is a gift from Athena," the visitors said. "The goddess of weaving must be your teacher." Arachne did not like this at all. She wanted people to praise her, not the goddess. "Nobody taught me anything," Arachne said proudly. "I am better than Athena. Let her come here and have a contest with me. I will accept any punishment from her, but she will never beat me."

Athena heard these proud words, and she decided to visit Arachne. But she did not come as a goddess. She came as an old woman with white hair, who walked slowly with a stick. "Listen to me, my child," said the old woman. "Old age brings some good things, and one of them is experience. Try to be the best weaver among people. But do not put yourself above a goddess. Ask Athena to forgive you for your words. She will forgive you, because she is kind to people who are sorry."

Arachne stopped her work and looked at the old woman angrily. "You are old, and your mind is weak," said Arachne. "Give your advice to your own daughters. I can think for myself. Why does Athena not come here? Why is she afraid of a contest?" "She has come," said the old woman. At once, the old woman threw off her disguise and stood there as Athena. The spirits and the women of Lydia fell to their knees in front of the goddess. Arachne was the only one who stayed standing. Her face went red for a moment, then turned pale again. But she did not change her mind, and Athena gave her no more warnings.

The two weavers set up their looms side by side and began to work. Their hands were moving quickly, and the colored threads were flying across the looms. Athena showed the twelve great gods sitting calmly on their high seats. She showed her own contest with the god of the sea for a famous city. In the four corners, she wove small pictures of people who fought the gods. Every one of them lost, and the gods turned them into mountains, birds or stone. Athena hoped that Arachne would understand the warning.

Arachne made a very different picture. She showed the gods at their worst, telling lies and playing tricks on people. Her work was full of life, and every color was exactly right. Around the edge, she wove a border of flowers and green leaves.

Athena looked closely at Arachne's work, but she could find no mistake in it. This made the goddess even angrier. Arachne's work was as good as her own, and it showed no respect for the gods. Athena pulled the cloth from the loom and tore it apart.

Then she sprinkled Arachne with drops from a magic plant. "You love your thread so much," Athena said. "So you will make thread forever, you and all your children after you." At once, Arachne's hair fell away, and her head became tiny. Her body grew very small, and her long fingers turned into thin legs. Arachne was now a spider.

But she still had her old skill. From her small body, she pulled out a fine thread and began to weave. And spiders still weave in the same way today.

## Level B2

Long ago, in the country of Lydia, there lived a young woman named Arachne, whose family was far from important. Her father earned a modest living in a small town by dyeing wool a rich purple. Her mother, who came from the same simple background, had died some years before. Yet Arachne's name was known in every town of Lydia, because nobody alive could weave as she did. Her little town was of no importance, but her skill had made her famous far beyond it.

People traveled for days just to watch her at her loom. Even the nymphs of the mountain and the river left their woods and streams to see her. They enjoyed not only the finished work but also the work as it was being made. She rolled the rough wool into soft balls, pulled it out into long, cloud-like strands, and spun it into fine thread. Her thumb turned the spindle smoothly, and the thread grew longer and longer without a single break. Then, with a needle, she added pictures so lifelike that the figures seemed ready to step out of the cloth.

"Athena herself must have taught you," the visitors said. To them, this was the highest praise, since Athena was the goddess of weaving and of every fine skill. Arachne, however, was offended, as if the visitors had taken something from her. She did not want to share her glory with anyone, not even with a goddess. "Nobody taught me," she said, lifting her chin. "Let Athena come and compete with me. If I lose, I will accept whatever punishment she chooses."

Athena heard of this challenge and decided to give the girl a chance first. She disguised herself as an old woman, with gray hair at her temples and a stick to support her weak legs. "Not everything about old age is bad," she said to Arachne in a gentle voice. "Long years bring experience, so do not ignore the advice of someone older than you. Seek fame as the greatest weaver among mortals, but give way to the goddess. Ask her forgiveness humbly for your proud words, and she will forgive you."

Arachne threw down her thread and glared at the old woman. "Age has made you foolish," she said coldly. "You have simply lived too long. If you have daughters, give your advice to them. I know how to look after myself, and nothing you say will change my mind. Why does the goddess not come here herself? Why is she avoiding this contest?" "She has come," the old woman replied, and she threw off her disguise. There stood Athena in her true form. The nymphs and the women of Lydia bowed down before her. Only Arachne remained unafraid, though a sudden red rose in her cheeks and then faded, like the sky at dawn. She held firmly to her challenge, eager for the prize and blind to the danger. Athena did not refuse, and she gave no further warning.

They set up two looms side by side and stretched the long threads tight. Both weavers worked quickly, their dresses tied up at the chest, and their skilled hands hardly paused. Purple threads and threads of gold ran across the looms. The colors changed as gently as the colors of a rainbow after a storm. Each shade was clearly different from the next, yet the eye could not see where one ended.

Athena wove the twelve great gods, seated in calm majesty on their high thrones. The king of the gods sat in the middle, and each of the others looked exactly like himself or herself. In the center she showed her own contest with the god of the sea for the right to name a famous city. The sea god made a spring of salt water rush out of a rock. Athena's gift was an olive tree, heavy with fruit, and the watching gods were amazed. They judged her the winner, and the city took her name. In each of the four corners, she placed a smaller scene as a warning to her rival. Each one showed mortals who had dared to compete with the gods and had been turned into mountains, birds or stone. Around the edge, she wove a border of olive leaves, the sign of peace, and with that her work was finished.

Arachne's picture told a very different story. She showed the gods behaving badly: lying, disguising themselves to fool mortals and using their power unfairly. She gave every god the right face and placed each scene in the right setting. Each scene was so real that the people in it seemed to breathe. The sea, the sky and the animals all looked exactly as they do in life. Around her work, she wove a narrow border of flowers and climbing leaves.

Athena studied the cloth closely, searching for a single fault. She found none, and even the most jealous eye could not have found anything wrong with it. Arachne's success, and the disrespect in her pictures, filled Athena with anger. She pulled the cloth from the loom and tore it apart, with all its stories of the gods.

Then Athena turned to Arachne herself. "Since you love your thread so dearly," she said, "you will keep making it forever, and so will all your children." She sprinkled Arachne with the juice of a magic herb. At once Arachne's hair fell away, and her head shrank to a tiny size. Her whole body became small, and her slender fingers stuck to her sides as thin legs. The rest of her became a round little body. Arachne had become a spider.

Yet she had not lost her skill. From her own body she still draws out a fine thread, and she still weaves her delicate webs. Spiders have done the same ever since.

## Level C1

In the old days, when the gods still walked among mortals and took a keen interest in their affairs, there lived in Lydia a young woman named Arachne. Nothing about her birth promised greatness, for her father earned a modest living in a small and unremarkable town by dyeing wool a deep, rich purple. Her mother, a woman of the same humble background, had died some years before, leaving father and daughter to manage on their own. Yet the name of Arachne had traveled through every town of Lydia, carried from mouth to mouth by those who had seen what her hands could do.

What they had seen was weaving of a kind the country had never known. So remarkable was her work that the nymphs of the mountain and the river would leave their shady woods and cool streams simply to stand beside her loom. It was not only the finished cloth that drew them, but the making of it, which held a grace of its own. She would roll the rough wool into soft balls, draw it out into long strands as light as clouds, and twist it into thread with a quick turn of her thumb. Then, with a fine needle, she would bring pictures to life, and the figures she created seemed ready at any moment to step down from the cloth and walk away.

"Surely Athena herself has taught you," the watchers would say, and they meant it as the warmest praise they could offer. Athena, after all, was the goddess of weaving and of every skill that called for clever hands and a patient mind. Arachne, however, heard no praise in the remark, only an insult, as though someone had reached out and taken from her the thing she valued most. She had no wish to share her glory with anyone, least of all a goddess she had never met. "Nobody taught me anything," she said, lifting her chin. "Let Athena come and compete with me, and if she defeats me, I will accept whatever punishment she sees fit."

Word of the challenge soon reached Athena, who chose, at first, to answer it with patience rather than with power. She took the shape of an old woman, with thin gray hair at her temples and a stick to support her unsteady legs, and in that form she came to Arachne. "Not everything that age brings is to be despised," she began in a mild voice. "Long years give us experience, and experience is worth listening to, so do not turn away from the advice of someone older than you. Seek all the fame you like as the finest weaver among mortals, but give way to the goddess. Ask her forgiveness, humbly, for the proud words you have spoken, and she will grant it, for she does not refuse those who come to her with an honest heart."

Arachne let the thread fall from her fingers and turned on the old woman a look as cold as winter. "Your mind has grown weak with your years," she said, "and it is plain that you have lived far too long. If you have daughters, or daughters-in-law, by all means give them your advice, for they may have some use for it. I have none, since I am perfectly able to look after myself, and nothing you say will move me an inch. Why does the goddess not come here herself? Why is she so careful to avoid this contest?" "She has come," the old woman replied, and with those words she cast off her disguise.

Where the bent figure had stood a moment before, Athena now stood in her true form. The nymphs bowed low before her, and the women of Lydia bowed with them, each one eager to show her respect. Arachne alone remained unafraid, though a sudden red rose in her cheeks and faded again, just as the sky blushes at dawn and grows pale once the sun is up. She held fast to her challenge, eager for the prize and blind to the danger she was running toward. Athena did not refuse, nor did she waste another word of warning on a girl who would not listen.

Without delay, the two of them set up their looms side by side and stretched the long threads tight. They worked with their dresses tied up at the chest, and their skilled hands moved so swiftly that the hours seemed to pass unnoticed, so great was the pleasure of the work. Threads of purple ran across the looms, along with threads of gold, and each new color melted into the next. It was like a rainbow after a storm, when the eye can tell that two neighboring shades are different and yet cannot say where one ends and the other begins.

On her loom, Athena wove the twelve great gods, seated in solemn majesty on their high thrones, with the king of the gods at their center. Each of them looked exactly like himself or herself, so that any viewer could have named them at a glance. In the middle of the scene, she showed her own old contest with the god of the sea, which had been held to decide who should give a famous city its name. The sea god made a spring of salt water rush out of a rock, but Athena's gift was an olive tree, heavy with gray-green fruit. At the sight of it, the watching gods were filled with wonder. They judged her the winner, and the city took her name. In each of the four corners, she placed a smaller scene, meant as a warning to her rival, of mortals who had dared to compete with the gods. Every one of them had lost, and every one had been turned into something else, a mountain, a bird or a block of stone. Around the whole design she wove a border of olive leaves, the sign of peace, and with that her work was done.

Arachne's loom told a very different story. Across it, she set out the gods at their worst: lying, disguising themselves to fool unsuspecting mortals, and using their great power without any sense of fairness. She gave every god the right face and placed each scene in its proper setting, so that the sea looked like the sea and the sky like the sky. The figures were so real that they seemed to breathe. Around the whole picture ran a narrow border of flowers and climbing leaves.

Athena studied the finished cloth with a long, searching look, hunting for even a single fault. She found none, and not even the most jealous eye in the world could have found one. It was precisely this success, together with the disrespect that ran through every scene, that filled the goddess with anger. She pulled the cloth from the loom and tore it apart, with all its stories of the gods and their misdeeds.

Then Athena turned her attention to the weaver herself. "Since you love your thread so dearly," she said, "you shall go on making it forever, and so shall all your children after you, to the end of time." She sprinkled Arachne with the juice of a magic herb, and the change began at once. Arachne's hair fell away, and her head shrank until it was tiny, while her whole body grew smaller and smaller. Her slender fingers clung to her sides as thin legs, and what remained of her became a small, round body. The proud weaver of Lydia had become a spider.

Yet the goddess had not taken away her art. From her own body, Arachne still draws out a fine thread, and she still weaves her delicate webs in quiet corners, just as patiently as she once worked at her loom. Her children have done the same in every age since.
