# The Stolen White Elephant

Summary: A rare white elephant goes missing in New York, and a famous detective starts a very expensive search.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): elephant, detective, telegram, reward

## Level A1

The King of Siam had a white elephant. He wanted to give it to a queen. She lived in a land far away. I worked for him. My job was to take her the elephant.

It was a long way. On the way, we stopped near New York. The elephant stayed in a big house. But one morning, it was not there. Someone took it in the night! I felt very bad.

I went to the New York police. There I met Blunt, a great detective. Blunt asked me a lot of questions. "How big is the elephant? What does it eat?" he asked. He wrote down every answer. Then Blunt looked at me. "My men can find your elephant. But first, I need money for a reward."

I gave him a lot of money. Blunt sent his detectives to every town. He put the news in every paper too. Soon the telegrams came. "The elephant is here!" said one telegram. "It ate all our food!" said another. "It came into our school!" said one more. But each telegram came from a different town. These towns were very far from each other.

Blunt liked every telegram. "We are close now!" he told me. But the detectives always needed more money. They needed trains, food, and rooms. So I paid, and I paid again. After many weeks, I had no money left.

Then, one morning, Blunt ran into my room. "We found the elephant!" he said. Where was it? It was under the police station! There was a big room down there. No light came into that room. The elephant was there, and it slept. Nobody saw it, because there was no light.

The detectives felt great. "We are the best detectives!" they said. The papers wrote good things about Blunt. I lost all my money on this job. But I still think Blunt is great.

## Level A2

The King of Siam had an argument with a queen far away. Later, he wanted to be friends with her again. So he decided to send her a special present, a white elephant. In Siam, a white elephant is a very important animal. I worked for the king, and he gave the job to me. I had to take the elephant to her.

The trip by sea was long and hard. On the way, we stopped in a town near New York. The elephant needed rest, so it stayed there for two weeks. Then, one morning, the elephant was gone. Someone took it away in the night! I could not believe it.

I ran to the police station in New York. There I met Blunt, a very famous detective. Everyone in the country knew him. He was a quiet, serious man, and he listened well. Then he asked me a long list of questions. "How big is the elephant?" asked Blunt. "How old is it? What does it like to eat?" I told him everything, and he wrote it all down. The elephant was about nineteen feet high. It ate almost anything, even books and clothes.

Blunt called his best detectives into the room. "Find this elephant. Look in every town and every field," he told them. Then he turned to me. "We must offer a reward of twenty-five thousand dollars," said Blunt. I agreed, and I paid for everything. The story about the elephant was soon in every newspaper.

Soon the telegrams started to arrive. They came every hour, from all over the country. "The elephant is here, in our town," said the first one. "It walked through a farm and ate everything," said the next one. "It broke the door of our train station," said another. Some telegrams came from towns very far from each other. But a few of them arrived at the same time. Blunt read each one with a happy face. "Every telegram brings us closer," he told me.

But the search cost a lot of money. The detectives traveled by train from town to town. They needed hotels, food, and new clothes. Every week, I paid their bills. The reward grew bigger and bigger. After a few months, my money was nearly all gone.

Then, early one morning, Blunt came to me with a big smile. "Come with me. We have news for you," he said. We went down to a room under the police station. It was large and dark. At the back of the room, the elephant lay on the floor. It was in a deep sleep. It was there in the building, very near the detectives!

All the detectives stood around Blunt and called out his name. "Blunt is the best detective in the world!" they said. The next day, every newspaper said good things about him. For me, the story ended badly. I lost my job and all my money. But even now, I think Blunt is a great detective.

## Level B1

Some years ago, the King of Siam quarreled with a great queen across the sea. The quarrel was about a line on a map, and it soon ended. Still, the king wanted to show his good feelings. So he decided to send the queen a very special present: a white elephant. In Siam, a white elephant is a sacred animal, and only kings can own one. I was working for the king at the time, and he trusted me. My task was to bring the elephant safely to the queen.

We traveled for many weeks by sea. On the way, we stopped in a town across the river from New York. The elephant was weak after the long journey, so I let it rest there. For two weeks, everything went well. Then, one morning, I found its stall empty. Somebody took the elephant away during the night! I was shocked, and I had no idea what to do.

I rushed to police headquarters in New York and asked for Inspector Blunt. Everyone knew his name, because he was the most famous detective in the country. He was a calm, heavy man who spoke slowly and thought carefully. He listened to my story without moving at all. Then he took out a notebook and began to ask questions. "What is the elephant's name?" asked Blunt. "How tall is it? How long is its trunk? What does it usually eat?" I answered everything. The elephant was about nineteen feet tall, and it ate almost anything. It once ate a whole pile of books, and it seemed to enjoy them. Blunt wrote every detail in his notebook and nodded.

Next, Blunt called a group of detectives into his office. "This is a difficult case. Thieves took the elephant, and we will catch them," he told them. He sent them out in every direction, to towns, farms, and railway stations. Then he turned to me with a serious look. "We need a reward. Twenty-five thousand dollars will help people to remember things," said Blunt. I agreed at once. By the next day, the whole story was in every newspaper.

Soon the telegrams began to arrive at headquarters. They came day and night, from all parts of the country. "The elephant is here, and we saw it this morning!" said one. "It walked through a farmer's garden and ate all his cabbages," said another. A third telegram said the elephant knocked down a fence near a church. The strange thing was the distance between these places. Some of the towns were hundreds of miles apart, yet the telegrams arrived together. Blunt did not seem worried about this at all. "Every message brings us closer," he said calmly.

Meanwhile, the search was costing me a fortune. The detectives needed train tickets, hotel rooms, meals, and horses. Each week, I received a new pile of bills, and I paid them all. Blunt also raised the reward, first to fifty thousand and then to one hundred thousand dollars. He even put notes in the newspapers for the thieves. He was planning to meet them and buy the elephant back. Weeks turned into months, and my money slowly disappeared.

Then, early one morning, Blunt came into my room. His face was shining with pride. "Come with me," he said. I followed him to police headquarters, and we went down into the basement. It was a huge, dark place full of old boxes. In the farthest corner lay the white elephant, fast asleep. It was right there, under the feet of the detectives! Nobody noticed it, because nobody ever went into that dark corner.

The detectives gathered around Blunt and cheered loudly. "Three cheers for Blunt, the greatest detective alive!" they cried. The newspapers praised him the next day, and his fame grew even greater. As for me, I lost everything. My job was gone, and so was every dollar I had. Yet, even today, I believe that Inspector Blunt is a truly wonderful detective.

## Level B2

Some years ago, a disagreement arose between the King of Siam and a powerful queen across the sea. It concerned a border between their lands, and it was settled without much trouble. Even so, the king wished to show that he held no hard feelings. He decided that the finest present he could offer was a white elephant. In Siam such an animal is considered sacred, and only a king may own one. I was then serving in the king's government, and I was given the honor of delivering it.

The voyage was long, and by the time we reached America the elephant looked tired and thin. We stopped in a town just across the river from New York so that it could recover its strength. For two weeks it rested quietly, eating well and growing stronger every day. Then, one morning, I went to its stall and found it empty. During the night the elephant had vanished, and nobody had seen or heard a thing. My whole career depended on that animal, and I felt sick with worry.

I went straight to police headquarters in New York and asked for Inspector Blunt. His name was known everywhere, for he was said to be the cleverest detective in the country. He turned out to be a large, calm man with a slow voice and a steady gaze. He listened to my story without interrupting, as if I were describing a lost umbrella. When I had finished, he opened a thick notebook and began his questions. "First, I need a complete description," said Blunt. "Tell me its height, its weight, the length of its trunk, and anything unusual about it." I explained that it was nineteen feet tall and as white as fresh milk. "And what does it eat?" he asked. I told him that it would eat almost anything, including furniture, clothing, and books. Blunt wrote all of this down with great care, as though every word might decide the case.

Then he called in his detectives, and the office soon filled with quiet, serious men. "Gentlemen, a valuable elephant has been stolen," Blunt announced. "The thieves cannot hide an animal of this size for long, so we must move quickly." He divided the country into areas and sent his men off by the next trains. He also ordered thousands of copies of the elephant's description to be printed. Finally, he turned to me and proposed a reward of twenty-five thousand dollars. I accepted without hesitation, since I would have paid anything to get the elephant back. Before I left, I asked him what he thought had happened. Blunt leaned back in his chair and smiled for the first time. "An elephant does not simply wander off on its own," he said. "This was planned by experienced criminals, and I already have a good idea who they are." He mentioned two well-known thieves, although he admitted that one of them was in prison at the time. This did not seem to weaken his theory in the slightest. I left his office feeling that the elephant was as good as found.

By the following morning, the case was the main story in every newspaper. Shortly afterward, the telegrams began to pour in from all over the country. One reported that the elephant had passed through a small farming town at dawn. Another said that it had eaten a farmer's entire supply of hay and then wandered off. A third claimed that it had knocked down a fence and frightened a herd of cows into the river. A circus owner even offered money for the right to paint advertisements on its sides. What puzzled me was that some of these towns were hundreds of miles apart. Yet the messages often arrived within minutes of each other. Blunt, however, was not troubled in the least. "Each telegram brings us closer," he told me, smiling patiently.

Meanwhile, the expenses were growing at an alarming rate. Every detective needed tickets, hotel rooms, meals, and occasionally a horse or a boat. At the end of each week I received a mountain of bills, and I paid every one of them. Blunt soon advised me to double the reward, and then to double it again. He also placed mysterious messages in the newspapers, addressed to the thieves themselves. He told me privately that he was arranging a secret meeting to buy the elephant back. I trusted him completely, even though my savings were almost gone.

Weeks became months, and the telegrams kept arriving. The elephant, if the reports were true, was traveling faster than any train. My government sent me anxious letters, and I had no good answers for them.

Then, early one morning, Blunt appeared at my door, glowing with triumph. "Come with me," he said, and he would explain nothing more. We drove to police headquarters and went down a narrow staircase into the basement. It was an enormous, gloomy space, lit by only a single lamp. There, in the darkest corner, lay the white elephant, sound asleep on a pile of old sacks. It had been there, under the feet of the entire police force, for who knows how long. No one had thought to look in that corner, because no one ever went there.

The detectives crowded around their chief and cheered until the walls shook. "Three cheers for Blunt, the greatest detective who ever lived!" they shouted. Reporters arrived within the hour, and the next day every newspaper sang his praises. Not one detective seemed to think that the search had been strange in any way.

As for me, the adventure left me ruined. I lost my position, and the reward and the expenses had swallowed every dollar I owned. And yet, even now, I cannot help admiring Inspector Blunt. In my opinion, he remains the greatest detective the world has ever known.

## Level C1

Some years ago, a minor dispute over a border arose between the King of Siam and a powerful queen whose lands lay far across the sea. The matter was settled quickly and politely, but the king, being a generous man, wished to leave no trace of ill feeling behind. After much thought, he concluded that nothing could express his goodwill better than a white elephant, the rarest and most honored gift his kingdom could offer. In Siam, you must understand, a white elephant is a sacred creature, and only a monarch may possess one. I held a modest post in the king's service at the time, and it was to me, of all people, that the task of delivering this treasure was entrusted.

The voyage proved long and exhausting, and by the time we reached the coast of America, the poor elephant had grown visibly thin. I therefore arranged for it to rest in a town just across the river from New York, where it might recover before the final crossing. For two peaceful weeks it ate, slept, and regained its strength, and I began to congratulate myself on a mission almost completed. Then, one gray morning, I walked into its stall and found nothing there but straw. Overnight, an animal the size of a small house had disappeared without a sound, and with it, I feared, my entire career.

Hardly pausing to think, I hurried to police headquarters in New York and asked to see Inspector Blunt. His reputation was enormous; newspapers spoke of him as a man who could solve any mystery merely by sitting still and thinking about it. In person, he was large, unhurried, and almost alarmingly calm, with the patient expression of someone who has long since stopped being surprised by anything. He heard my story from beginning to end without a flicker of emotion, as though I had come to report a missing umbrella rather than a missing elephant. When I had finished, he drew a thick notebook toward him, dipped his pen, and began an examination of astonishing thoroughness.

"I shall need a complete description," said Blunt. "Height, weight, length of trunk, the condition of its ears, and any habits or peculiarities, however trivial they may seem." I told him that the elephant stood nineteen feet tall and was as white as fresh milk. He asked about its family, its age, its temper, and even the exact shape of its tail, and he recorded every answer with the gravity of a judge. "And its diet?" he inquired at last. I confessed that it would eat practically anything, including furniture, clothing, and books, and that it seemed to have no particular preference among them. Blunt received this information with a thoughtful nod, as if it confirmed a suspicion he had held all along.

He then summoned his detectives, and within minutes the office was crowded with silent, watchful men in dark coats. "Gentlemen, a valuable elephant has been stolen," Blunt announced, in the tone another man might use to mention the weather. "No thief can conceal a creature of this size for long, so we shall begin at once." With a few brisk orders he divided the entire country into districts and dispatched his men to the nearest railway stations. He arranged for thousands of descriptions to be printed and posted, and only then did he turn to me to discuss a reward. We agreed on twenty-five thousand dollars, a sum I would gladly have tripled to see my elephant again.

Before I left, I ventured to ask what he believed had happened. Blunt allowed himself a small, superior smile. "Elephants do not wander off by accident," he said. "This is the work of professionals, and I can already tell you who they are." He named two notorious thieves, and when I pointed out, with some hesitation, that one of them was then sitting in prison, he dismissed the objection with a wave of his hand. Such details, he explained, were of no importance to a mind that had already grasped the larger pattern.

By the next morning the theft was the talk of the nation, and soon after that the telegrams began to arrive. They came in a steady stream, then in a flood, from farms, villages, and cities in every corner of the land. One announced that the elephant had passed through a sleepy farming town at dawn, scattering chickens in every direction. Another reported that it had devoured a farmer's whole winter supply of hay and then strolled calmly away. A third declared that it had flattened a fence and sent a herd of startled cows splashing into a river. A circus owner, scenting an opportunity, offered a handsome fee for permission to paint advertisements on its sides. What struck me as curious was the geography: towns hundreds of miles apart often reported the elephant within the same hour.

Blunt, however, found nothing curious about it at all. He read each telegram with deep satisfaction, pinned it to an enormous map, and declared that the net was tightening. "Every message brings us nearer to the truth," he assured me, with the serene confidence of a man who has never once doubted himself. I wanted very much to believe him, and so, naturally, I did.

Meanwhile, the cost of the search was rising at a pace that would have alarmed a far richer man than me. Each detective required train tickets, hotel rooms, meals, and, now and then, a horse, a boat, or a guide. Every Saturday a fresh mountain of bills landed on my desk, and every Monday I paid it. On Blunt's advice I doubled the reward, then doubled it once more, until it stood at a hundred thousand dollars. He also began placing mysterious notices in the newspapers, addressed to the thieves in a private code of his own invention. He confided to me that negotiations were going well and that he would soon meet the criminals in person to buy the elephant back.

Weeks stretched into months, and still the elephant galloped across the country on paper, apparently faster than any express train. My superiors in Siam wrote me letters of growing anxiety, and I answered them with promises I no longer entirely believed. My savings dwindled, then vanished altogether, and I began to borrow.

Then, one bright morning, Blunt appeared at my lodgings, his broad face shining with triumph. "Come with me," was all he would say, and he would say nothing more until we reached police headquarters. There he led me down a narrow staircase into a vast, gloomy basement, lit by a single lamp that barely reached the walls. And there, in the farthest and darkest corner, lay the white elephant, sound asleep on a heap of old sacks, breathing as peacefully as a child. It had been resting there, it seemed, for a considerable time, directly beneath the feet of the entire police force. Nobody had thought of searching that corner, for the simple reason that nobody ever went there.

What followed was the proudest moment I have ever witnessed. The detectives gathered around their chief and cheered until dust fell from the ceiling. "Three cheers for Blunt, the greatest detective who ever lived!" they roared, and Blunt accepted their applause with dignified modesty. Reporters came running, and the next day not a single newspaper failed to celebrate his genius. Nor did any of his men appear to find anything odd in the manner of the discovery.

As for me, I emerged from the affair a ruined man. My position was lost, my reputation was in tatters, and the reward and expenses had consumed every dollar I possessed. Yet I find, strangely, that I bear Inspector Blunt no grudge whatsoever. On the contrary, I still regard him as the greatest detective the world has ever produced, and I say so to anyone who will listen.
