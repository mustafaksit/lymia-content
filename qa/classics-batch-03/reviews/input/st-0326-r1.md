# Robin Hood and Little John

Summary: Bored in the forest, Robin Hood goes looking for adventure and meets a giant stranger on a narrow bridge.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): bridge, staff, stream, outlaws

## Level A1

Robin Hood lived in Sherwood. Many men lived there with him. They were outlaws. All of them were Robin's friends.

One morning, Robin wanted something new. "Stay here, my friends," he said. "I want to go out by myself. Come to me at my call."

Robin walked for a long time. Then he came to a stream. A small bridge went over the water. Only one man could go over it.

Robin stepped on the bridge. A big man stepped on the other end. He was much bigger than Robin. "Go back!" said Robin. "No, you go back!" said the big man. The two men did not move.

The big man had a long staff. "Get a staff too," he said. "Then we can fight." "Good!" said Robin. He went away and cut a staff. Then he came back to the bridge.

The two men began to fight. Both of them were very strong. The staffs went up and down. For a long time, no man fell. Then the big man pushed Robin hard. Robin fell into the stream!

Robin sat in the water. Then he began to laugh. "You win, my friend!" said Robin. He came out of the water. Then he called to his friends.

His friends heard him and ran to him. "What happened?" one man asked. Robin showed them the big man. "He put me in the stream!" said Robin. The friends moved toward the big man. "Stop!" said Robin. "He is a good man. And he won the fight."

Robin turned to the big man. "Come and live with us," he said. "Be one of my friends." The big man said yes. "What is your name?" asked Robin. "I am John Little," said the big man.

One of Robin's men laughed. "John Little? But you are so big! Your new name is Little John!" Everyone laughed, and Little John laughed too. That night they all ate together. Little John had a new home.

## Level A2

Robin Hood lived in the green trees of Sherwood. Many strong men lived there with him. They were outlaws, so they made their home far from the towns.

One summer morning, Robin wanted some fun. "Nothing happens here," he said to his men. "Stay here and wait for me. I want to go out by myself today. Listen for my horn, and come quickly when you hear it."

Robin walked through the trees for many hours. At last, he came to a wide stream. There was a bridge over the stream, but it was very small. It was only one long piece of a tree. Two men could not pass on it.

Robin stepped onto one end of the bridge. At the same moment, a stranger stepped onto the other end. He was a very big man, much bigger than Robin. "Go back and let the better man pass!" called Robin. "No, you go back," answered the big man. "I am the better man."

Robin took his bow in his hands. "Do you want me to show you?" he asked. The big man did not move. "You have a bow, and I have only a staff," he said. "A good man does not fight like this."

Robin's face went red. "You are right," he said. He put down his bow and went into the trees. There he cut a good, strong staff. Then he came back to the bridge. "Now we are the same," said Robin. "The first man in the water loses."

They met in the middle of the bridge. The two staffs went up and down very fast. Robin was fast, but the big man was very strong. They fought for a long time, and no man fell. Then the big man gave Robin a great push. Robin went over the side and into the stream.

"Where are you now, my friend?" laughed the big man. Robin sat in the cold water and looked up at him. "I am down here with the fish," he said. Then he laughed too. He walked out of the stream and took his horn. He called his men with it three times.

Soon his men came running through the trees. "What happened to you?" asked one of them. "This man put me in the water," said Robin. The men moved toward the big man. They wanted to throw him in the stream too. "No, stop!" said Robin. "He won the fight, and he is a good man."

Robin turned to the big man. "Come and live with us in Sherwood," he said. "You can have new green clothes and a good bow." The big man smiled and held out his hand. "I want to join you," he said. "My name is John Little."

One of Robin's men began to laugh. "Little? That name is wrong for a man like you. From today, your name is Little John!" All the outlaws laughed and agreed. That night they ate together under the trees. Little John sat next to Robin, and they talked all night.

## Level B1

Robin Hood led a band of outlaws who lived in the forest of Sherwood. They dressed in green, hunted in the woods, and laughed at rich travelers. But one summer, two whole weeks went by without any adventure at all.

"I am tired of sitting under these trees," Robin told his men one morning. "Today I am going out alone to find something exciting. Stay here and listen for my horn. When I blow it three times, come to me as fast as you can."

He took his bow and his arrows and walked off through the forest. For a long time he met nobody. Then, near noon, he came to a wide stream with many stones in it. Across the water lay a narrow bridge, which was only a single log. It was wide enough for one man, but not for two.

Robin was halfway across when a stranger stepped onto the other end. The man was huge, a whole head and shoulders taller than Robin. He carried a thick wooden staff, and he walked with slow, heavy steps.

"Stand back, friend," called Robin. "Let the better man cross first." "Stand back yourself," answered the stranger calmly. "The better man is me, and I am not moving."

Robin took an arrow and put it to his bow. "I can show you who the better man is," he said. The stranger did not look worried at all. "You stand there with a bow, and I have nothing but a stick," he said. "Only a coward fights a man that way."

Robin's face went hot. Nobody ever called him a coward, and he did not like it. "Wait here," he said. "I will cut a staff for myself, and then we will see." He laid his bow on the grass and went into the trees. There he cut a strong young oak branch and made it into a staff. When he came back, the stranger was still waiting in the same place.

"Now we are equal," said Robin. "We will fight on this bridge until one of us falls into the stream." "That suits me well," said the stranger.

They met in the middle of the log. At once the air was full of the loud crack of wood on wood. Robin was quick and clever, and he moved like a cat. The stranger was slower, but he was very strong. Back and forth they went, and neither man gave up a single step. Once Robin almost slipped, and once the stranger nearly lost his balance.

At last the stranger swung his staff with all his strength. It caught Robin on the side and knocked him clean off the bridge. Down he went into the stream with a great splash.

The stranger leaned on his staff and looked down at him. "And where are you now, my brave lad?" he asked. Robin was sitting in the water up to his chest. "I am floating down with the current," he answered. Then he began to laugh, and the stranger laughed with him.

Robin climbed out onto the bank. "You are a fine fighter," he said, "and you won fairly." Then he lifted his horn and blew three loud notes.

Soon there was a sound of running feet among the trees. Will Stutely and many of the outlaws came out of the forest. "Master, what happened?" cried Will. "You are wet from head to foot!" "This fellow knocked me into the stream," said Robin, still smiling.

The men moved toward the stranger. They wanted to give him a bath too. "Leave him alone," Robin ordered. "He beat me in a fair fight, and I like him for it."

Then Robin turned to the big man. "Will you join my band?" he asked. "You will have green clothes, a good bow, and friends for life." The stranger thought for a moment and then held out his hand. "Gladly," he said. "My name is John Little, and I am your man."

Will Stutely laughed so hard that he had to sit down. "John Little?" he said. "That is the wrong name for the biggest man in Sherwood. From now on, we will call you Little John." All the outlaws cheered, and the big man laughed louder than anyone.

That evening they made a great fire and had a feast under the oak trees. Robin gave Little John a new green coat and a long bow. And from that day, Little John was Robin's best friend.

## Level B2

In the days when Robin Hood was still a young man, he lived as an outlaw in the great forest of Sherwood. More than a hundred men followed him, all dressed in green, all skilled with the bow. They lived well enough among the oaks, but sometimes life there became very quiet.

One bright summer morning, Robin stood up and stretched. "For two weeks we have done nothing but sit here," he said to his men. "Today I am going out to look for an adventure of my own. Stay here in the forest and keep your ears open. If I get into trouble, I will blow my horn three times, and you must come at once."

He picked up his bow, put a handful of arrows in his belt, and set off. He walked along dusty paths and through thick bushes, but nothing interesting happened. At last, a little after noon, he came to a broad stream that ran fast over smooth stones. A narrow bridge crossed it, though it was really no more than a single log. Two men could not pass each other on it without one of them getting wet.

Robin had just reached the middle when a stranger stepped onto the far end. He was the tallest man Robin had ever seen, nearly a head and a half above him. His shoulders were broad, and he carried a heavy wooden staff as easily as a feather.

"Stand back, fellow," said Robin, "and let the better man cross first." "Stand back yourself," replied the stranger, without moving an inch. "As far as I can see, the better man is standing at this end."

Robin laughed, but there was an edge to it. He took an arrow from his belt and fitted it to the string. "Perhaps I should prove it to you," he said. The stranger only looked at him with mild disgust. "You stand there with a fine bow aimed at me," he said, "while I hold nothing but a plain staff. In my village, that is how a coward fights."

No one had ever called Robin a coward, and the word stung him. "You are right," he said, lowering the bow. "It would not be a fair contest. Wait for me here, and I will fetch a staff of my own." He laid his bow on the bank and went into the trees. After a short search he found a young oak and cut himself a staff a little longer than his height. When he returned, the stranger was leaning on his staff exactly where he had left him, whistling a tune.

"Here is the bargain," said Robin. "We fight on this bridge, and the first man to fall into the water loses." "Agreed," said the stranger, and he stepped forward.

They met in the middle of the log, and at once the forest rang with the crack of wood against wood. Robin was the quicker of the two, and he darted and dodged like a fox. The stranger was slower, but every blow he gave was like the kick of a horse. Once Robin landed a sharp knock on the big man's side, and the stranger only grunted. Once the stranger nearly swept Robin's feet away, and Robin had to jump to keep his balance. Neither of them would give up a single step, and the fight went on and on.

Then, at last, the stranger gathered all his strength into one great swing. It caught Robin before he could block it, and over he went, heels above his head. He landed in the stream with a splash that sent water flying in every direction.

For a moment the stranger looked down at the bubbles. "Well, my good lad," he called, "and where are you now?" Robin's head came up out of the water, and he shook it like a wet dog. "Floating down with the current, it seems," he said. Then he began to laugh, and soon the stranger was laughing so hard that he had to hold on to his staff.

Robin waded to the bank and climbed out, with water running from his clothes. "You are the best fighter I have met in many years," he said honestly. "I was beaten fairly, and I have no complaint." Then he took his horn from his belt and blew three loud blasts that echoed through the trees.

Before long, the bushes began to shake, and a crowd of men in green came running out. At their head was Will Stutely, Robin's most trusted man. "Good master," he cried, "what has happened to you? You are soaked to the skin!" "This fellow tumbled me into the stream," said Robin, nodding at the stranger.

At once the outlaws seized the big man. "Then he can go in after you," said Will, "and see how he likes it." "No, let him go," said Robin firmly. "He beat me in a fair fight, and he deserves our respect, not a bath."

Robin turned to the stranger, who stood calmly among the men. "Listen, my friend," he said. "Join my band, and you will have good green clothes, a share of everything we gain, and the best company in England. What do you say?" The stranger looked around at the cheerful faces and then held out his great hand. "I say yes, with all my heart," he answered. "My name is John Little, and from today I am yours."

Will Stutely nearly fell over laughing. "John Little!" he said. "The biggest man in the whole forest, and he is called Little. That name must be changed at once. From this hour, we shall call him Little John." The outlaws shouted their approval, and the big man laughed as loudly as any of them.

That evening they lit a great fire among the oaks and held a feast for their new friend. Robin gave Little John a fresh suit of green and a strong bow of his own. And so the man who had knocked Robin Hood into a stream became his closest and most loyal companion.

## Level C1

Robin Hood was little more than a youth when he gathered his outlaws in Sherwood, yet by the summer of this tale he had a hundred men at his back. They wore green so that the leaves would hide them, they could split a willow wand at a hundred paces, and on the whole they lived merrily beneath the oaks. Yet even a merry life can grow dull, and for fourteen days nothing whatever had happened to break the calm.

On the fifteenth morning Robin rose from the grass, stretched until his joints cracked, and announced that he had had enough of idleness. "Two weeks without so much as a single adventure," he said, "and I am beginning to feel like an old man by the fire. I am going out alone to see what the world has to offer. Stay here, all of you, but keep your ears open, for if I find myself in trouble, I shall blow three blasts on my horn, and I expect to see you running."

His men promised, and off he went, his bow over his shoulder and a bundle of arrows at his belt. He followed the edge of the highway for a while, then turned aside along narrow paths where the brambles caught at his sleeves, but the forest seemed determined to stay quiet. It was a little past noon when he came at last to a broad, shallow stream that ran chattering over a bed of pebbles. Across it lay a bridge of sorts, though it was nothing more than a single log, so narrow that two people meeting upon it could not possibly pass.

Robin was halfway over when a figure stepped onto the far end, and he stopped to take a good look at it. The newcomer was, without exaggeration, the tallest man he had ever laid eyes on, standing nearly seven feet in his shoes and broad enough across the shoulders to fill a doorway. In one fist he carried a blackthorn staff, and he held it as lightly as a child might hold a reed. Neither of them made the slightest move to step aside.

"Stand back, fellow," said Robin pleasantly, "and let the better man cross first." "Stand back yourself," came the reply, delivered with perfect calm. "If we are to talk of better men, I rather think you are looking at him."

Robin laughed, though not entirely with pleasure, and drew an arrow from his belt. "Then perhaps I ought to settle the question," he said, laying the arrow to the string. The giant regarded the bow with an expression of weary contempt. "There you stand with a fine longbow pointed at me," he observed, "and here I stand with nothing but a plain stick. Where I come from, we have a word for a man who fights like that, and it is not a kind one."

It was the one charge to which Robin had no answer, for nobody had ever called him a coward, and the thought of deserving the word was unbearable. He let the string go slack and lowered the bow. "You have the right of it," he admitted. "Wait where you are, and I shall cut myself a staff to match yours, and then we shall see who goes back." He laid his bow and arrows on the bank and pushed into the thicket, where he soon found a young oak growing straight and true. From it, he cut a staff some six feet long, trimmed it, and tested its weight as he walked back to the stream. The stranger, meanwhile, had not stirred an inch; he was leaning on his blackthorn exactly where Robin had left him, whistling a tune and watching the water slide by.

"Here are the terms," said Robin, stepping onto the log. "We fight here on the bridge, and whoever is first to go into the stream has lost." "Nothing could suit me better," said the stranger, and he came to meet him.

What followed was the finest bout either of them had ever fought. The two staffs whirled and met with a crack that sent the birds scattering from the branches. For a long while the contest was as even as a pair of scales. Robin, lighter and quicker, danced and feinted and darted in, landing a sharp rap on the big man's ribs that would have doubled up a lesser fellow. The stranger merely grunted, shifted his feet, and answered with a sweeping stroke that Robin escaped only by leaping clear and landing, by some miracle, on the log again. Neither would yield a single step, and both were soon breathing hard, though each was too proud to show it.

At last the stranger gathered his whole enormous strength into one great swing. Robin saw it coming a moment too late, and the blackthorn caught him squarely. With a look of the purest astonishment he sailed off the bridge, heels over head, and vanished into the stream with a splash that soaked both banks.

For a moment there was nothing but ripples, and the stranger peered down at them with genuine interest. "Well, my fine lad," he called, "and where might you be now?" Robin's head rose out of the water, streaming like a spaniel's. "Floating gently downstream with the current," he replied, with as much dignity as he could gather, "and finding it rather cooler than I expected." Then the absurdity of it overcame him, and he began to laugh, and the giant on the bridge laughed with him until he had to lean on his staff to keep upright.

Robin waded ashore and wrung out his sleeves, and when he spoke again there was no trace of resentment in his voice. "I have fought a good many men," he said, "and I have never met your equal with a staff. You beat me fairly, and I bear you no grudge for it." Then he put his horn to his lips and blew three long blasts that rolled away among the trees.

The answer came quickly: a crackling of branches, a drumming of feet, and then a crowd of men in green burst out of the undergrowth with their bows already strung. Leading them was Will Stutely, the most trusted of all Robin's men, who stopped short at the sight of his master. "Good master," he exclaimed, "what in the world has befallen you? You are soaked from head to foot!" "Why, nothing much," said Robin, "except that this tall fellow here has just tumbled me into the stream."

That was enough for the outlaws. Half a dozen of them laid hold of the stranger at once, and Will Stutely declared that the fellow should take a swim himself and see how he enjoyed it. "Let him be," Robin commanded, and they let go at once. "He beat me man to man, with no trick and no advantage, and a fellow like that is owed respect, not a ducking."

Robin turned to the stranger, who had watched the whole scene with untroubled good humor. "Hear me, friend," he said. "Join my band, and you shall have three suits of good green cloth every year, a fair share of all we gain, and the merriest company in the whole of England. What do you say to that?" The giant looked slowly around at the ring of grinning faces, then at Robin, dripping and cheerful, and held out a hand the size of a shovel. "I say yes, and gladly," he answered. "My name is John Little, and from this hour I am your man."

At this, Will Stutely let out such a whoop of laughter that he very nearly toppled into the stream himself. "John Little!" he cried, wiping his eyes. "The biggest man ever to set foot in Sherwood, and he calls himself little! That will never do. A new member of our band deserves a new name, and I hereby give him one: from this day forward, he is Little John." The outlaws roared their approval, and none roared louder than the newly named Little John himself.

That evening they built a great fire in a glade among the oaks and feasted in his honor, with roasted meat, fresh bread, and songs until the stars came out. Robin presented his new comrade with a suit of Lincoln green and a stout longbow of his own, and Little John took his place at Robin's right hand. It was a place he would keep for the rest of their days together, for the man who had knocked Robin Hood off a log became the truest friend he ever had.
