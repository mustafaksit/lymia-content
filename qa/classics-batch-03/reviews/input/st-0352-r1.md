# The Magic Shop

Summary: A father and his little son visit a strange magic shop in London where the magic seems real.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): shop, magic, soldiers

## Level A1

A father and his child walked in London. The child's name was Gip. They were on Regent Street. Gip saw a small shop. "Look! A magic shop!" said Gip. He took his father's hand. "Can we go in, please?" asked Gip. The father said yes.

A tall man stood in the shop. "Is this real magic?" asked Gip. "Yes!" said the man. "We only sell real magic here."

"What is in your pocket?" asked the man. "Nothing," said the father. The man's hand went into the pocket. He took out two little birds! The birds flew around the room. Gip smiled, but the father did not.

Next, the man put down a small box. Then the box began to grow. It grew bigger and bigger. Soon it was as big as a door.

Then the man showed Gip some toy soldiers. He said a magic word. The little soldiers stood up. They walked across the table in a line. "I want them!" said Gip. "They are yours," said the man. He told Gip the magic word.

Gip walked around the big box. Then the father could not see him. "Gip! Where are you?" called the father. There was no answer. The father felt great fear. "Where is my child?" he asked the man. The man only looked at him and smiled.

The father ran and opened the door. Now he was out on Regent Street. And Gip was there, next to him! Gip held some boxes in his arms. The father turned around to look. The magic shop was not there now. He saw only other shops.

At home, Gip opened the boxes. In one box, he found the toy soldiers. Gip said the magic word to them. The soldiers stood up and walked! The father did not understand it. But Gip was home with him. And the toys really were magic.

## Level A2

One afternoon, a father walked in London with his little son. The boy's name was Gip. They were on Regent Street, and there were many people there. Then Gip stopped in front of a small shop. In its window there were toys, boxes and glass balls. Above the door were the words "Magic Shop." "Please, can we go in?" asked Gip. He pulled his father's hand. So they went in together.

The shop was small and dark. A tall, thin man stood behind a long table. He looked at Gip and smiled. "Is this a real magic shop?" asked Gip. "Of course," said the man. "We sell only real magic here." The man showed them his empty hand. Then he closed it and opened it again. Now there was a glass ball in it. He gave the ball to Gip. "From nothing!" said Gip with a big smile.

Then the man looked at the father. "There is something in your pocket, sir," he said. "No, there is nothing in it," said the father. The man put his hand into the father's pocket. He took out a small bird, and then another one. The two birds flew around the room and sang. Then, all at once, they were not there. Gip laughed, but the father did not like it at all.

Next, the man put a little box on the floor. "Watch," he said, and the box began to grow. It grew bigger and bigger. Soon it was as big as a door.

Then the man took down a box of toy soldiers. He said a magic word, and the little soldiers stood up. They walked across the table in a long line. "Oh, I want them!" said Gip. "Then they are yours," said the man. The man told Gip the magic word, and Gip said it back.

After that, Gip went to look at the big box again. He walked around it, and then the father could not see him. "Gip!" called the father, but there was no answer. The father began to worry, and then he felt real fear. "Where is my son?" he asked the man. The man did not answer, and he only smiled at the father.

The father ran to the door and pulled it open. All at once, he was outside on Regent Street. And there was Gip, next to him! Gip held some boxes in his arms, and he looked very happy. The father took Gip's hand and held it for a long time. Then he turned around to look at the shop. But the magic shop was not there. He saw only other shops, the same as every day.

At home, Gip opened the boxes on the table. In one box he found the toy soldiers. Gip said the magic word, and the soldiers stood up. They walked across the table, just like in the shop. The father watched them, and he did not know what to think. But Gip was home again, and he was happy. And the toys really were magic.

## Level B1

One afternoon, a father was walking along Regent Street in London with his little son, Gip. The street was full of people and noise, and Gip was holding his father's hand. Suddenly the boy stopped in front of a small shop that the father did not remember. Its window was full of toys, boxes and shining glass balls. Above the door, in old gold letters, were the words "Magic Shop." "Please, can we go in?" Gip asked, and he pulled hard at his father's hand. The father smiled and agreed, so they went in together.

Inside, the shop was small and rather dark. A tall, thin man was standing behind a long counter. He had very long fingers, and his eyes seemed to laugh at everything. "Is this a real magic shop?" Gip asked him. "Of course it is," the man answered. "We sell only real magic here, young sir." The man opened his empty hand, and suddenly there was a glass ball in it. He gave the ball to Gip, who held it like a treasure. "Out of nothing!" Gip said with a big smile.

Then the man, who was the shopkeeper, turned to the father. "There is something in your pocket, sir," he said. "No, there's nothing in it," the father replied. The shopkeeper reached into the father's coat pocket anyway. He took out a small bird, and then a second one. The two birds flew around the room, singing loudly. A moment later, they simply were not there anymore. Gip laughed and clapped, but the father did not like it very much. He did not enjoy having a stranger's hand in his pocket.

Next, the shopkeeper placed a little box on the floor. "Watch carefully," he said, and the box began to grow. It grew bigger and bigger until it was as big as a door. Gip stared at it with wide, happy eyes. The father, however, was starting to feel uncomfortable.

After that, the shopkeeper took down a box of toy soldiers from a high shelf. He set them on the counter and said a strange, short word. At once, the little soldiers stood up and began to march. They walked across the counter in a long, neat line. "Oh, I want them!" Gip said happily. "Then they are yours," said the shopkeeper. He bent down and told Gip the magic word, and Gip repeated it. "Say that word, and they will march for you," the shopkeeper told him.

While the two men were talking, Gip went to look at the big box again. He walked around it, and then the father could not see him. "Gip!" the father called, but there was no answer. He looked behind the box, but his son was not there. The father felt a cold wave of fear. "Where is my son?" he asked the shopkeeper. The shopkeeper did not answer, and he only smiled at the father.

The father ran to the door and pulled it open. All at once, he was standing outside on Regent Street. People were walking past him, just as they did on any other day. And there was Gip, right beside him! The boy was holding some boxes in his arms, and he looked very happy. The father took Gip's hand and held it for a long time. Then he turned around to look at the shop. But the magic shop was gone. In its place, he saw only ordinary shops that he knew well.

At home, Gip opened the boxes on the kitchen table. In one box he found the toy soldiers from the shop. Gip said the magic word, and the soldiers stood up at once. They marched across the table, just as they did in the shop. The father watched them and did not know what to think. But Gip was safe and happy, and that was the most important thing. And the toys, it seemed, really were magic.

## Level B2

One afternoon, a father was walking along Regent Street in London with his little son, Gip. He had noticed the magic shop once or twice before, though never, it seemed, in quite the same place. This time it was Gip who saw it first. The boy stopped short in front of its window and would not move another step. Behind the glass lay toys of every kind, painted boxes, and glass balls that caught the light. Above the door, in faded gold letters, were the words "Magic Shop." "Please, can we go in?" Gip begged, pulling at his father's hand with all his strength. The father had planned to walk on, but he could not refuse that eager face, so in they went.

The shop was small and dim, and it smelled faintly of dust and old paper. Glass cases ran along the walls, crowded with objects the father could not name. Some of them seemed to shift slightly whenever he was not looking straight at them. Behind a long counter stood a tall, thin man with remarkably long fingers. His eyes moved quickly from the father to the boy, and he seemed to find them both amusing. "Is this a real magic shop?" Gip asked him seriously. "Of course it is, young sir," the shopkeeper answered. "We sell nothing but genuine magic here." He held out an empty hand, closed it, and opened it again. A glass ball now rested in his palm, as if it had been there all along. He handed it to Gip, who turned it over and over in wonder. "Out of nothing!" the boy whispered.

Then the shopkeeper turned his attention to the father. "I believe you have something in your pocket, sir," he remarked. "I assure you there is nothing in it," the father replied, a little stiffly. The shopkeeper reached into the father's coat pocket all the same. Out came a small bird, and then a second one, both fluttering and alive. The two birds circled the room, singing loudly, and then they simply vanished. The father glanced at the ceiling and at the closed door, but there was no gap through which they could have escaped. Gip laughed and clapped his hands with delight, and he begged the shopkeeper to do it again. His father, however, did not care for the trick at all. It is not pleasant to have a stranger pull living creatures out of one's own coat.

Next, the shopkeeper set a tiny box on the floor. "Watch closely," he said, and before their eyes the box began to grow. It swelled steadily, bigger and bigger, until it was as large as a door. Gip gazed at it as though it were the most wonderful thing in the world. The father, meanwhile, was growing more and more uneasy, though he could not have said exactly why. Everything about the place seemed slightly wrong, like a room seen in a dream.

The shopkeeper now reached up to a high shelf and brought down a box of toy soldiers. He arranged them on the counter and spoke a single strange word. Instantly the little painted soldiers stood up straight and began to march. Their tiny feet tapped on the wood like rain on a window. They crossed the counter in a long, neat line, turning smartly at the edge. "Oh, I want them!" Gip cried, his face shining. "Then they are yours," said the shopkeeper with a small bow. He bent down and told Gip the magic word, and the boy repeated it carefully. "Say that word, and they will march for you whenever you wish," the shopkeeper explained.

While the two men were talking, Gip wandered off to look at the enormous box again. He walked around it, and when the father glanced over, the boy was no longer there. "Gip!" the father called, but no answer came. He hurried behind the box, but his son was nowhere to be seen. A cold wave of fear swept over him. "Where is my son?" he demanded, turning to the shopkeeper. The shopkeeper said nothing at all; he only smiled his strange, patient smile.

The father ran to the door and pulled it open. In that instant, he found himself standing outside on Regent Street in the afternoon light. People hurried past him, exactly as they did on any ordinary day. And there, right beside him, stood Gip. The boy was holding several boxes in his arms, and he looked perfectly happy and calm. The father had no idea how the boxes had got there, and Gip did not seem to know either. The father seized his son's hand and held on to it tightly. Then he turned around to look at the shop. He fully expected to see the dark window and the faded gold letters behind him. But the magic shop was gone. Where it should have been, there were only the ordinary shops he had passed a hundred times.

At home, Gip unpacked the boxes on the kitchen table. In one of them lay the toy soldiers from the shop. He spoke the magic word, and the soldiers rose at once and began their march. They crossed the table in their neat line, just as they had done on the counter. The father watched them for a long while, and he did not know what to think. Gip, for his part, seemed to find nothing surprising in any of it. To him, a magic shop was simply a place where magic was sold, and he had always known such places existed. Still, Gip was safe and happy, and in the end that was what mattered most. As for the toys, there was no doubt about them: they really were magic.

## Level C1

One afternoon, a father was strolling along Regent Street in London with his small son, Gip, the boy's hand tucked firmly into his own. He had noticed the magic shop once or twice before, though never, so far as he could recall, in quite the same spot, and he had always walked past it. This time, however, it was Gip who caught sight of it first, and Gip was not a boy to walk past anything. He stopped short in front of the window and became, in an instant, as immovable as a lamp post. Behind the glass lay toys of every description, painted boxes with brass corners, and glass balls that gathered the afternoon light and held it. Above the door, in gold letters so faded that they seemed half asleep, were the words "Magic Shop." "Please, can we go in?" Gip pleaded, hauling on his father's hand with the whole weight of his small body. The father had intended to walk on, but there are faces a parent cannot refuse, and this was one of them, so in they went.

The shop was narrow and dim, and it smelled of dust, old paper and something faintly sweet that the father could not place. Glass cases lined the walls, crowded with objects he could not name, and some of them seemed to shift a little whenever he was not looking directly at them. Behind a long counter stood a tall, thin man with remarkably long fingers and the air of someone who had been expecting them for years. His eyes travelled from the father to the boy and back again, and they appeared to find both of them quietly amusing. "Is this a real magic shop?" Gip asked, with the gravity that small boys reserve for truly important questions. "Naturally, young sir," the shopkeeper replied. "We sell nothing but genuine magic here; the other sort we leave to lesser establishments." He held out an empty hand, closed it slowly, and opened it again. A glass ball now rested in his palm, as calmly as if it had lived there all its life. He passed it across the counter to Gip, who turned it over and over in a kind of reverent silence. "Out of nothing!" the boy breathed at last.

It was then that the shopkeeper turned his attention to the father. "I rather think, sir, that you have something in your pocket," he observed pleasantly. "I assure you there is nothing in it at all," the father answered, with a stiffness he immediately regretted. The shopkeeper, untroubled, slipped his long fingers into the father's coat pocket all the same. Out came a small bird, then a second, both of them fluttering, warm and unmistakably alive. The pair circled the shop twice, singing as loudly as if it were spring, and then, between one note and the next, they were simply not there. The father glanced at the ceiling and at the closed door, but there was no gap through which a bird could have escaped. Gip laughed, clapped his hands and begged for the trick to be done again. His father, for his part, did not care for it in the least, for there is something deeply unsettling about a stranger producing living creatures from one's own coat.

Next, the shopkeeper set a tiny box upon the floor, no bigger than a matchbox. "Observe," he murmured, and before their eyes the box began to grow. It swelled steadily and without a sound, larger and larger, until it stood as high and wide as a door. Gip gazed at it as though it were the most wonderful object in all creation, which, at that moment, it very possibly was. The father, meanwhile, was growing steadily more uneasy, though he could not have explained exactly why. Everything about the place felt very slightly wrong, like a familiar room seen in a dream, where the furniture is all correct and nothing is quite right.

The shopkeeper now reached up to a high shelf and lifted down a box of toy soldiers, which he arranged along the counter with great care. Then he spoke a single word, short and strange, that neither of his visitors had ever heard before. At once the little painted soldiers straightened their backs and set off on a march, their tiny feet tapping on the wood like rain against a window. They crossed the counter in a long, neat column, wheeling smartly when they reached the edge. "Oh, I want them!" Gip cried, his whole face shining. "Then they are yours," said the shopkeeper, with a small and rather courtly bow. Bending down, he told Gip the magic word, and the boy repeated it twice, very carefully, so as not to forget it. "Say that word, and they will march for you whenever you wish," the shopkeeper explained, straightening up again.

While the two men were speaking, Gip drifted away to admire the enormous box once more. He wandered round to the far side of it, and when the father next glanced across the shop, the boy was no longer there. "Gip!" the father called, and then again, more sharply, but no answer came. He hurried round the box, but his son was nowhere to be seen, and the shop was suddenly very quiet. A cold wave of fear rose in him, quite unlike anything he had felt before. "Where is my son?" he demanded, swinging round to face the shopkeeper. The shopkeeper said nothing whatever; he merely smiled his strange, patient smile, as though he found the question a little foolish.

The father ran to the door and wrenched it open. In that same instant, he found himself standing outside on Regent Street, in the ordinary light of the ordinary afternoon. People hurried past him with their parcels and umbrellas, exactly as they did on any other day of the week. And there, right at his side, stood Gip, as calm and contented as a boy could be. He was holding several boxes in his arms, and how they had got there the father had no idea, nor, it seemed, did Gip. The father seized his son's hand and resolved to keep hold of it for a very long time. Only then did he turn to look back at the shop, fully expecting to see the dark window and the sleepy gold letters behind him. But the magic shop was gone. Where it should have stood there was only the familiar row of shops he had passed a hundred times, each of them dull, respectable and entirely without magic.

At home, Gip unpacked the boxes on the kitchen table with the solemn air of a man opening treasure chests. In one of them, packed neatly in their rows, lay the toy soldiers from the shop. He leaned over them and spoke the magic word, and at once they rose to their feet and began their march. They crossed the table in their neat column, just as they had crossed the counter, wheeling smartly at the edge. The father watched them for a long while and found that he did not know what to think. Gip, for his part, seemed to find nothing surprising in any of it. To him, a magic shop was simply a place where magic was sold, and he had always known that such places existed. His father was not so sure about the shop, or the shopkeeper, or what exactly had happened behind that fading door. Still, Gip was safe and happy, and in the end that was what mattered most. As for the toys, there could be no doubt about them at all: they really were magic.
