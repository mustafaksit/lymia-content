# Silas Marner

Summary: A lonely weaver loses his gold, but a little girl with golden hair changes his life.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): weaver, gold, loom

## Level A1

Silas Marner was a weaver. He lived in Lantern Yard. One day, some money went missing. "Silas took it," said his friend. It was not true. But the people believed the friend. So Silas left his home.

He went to a small town, Raveloe. There he worked at his loom all day. People paid him in gold. Every night he looked at his gold. The gold was his only friend.

One night, Silas went out. Then Dunstan Cass came to Silas's house. He was a bad young man. Dunstan found the gold and took it. No one saw Dunstan again.

Silas came home. His gold was not there! People looked, but no one found it. A kind woman, Dolly Winthrop, visited him.

It was the last night of the year. Silas stood at his open door. He stood very still. He did not see anything. A little girl came into the house. Then Silas saw gold near the fire! "My gold is back!" said Silas. But it was the little girl's hair. She was asleep by the fire.

No one came for her. Silas wanted help. He took her to a big house. There was a party there. Godfrey Cass saw the girl. She was his child! But Godfrey told no one.

"I want to keep her," said Silas. He gave her a name, Eppie. Dolly helped him with the child. Silas loved Eppie more than his gold. He talked to people again.

Sixteen years went by. Men took the water from an old pit. In the pit, they found Silas's gold! So Dunstan took it years ago. Dunstan was Godfrey's brother. Godfrey felt bad about his secret. He told his wife about Eppie.

Godfrey and his wife came to Silas. "Eppie," said Godfrey, "you are my child. Come and live with us." Eppie held Silas's hand and said no. "Silas is my father," said Eppie. Silas and Eppie stayed together.

## Level A2

Silas Marner was a weaver, and he lived in Lantern Yard. He went to a small church there, with his best friend. One day, some money from the church went missing. Then Silas's friend said, "Silas took it." This was not true, but the church people believed the friend. Silas wanted to marry a young woman, but she left him. So Silas left Lantern Yard.

He walked a long way to a village, Raveloe. There he worked at his loom all day. People paid him in gold. Silas kept the gold in two bags under the floor. Every night he took it out and looked at it. For fifteen years, the gold was his only friend.

One dark night, Silas went out for a short time. That night, Dunstan Cass came along the road. He was Godfrey Cass's younger brother, and he always wanted money. He saw a light in Silas's house and went in. No one was there, and soon Dunstan found the gold. He took it and walked away. After that night, no one in Raveloe ever saw Dunstan again.

When Silas came home, his gold was not there. The village people looked for it, but they did not find it. Dolly Winthrop, a kind woman from the village, often visited him.

On New Year's Eve, Silas stood at his open door. Sometimes he stood very still, like a man in a dream. Then he did not see or hear anything. At that moment, a little girl walked into his house. She was about two years old, and her hair was like gold. She sat down by the fire and went to sleep. Silas turned around and saw gold near the fire. "My gold is back!" said Silas. But it was the little girl's hair.

No one came for her. He took her to the Cass family's big house for help. There was a New Year party there. Godfrey Cass saw the little girl, and his face went white. She was his own child! Godfrey wanted to marry a young woman from a good family. He was afraid to lose her, so he said nothing.

"She came to me, so I want to keep her," said Silas. He gave her a name from his own family: Eppie. Silas loved Eppie much more than his gold. Godfrey married the young woman, but they had no children.

Sixteen years went by. Then Godfrey's men took the water out of an old pit. In the pit, they found Silas's gold! So Dunstan took the gold on that dark night. This news made Godfrey think about his own child. He told his wife everything about Eppie.

That evening, Godfrey and his wife came to Silas's house. "Eppie," said Godfrey, "you are my daughter. Come and live with us in our big house." "Thank you," said Eppie, "but I can't leave my father. He took care of me, and I love him." Godfrey and his wife went home without her. The next year, Eppie married Dolly's son. They all lived together in Silas's little house.

## Level B1

Silas Marner was a weaver who spent his days at a loom. When he was young, he lived in Lantern Yard, a street in a northern town. He belonged to a small church there, and he had a close friend. He was also planning to marry a young woman from the church.

Then one day, a bag of church money went missing. Silas's friend told everyone that Silas was the thief. Soon the empty bag turned up in Silas's room. Silas knew that he was innocent, but the church leaders did not believe him. They decided that he was guilty, and they sent him away. Soon after, the young woman married his friend instead. Silas left the town.

He walked for many days until he came to Raveloe, a quiet village. There he took a small stone cottage near an old pit full of water. He sat at his loom from morning until night and spoke to almost no one. The village people paid him well for his cloth, often in gold coins. Silas kept the coins in two leather bags under the floor. Every night he took them out and counted the gold.

One dark, wet night, Silas went out for a short time. He did not lock his door because he planned to come back quickly. That same night, Dunstan Cass was walking along the road in the rain. Dunstan was the younger son of the squire, the most important man in Raveloe. He was lazy and selfish, and he always needed money. He saw a light in the cottage window and went in. The room was empty, so he searched the floor and found the two bags. He carried them out into the dark. After that night, no one in Raveloe ever saw Dunstan again.

When Silas came back and saw the empty hiding place, he gave a terrible cry. He ran through the rain to the village and told everyone about the thief. The village people searched for the gold, but they found nothing. Dolly Winthrop, a kind and patient woman, often came with a plate of cakes.

On New Year's Eve, snow was falling over Raveloe. Since the robbery, Silas often stood at his open door and looked out. He was hoping that his gold might somehow come back to him. Sometimes Silas went completely still, like a man in a dream. Then he could not see or hear anything for several minutes. That night it happened again while he was standing at the door. At that moment, a little girl walked through the snow and into his house. She was about two years old, with soft golden curls. She sat down by the warm fire and fell asleep.

When Silas woke from his dream, he saw something shining near the fire. "My gold has come back!" Silas thought. He reached out, but instead of hard coins, he touched soft, warm hair. Silas looked for her family outside, but no one came looking for her.

Silas carried the child through the snow to the squire's big house. He needed help, and a New Year party was taking place there. Godfrey Cass, the squire's elder son, was dancing at the party. When he saw the little girl, his face went white. She was his own daughter, but no one in Raveloe knew this. Godfrey wanted to marry a young lady from a good family. He was afraid of losing her, so he kept his secret.

"She came to me, and I will keep her," said Silas. He named her Eppie, after his little sister. Dolly showed him how to wash, dress and feed a small child. He took her out into the fields, talked with his neighbors, and went to church. He loved Eppie far more than he ever loved his gold. Meanwhile, Godfrey married the young lady, but they had no children.

Sixteen years later, Godfrey's workers took the water out of the old pit. At the bottom, they found Silas's two leather bags of gold. Godfrey understood at once that Dunstan took the money on the night he disappeared. The news shook him, and he decided to stop hiding his own secret. That day he told his wife the truth about Eppie.

In the evening, Godfrey and his wife visited Silas's cottage. "Eppie," said Godfrey, "you are my daughter. Come and live with us, and you will be a fine lady." Silas went pale, but he said that Eppie must choose for herself. Eppie took Silas's hand and held it tightly. "Thank you, sir," said Eppie, "but I cannot leave my father. He loved me when I had no one, and my place is here." Godfrey and his wife went home in silence. The next year, Eppie married Dolly's son, and they all lived together in the cottage.

## Level B2

Silas Marner was a weaver, a pale, short-sighted man who spent long hours bent over his loom. As a young man he had lived in Lantern Yard, a narrow street in a smoky northern town. He belonged to a small, strict church there, and he was engaged to a young woman. His closest friend was a member of the same church.

Everything changed when a bag of church money disappeared from the room of a sick old man. Silas's friend said that he suspected Silas, and the empty bag was later found in Silas's own home. Silas swore that he was innocent, but the church leaders decided to let God settle the question by drawing lots. The lots declared him guilty, and he was sent away in disgrace. A few weeks later, the young woman married the friend who had accused him. Silas lost his faith in God and in people, and he left the town.

He finally settled in Raveloe, a peaceful village hidden among woods and fields, far from any main road. He rented a small stone cottage beside an old pit full of dark water, and he lived there completely alone. The villagers found him strange, but they needed his cloth, and they paid him well for it, often in gold. Silas kept his coins in two leather bags under the loose bricks of the floor near his loom. Each night he spread them out on the table, counted them, and enjoyed their bright shine. Over fifteen years, the gold slowly became the only thing he cared about.

One dark, wet night, Silas went out for a short time to fetch something for his work, leaving the door unlocked. At that same moment, Dunstan Cass was making his way home along the muddy road. Dunstan, the younger son of the squire, was idle, selfish and always short of money. Like everyone in the village, he had heard rumors about the weaver's gold. Seeing a light in the cottage, he pushed the door open and found the room empty. It did not take him long to discover the loose bricks in the floor and the two heavy bags beneath them. He carried them out into the rain, and after that night nobody in Raveloe ever saw him again.

When Silas returned and found the hiding place empty, he could hardly breathe. He hurried through the rain to the village and told everyone that he had been robbed. The villagers had always kept their distance, but they were moved by his despair, and they searched for the thief without success. The kindest of them was Dolly Winthrop, a patient, motherly woman who began to visit him with little cakes. Once she came with her small son, and the boy sang a Christmas song for the lonely weaver. She gently encouraged Silas to go to church, and although he did not go, he was grateful for her company.

On New Year's Eve, snow lay thick over Raveloe. Since the robbery, Silas had developed a habit of opening his door and staring out into the darkness. It was as though he hoped his money might somehow return by itself. That night, while he stood in the doorway, one of his strange fits came over him. Since his youth, he had sometimes frozen like this for minutes, unable to see or hear anything. In those minutes, a little girl of about two wandered out of the snowy darkness and walked past him. She sat down by the warm fire, and soon she was fast asleep.

When Silas came to himself, he closed the door and turned toward the fireplace. His weak eyes caught a glimpse of something golden on the floor. "My gold has come back!" Silas thought, and his heart began to race. He reached out, but instead of cold, hard coins, his fingers met soft, warm curls. The child opened her eyes, looked up at him, and smiled. Silas searched the lane outside for her family, but nobody came looking for her.

Not knowing what to do, Silas carried the child to the squire's house, where a New Year party was in full swing. Among the dancers was Godfrey Cass, the squire's elder son. The moment Godfrey saw the little girl, he turned pale, because she was his own daughter. He had hidden this secret from his father and from everyone else in Raveloe. He loved a young lady from a respectable family, and he feared the truth would cost him both her and his inheritance. So, although his conscience troubled him, he kept silent and let Silas take the child home. Later, he sent small gifts to the cottage, but he never explained why.

"She came to me, and I mean to keep her," Silas told the villagers. He named her Eppie, after his mother and his little sister. Dolly taught him how to feed, wash and dress a small child, and she laughed kindly at his mistakes. As Eppie grew, the weaver's narrow life opened up like a window. He took her out into the fields to pick flowers, he chatted with his neighbors, and he even began going to church. The gold had shut him away from the world, but the child led him back into it. Meanwhile, Godfrey married the young lady he loved, yet to their great sorrow they had no children of their own.

Sixteen years passed, and Eppie became a cheerful, warm-hearted young woman. Then Godfrey, who now owned the land around the cottage, had the old pit drained. At the bottom, the workmen discovered Silas's two leather bags, still full of gold. Godfrey realized immediately that Dunstan had taken the money on the night he vanished. The discovery shook him deeply, and he decided that every secret comes to light in the end. That afternoon, he finally told his wife the truth about Eppie.

In the evening, Godfrey and his wife came to Silas's cottage. "Eppie," said Godfrey, "you are my daughter, and I want to take care of you now. Come and live with us, and you will have fine clothes, a big house and everything a lady could wish for." Silas turned pale, but he said quietly that Eppie must choose for herself. Eppie took Silas's hand and held it tightly. "Thank you, sir," said Eppie, "but I can't leave my father. He took me in when I had nobody, and he has loved me every day since then." Godfrey and his wife went home in silence, understanding that they had come too late. The next year, Eppie married Dolly's son, and the young couple made their home with Silas in the little stone cottage.

## Level C1

In the years before he came to Raveloe, Silas Marner had lived in Lantern Yard, a cramped street in a smoky manufacturing town in the north. There he belonged to a small, earnest chapel whose members watched over one another's souls with anxious care. He was a weaver by trade, pale and short-sighted, and his neighbors thought of him as a quiet young man of unusual faith. He had a friend whom he trusted completely, and he was engaged to a young woman with whom he expected to share a modest, contented life.

That life collapsed in a single week. A bag of chapel money vanished from the sickroom of an old deacon whom Silas had nursed at night, and his trusted friend began to hint at suspicions. When the empty bag turned up in Silas's own lodgings, the congregation did not call in a magistrate; instead they prayed, drew lots, and let the lots speak for God. The lots pronounced him guilty. Silas, who knew himself innocent, concluded that there was no just God in heaven, and within a month his promised bride had married the very friend who had accused him. He left the town with his loom on his back and his heart closed against the world.

Raveloe, where he finally settled, was a village of comfortable farms and deep lanes, tucked away so far from the main road that strangers were met with suspicion. Silas took a stone cottage beside an abandoned pit full of dark water, and there, for fifteen years, he wove from dawn until dusk, speaking to his customers only about their orders. The villagers whispered that there was something uncanny about the pale weaver, yet they paid him well, and much of their payment came in gold. Silas hid the coins in two leather bags beneath loose bricks near his loom. Every night, with the shutters closed, he poured them into shining heaps and counted them with a strange tenderness. Having lost everything else, he gave his love to the only thing that remained, and the gold, bright and silent, seemed almost to return it.

One dark, wet night, Silas slipped out briefly to fetch something for his work, leaving the door unlocked, sure that no one would venture down the lane in such weather. He had no idea that Dunstan Cass was making his way back to the village on foot through the mud. Dunstan, the younger son of the squire, was a lazy, sneering young man who lived on his family's money and on whatever he could squeeze from his elder brother, Godfrey. He was always desperate for cash, and like everyone else in Raveloe he had heard rumors about the weaver's hoard. Seeing the glow of the fire through the window, he pushed open the door, found the room deserted, and soon discovered the loose bricks and the two heavy bags beneath them. He stepped back out into the rain with the gold in his arms, and from that night on, no one in Raveloe ever set eyes on him again.

When Silas came home and found the hollow beneath the bricks empty, he let out a cry that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his body. He stumbled through the rain to the village, and the villagers, who had never thought of the weaver as a man with feelings, were startled by his grief. They searched, argued, and suspected a passing peddler, but the gold had disappeared as completely as if the earth had swallowed it. Yet the loss did what fifteen years of weaving had not: it opened Silas's door to his neighbors. Chief among them was Dolly Winthrop, a patient, motherly woman who arrived one Sunday with her little son and a plate of cakes stamped with letters she could not read. While the boy sang a Christmas carol in his clear, small voice, Dolly urged Silas to come to church, and something in him began, very faintly, to thaw.

New Year's Eve came in with heavy snow. Since the robbery, Silas had fallen into the habit of opening his door at night and gazing out into the darkness, as though the lost gold might somehow find its way home. That night, as he stood in the doorway, one of the strange fits that had troubled him since youth came over him, and he froze, blind and deaf to the world. In those empty minutes, a little girl of about two came wandering out of the snowy darkness, drawn by the light of the fire, and toddled straight past him into the cottage. She settled on the warm hearth, held out her small hands to the flames, and before long was sound asleep.

When the fit passed, Silas shut the door without noticing anything and turned back toward the fire. His weak eyes caught a gleam of gold on the floor before the hearth, and his heart leapt with a wild, impossible hope that his treasure had returned. He stooped and reached out, but instead of the hard, cold edges of coins, his fingers sank into soft, warm curls. The child stirred, opened her eyes, and looked up at the bent old weaver without the slightest fear. Silas went out into the lane and called into the night, but no one answered, and no one came looking for her.

Bewildered, and wanting help, he wrapped the child in his coat and carried her through the snow to the squire's house, where a New Year's dance was at its height. Among the dancers stood Godfrey Cass, the squire's elder son, flushed and happy beside the young lady he hoped to marry. At the sight of the golden-haired child in the weaver's arms, Godfrey turned the color of ashes, for the little girl was his own daughter. To claim her would mean confessing a secret he had hidden from his father and all Raveloe, and losing both the young lady and his inheritance. So he swallowed the truth and said nothing, while Silas, unaware of the storm he had stirred, declared that he would keep the child himself. In the months that followed, Godfrey quietly sent money and small comforts to the cottage, but he never once explained why.

Silas named her Eppie, after his mother and little sister, and with Dolly's help he learned, clumsily but devotedly, how to care for a small child. Eppie proved a lively, determined little person; once she slipped away while he was weaving, and he found her sitting happily in a muddy ditch, far too pleased to feel ashamed. Where the gold had demanded that he shut himself away, the child demanded the opposite, pulling him out into the sunlight, into conversations with neighbors, and at last through the church door. Meanwhile, Godfrey married the young lady he loved, but the years brought them no children of their own, and his hidden debt to the weaver's cottage grew heavier with every season.

Sixteen years passed, and Eppie grew into a warm, quick-witted young woman whom half the village admired. Then Godfrey, who now owned the land around the cottage, ordered the old pit to be drained. When the water sank away, the workmen found Silas's two leather bags lying in the mud at the bottom, the gold still inside them. Godfrey understood at once that Dunstan had taken the money on the night he vanished, and the discovery struck him like a judgment. If his brother's wicked deed could rise out of the mud after sixteen years, he reasoned, then his own secret could not stay buried forever. That same afternoon he sat down with his wife and, with great difficulty, told her everything about Eppie.

In the evening, Godfrey and his wife walked down to the cottage, confident that the girl would welcome the chance of a better life. "Eppie," said Godfrey, "you are my daughter, and it is time I did my duty by you. Come and live with us, and you will be a lady, with fine clothes, a carriage, and everything that money can buy." Silas trembled, but he forced himself to say that Eppie must choose for herself, and that he would not stand in her way. Eppie crossed the room, took the old weaver's hand, and held it tightly. "Thank you, sir," said Eppie, "but I can't leave my father, and I have no wish to be a lady. He took me in when there was no one else, he has loved me every day since then, and I have never known any other father."

Godfrey walked home beside his wife in heavy silence, knowing that he had come sixteen years too late. The following year, Eppie married Dolly's son, and the young couple settled in the stone cottage with Silas, where Godfrey paid for a new garden as a last kindness. Silas never again counted coins by the firelight, for his treasure, as he often told Dolly, now walked and talked and called him father.
