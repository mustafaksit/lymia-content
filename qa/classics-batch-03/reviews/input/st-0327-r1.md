# William Tell

Summary: A quiet mountain hunter refuses to bow to a proud governor's hat and faces a hard test.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): apple, hat, crossbow

## Level A1

William Tell lived in Switzerland. He had a wife and a little son. The child's name was Walter. Tell had a crossbow. No one could shoot like him.

A bad man named Gessler ruled the land. The people did not like him. One morning, Gessler came to Altdorf. He put his hat up in the town. Then he called all the people. "Bow to my hat!" said Gessler. Two soldiers stood near the hat all day.

Tell came to the town with Walter. They walked past the hat. Tell did not bow. "It is only a hat," said Tell. The two men took him to Gessler.

Gessler looked at Tell's crossbow. Then he pointed at Walter. "Shoot an apple off his head!" said Gessler. "No, I cannot!" said Tell. "You must do it," said Gessler.

Walter stood very still. The apple was on his head. "I am not afraid, Father," said Walter. Tell took out two arrows. He looked hard at the apple. Then he shot. The arrow cut the apple in two! Walter ran to his father. All the people called out his name.

But Gessler saw the second arrow. "Why do you have two?" he asked. "The second one was for you," said Tell. Gessler did not like that. "Take him away!" he said to his men. They put Tell in a boat.

Gessler got in the boat too. They went out on a big lake. Then a bad storm came. The water went up and down. "Only Tell can help us!" said one man. So Gessler let Tell help. Tell took the boat near the land. Then he got out and ran far away.

Soon all the people stood together. Gessler left the country and never came back. The people of Switzerland were free at last. Tell went home to his family.

## Level A2

Long ago, William Tell lived in the high country of Switzerland. He had a small house, a wife and a young son, Walter. Tell was a strong man, and he did not talk much. With his crossbow, he could hit anything. People said he was the best shot in the country.

At that time, a hard man named Gessler ruled the land. He took money and food from the people, so they were poor. Gessler wanted everyone to fear him. One day, he put his hat on a long pole in Altdorf. "Every person must bow to this hat," he told the people. "When you bow to my hat, you bow to me." Two soldiers stayed there to watch.

A few days later, Tell walked into the town with Walter. They passed the hat, but Tell did not stop. He did not even look up at it. The two soldiers took hold of him at once. Many people came to see what was wrong.

Just then, Gessler arrived. He knew Tell and his crossbow. "So you are the great shot," Gessler said with a cold smile. "Put an apple on your son's head and shoot it off. Hit the apple, and you can go free." Tell went white. "Please, ask anything else of me," he said. "Do it, or you both go to prison," said Gessler.

Walter stood very still with the apple on his head. "Do not worry, Father," said Walter. "I know you can do it." Tell took two arrows and put one in his crossbow. For a long moment, nobody made a sound. Then Tell let the arrow go. It went right through the apple, and the apple fell in two. Walter ran to his father, and the people called out his name.

But Gessler saw the second arrow. "Why did you take two?" he asked. Tell looked him in the eye. "The first was for the apple," he said. "The second was for you." Gessler's face went dark. "Take him away," he told his men.

They put Tell in a boat on the lake of Lucerne. Gessler and his men got in too. Out on the water, a great storm began. The wind was very strong, and the water rose high. Gessler's men could not control the boat. "We are lost!" one of them said. "Only Tell knows this water well enough."

So Gessler told his men to free Tell's hands. Tell took the boat close to the land. Suddenly, he picked up his crossbow and jumped out. With one foot, he pushed the boat back into the water. Then he ran up the side of the mountain and was gone.

The news went from village to village. Soon the people of Switzerland stood together against Gessler. He had to run from the country, and he never came back. At last the people were free. Tell went home, and his family was together again.

## Level B1

Long ago, in the mountains of Switzerland, there lived a hunter called William Tell. He had a wife, a small house and a young son named Walter. Tell was a quiet man who preferred the forest to the town. But everyone knew his name, because nobody could shoot a crossbow like him.

In those days, a governor called Gessler ruled the land. He was a proud and cruel man who served a foreign emperor. He took the farmers' money and laughed at their complaints. The people hated him, but they were afraid to say so.

One spring, Gessler thought of a new way to show his power. In the main square of Altdorf, he put his hat on top of a tall pole. "From today, everyone who passes this hat must bow to it," he announced. "Bowing to my hat is the same as bowing to me." He ordered two soldiers to guard the pole day and night. Most people took a longer way round, so they did not have to pass it.

A few days later, Tell came down from the mountains to visit Altdorf. Walter was with him, talking happily and holding his father's hand. They crossed the square, and Tell walked straight past the pole. He did not look up, and he did not bow. At once the two soldiers took hold of him. "You have broken the governor's law!" one of them shouted. A crowd quickly gathered around them.

At that moment, Gessler rode into the square with his men. He listened to the soldiers and then turned to Tell with a cold smile. "They say you are the finest shot in the land," he said. "Then show me. Your son will stand under that tree with an apple on his head. Shoot the apple from a hundred steps away. Hit it, and you are free. Refuse, and you will both go to prison."

Tell's face went white. "Ask me for my life, but not this," he said. Some people in the crowd also asked Gessler to show mercy, but he only laughed. Then Walter spoke in a clear voice. "I am not afraid, Father. I will stand still. I know you will not miss."

Walter stood under the tree, and a soldier placed the apple on his head. Tell took two arrows from his belt. He hid one under his coat and put the other in his crossbow. The whole square was silent. Tell raised the crossbow, aimed for a long moment and let the arrow fly. It went straight through the apple and carried it to the ground. Walter was safe! He ran to his father, and the crowd cheered.

Gessler, however, had noticed the second arrow. "Why did you hide another arrow?" he asked. Tell was silent at first, but the governor promised to spare his life. "Then I will tell you the truth," Tell said. "The second arrow was for you." Gessler's face grew dark. "I promised you your life, and I will keep that promise," he said. "But you will spend it in my deepest prison, far from the sun."

The soldiers tied Tell's hands and took him to a boat on the lake of Lucerne. Gessler wanted to carry him across the water to his castle. While they were crossing, a sudden storm came down from the mountains. Huge waves crashed against the boat, and the boatmen lost control. "Only Tell can steer us through this," one of them cried. Gessler had no choice, so he ordered his men to free the prisoner.

Tell took control and guided the boat towards the shore. When they came close to a flat rock, he picked up his crossbow and jumped. As he landed, he pushed the boat back into the waves with his foot. Then he climbed up into the mountains and disappeared among the trees.

News of Tell's courage spread from village to village. The people of Switzerland found new hope, and they stood together against Gessler. In the end, the governor had to flee the country, and he never returned. The people were free at last, and Tell went home to his wife and son.

## Level B2

Long ago, high in the mountains of Switzerland, there lived a hunter named William Tell. His home was a simple wooden house beside a mountain stream, which he shared with his wife and their young son, Walter. Tell was a man of few words who felt more at ease among the rocks and pine trees than in busy towns. Yet his name was known in every valley, for no one could match his skill with a crossbow.

At that time, the Swiss lands were governed in the name of a powerful foreign emperor. His representative was a governor called Gessler, a proud man who treated the farmers as if they were his servants. He raised taxes, built new castles and punished anyone who dared to complain. The people suffered in silence, although many of them had begun to talk quietly about freedom. Men from the different valleys had even met in secret and promised to stand together when the moment came.

One spring, Gessler decided to test how far the people would obey him. In the market square of Altdorf, he had a tall pole set up and placed his own hat on top of it. Then he announced a new law: everyone who passed the hat had to bow to it, as if to the governor himself. Two soldiers were ordered to stand beside the pole and arrest anyone who refused. Most people simply avoided the square, choosing longer paths through the narrow lanes behind it.

Tell, who rarely came down from the mountains, knew nothing about the new law. A few days later, he walked into Altdorf with Walter, who was delighted to be in town. They crossed the square, and Tell passed the pole without even glancing at the hat. Immediately the two soldiers stepped forward and took hold of him. "You failed to bow to the governor's hat," one of them said. "In the name of the law, you are under arrest." Tell tried calmly to explain, but the soldiers would not listen, and soon a worried crowd had gathered around them.

At that very moment, Gessler rode into the square, returning from a hunt with his followers. When he heard what had happened, a thin smile spread across his face. "So this is the famous William Tell," he said. "I am told you can hit an apple on a tree from a hundred paces. Very well, let us see whether the stories are true. Your son will stand under that tree with an apple on his head, and you will shoot it off. If you succeed, you will go free. If you refuse, both of you will be thrown into prison."

Tell could hardly believe his ears. "My lord, ask for anything else," he said in a low voice. "I will give you my own life, but do not make me aim at my child." A young nobleman in Gessler's company also protested, and several people in the crowd begged the governor to change his mind. Gessler, however, only looked amused, as if he were watching a play put on for his pleasure.

It was Walter who broke the tense silence. "There's nothing to fear, Father," he said, lifting his chin. "I'll stand as still as a stone, and I won't even close my eyes. I know you won't miss." When a soldier offered to cover his eyes, the boy shook his head and walked to the tree alone.

The apple was placed on Walter's head, and the crowd stepped back. Tell drew two arrows from his belt. Without a word, he slipped one inside his jacket and fitted the other to his crossbow. For a long moment he did not move at all, and the whole square seemed to hold its breath. Then he raised the weapon, aimed and released the arrow. It flew straight and true, split the apple and carried the pieces into the grass behind the tree. Walter was completely unharmed. He ran laughing into his father's arms, and a great cheer rose from the crowd.

Yet Gessler had seen the second arrow. "Tell me, why did you take two arrows?" he asked. Tell hesitated, so the governor gave his word that the hunter's life would be spared, whatever his answer. "Then I will be honest," said Tell. "Had the first arrow harmed my son, the second was meant for you." The crowd fell silent, and Gessler's face darkened. "I promised you your life, and you shall keep it," he said coldly. "But you will spend the rest of it in a prison where you will never see the sun or the moon again."

Tell's hands were tied, and he was led down to a boat waiting on the lake of Lucerne. Gessler intended to take him across the water to his own castle, where no friend could reach him. They had not gone far, however, when a violent storm swept down from the mountains. The sky turned black, enormous waves rose over the sides, and the boatmen could no longer control the boat. "There is only one man here who knows this lake well enough to save us," one of them called out. Gessler had no choice, and he ordered the soldiers to free Tell's hands.

Tell took control and guided the boat with great skill through the waves towards the rocky shore. As they drew near a flat ledge of rock, he seized his crossbow and leaped from the boat. With one strong push of his foot, he sent the boat spinning back into the storm. Before anyone could stop him, he had climbed up through the trees and vanished into the mountains he knew so well.

The story of the apple and the escape travelled quickly from valley to valley. It gave the people the courage they had been lacking, and they rose together against the governor and his soldiers. In the end, Gessler was forced to flee the country, and he never returned. The people of Switzerland were free at last, and William Tell went home to his wife and son.

## Level C1

High among the mountains of Switzerland, where the snow lingers on the peaks well into summer, there once lived a hunter by the name of William Tell. His house was a modest wooden one beside a rushing stream, and he shared it with his wife and their young son, Walter, a bright and fearless boy. Tell was not a man for speeches or crowds; he was happiest on the steep paths above the tree line, where a single misstep could cost a life. Even so, his name was spoken with respect in every valley, for no marksman in the land could rival him with a crossbow.

Those were hard years for the people of the valleys. The lands around the lake of Lucerne were ruled in the name of a distant emperor, and his authority rested in the hands of a governor called Gessler. Proud, suspicious and quick to anger, Gessler treated the farmers as though they owed him not only their taxes but their obedience in every small thing. He built new strongholds on the hillsides, raised the dues on cattle and grain, and answered every complaint with a fine or a spell in his cells.

Fear kept most people silent, but silence is not the same as consent. In kitchens and barns, and in the fields after dark, men from the different valleys had begun to meet in secret. They spoke of old rights that had been taken from them, and at last they swore to stand by one another when the hour came. Tell knew of these meetings, yet he kept apart from them, believing that a quiet man served his family best by staying out of trouble.

Gessler, sensing the mood of the people without understanding it, resolved to find out exactly how far their obedience would go. In the market square of Altdorf, he had a tall pole set up, and on its tip he placed his own hat, trimmed with fine feathers. A herald then read out the new law: anyone who passed the hat was to bow before it, as humbly as before the governor in person. Two soldiers were posted beside the pole from dawn till dusk, with orders to arrest anyone who failed to show the proper respect. The townspeople, unwilling either to obey or to be punished, quietly took the long way round through the muddy lanes behind the church.

Into this square, a few days later, came Tell, with Walter skipping at his side. Having spent the week in the high pastures, he had heard nothing of the governor's order, and Walter was far too busy staring at the stalls to notice the pole. Tell strode past the hat without so much as raising his eyes. At once the soldiers blocked his way and seized him by the arms, declaring that he had insulted the governor and must answer for it. Tell explained, patiently at first and then with growing irritation, that he had meant no insult to anyone, but the men had their orders, and a murmuring crowd soon pressed around them.

At that moment there was a clatter of hooves, and Gessler himself rode into the square, returning from a hunt with a party of nobles and servants. He listened to the soldiers' account, and a slow, thin smile appeared on his face as he saw who their prisoner was. "So this is the famous Tell," he said, "the hunter who never misses. Your son tells me that you can strike an apple from a tree at a hundred paces. Let us put the boast to the test. The boy will stand beneath that linden tree with an apple on his head, and you will shoot it off. Succeed, and you walk away a free man; refuse, and you will both go to my prison."

For a moment Tell seemed not to understand what had been asked of him, and then he went pale. "My lord, take my life if you must, but do not ask a father to aim at his own child," he said hoarsely. A young nobleman in the governor's party stepped forward to protest, and voices in the crowd joined his, pleading for mercy. Gessler merely leaned back in his saddle, as if the whole scene had been arranged for his amusement, and repeated that the choice was Tell's to make.

In the end it was Walter who settled the matter. "Don't worry, Father," he said, with a steadiness that silenced those around him. "I'll stand as still as a rock, and I won't even shut my eyes. You've never missed, so why would you miss today?" When a soldier came forward with a cloth to cover his eyes, the boy pushed it away, walked to the tree on his own and turned to face his father.

The apple was set upon the boy's head, and the crowd drew back to leave a long, empty lane between father and son. Tell took two arrows from his belt, slipped one quietly inside his jacket and fitted the other to his crossbow. For what seemed an age he stood without moving, while the square held its breath and even the birds in the linden tree fell silent. Then, in one smooth motion, he raised the crossbow, sighted along it and loosed the arrow. It flew true, split the apple cleanly in two and carried both halves into the grass behind the tree, leaving the boy without a scratch. Walter ran, laughing, into his father's arms, and a roar of joy went up from every side of the square.

Gessler, however, had not taken his eyes off the hunter for an instant, and he had seen the second arrow vanish beneath the jacket. "A fine shot," he said, "but tell me why you needed a second arrow." Tell murmured something about the habits of hunters, and the governor, scenting a better answer, swore that the man's life was safe whatever he might say. "Then hear the truth," said Tell, lifting his head. "Had my first arrow harmed my son, the second would have been meant for you, and I would not have missed."

A hush fell over the square. Gessler's smile vanished, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. "I promised you your life, and you shall have it," he said at last, very softly. "But you will spend it in a cell so deep that neither sun nor moon will ever find you." Tell's hands were bound, and he was marched down to the lakeshore, where a boat stood ready to carry him to the governor's castle on the far side of the water.

They had not gone far when the wind began to change. Without warning, a storm came howling down from the passes, turning the calm lake into a field of white, heaving water and driving the boat towards the cliffs. The boatmen, pale with fear, could do nothing with the oars, and at last one of them cried out that only Tell knew the lake well enough to bring them through. Gessler, who valued his own safety above his pride, ordered the prisoner's ropes to be cut.

With his hands free, Tell took the steering oar and, calmly reading the waves, guided the boat along the foot of the cliffs. He knew exactly what he was looking for: a flat shelf of rock that ran out a little way into the water. As the boat swung close to it, he snatched up his crossbow, sprang onto the stone and, with one powerful thrust of his foot, sent the boat spinning back into the storm. By the time the boatmen had regained control, he had climbed through the pines and disappeared into the mountains that had always been his home.

The tale of the apple and the leap from the boat spread from valley to valley faster than any messenger could ride. What secret oaths had begun, Tell's defiance completed: the people rose together, and one by one the governor's strongholds fell into their hands. Gessler, his authority gone and his soldiers scattered, fled the country and was never seen in Switzerland again. The valleys were free at last, and William Tell went home to his wife and his son, whose apple had become the most famous in the land.
