# Diamonds and Toads

Summary: A kind girl gives water to a poor woman at a fountain and receives a strange gift.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): diamonds, toads, fountain, pitcher

## Level A1

A mother lived with her two girls. The older girl was like her mother. They were not kind. The younger girl was like her father. She was kind, good and pretty. But the mother loved only the older girl.

The younger girl did all the work. Two times a day, she got water. She walked far to a fountain. She brought the water home in a pitcher.

One day, a poor woman came there. "Can I have some water?" asked the woman. "Yes, of course," said the girl. She held up the pitcher for her.

But the woman was really a fairy. The fairy spoke to the girl. "You are very kind. Each word brings a flower or a diamond."

The girl came home late. "Why are you late?" asked her mother. "I am sorry, Mother," said the girl. Flowers and diamonds fell from her mouth! She told her mother about the woman.

The mother wanted diamonds too. So she sent the older girl there. "I don't want to," said the older girl. But her mother made her go.

There, a lady came out of the forest. "Can I have some water?" she asked. "Get it yourself!" said the older girl. But the lady was the same fairy. The fairy looked at her. "You are not kind. Each word brings a snake or a toad."

The older girl went home. "Well?" asked her mother. "Well, Mother?" said the older girl. Out came two snakes and two toads! "Your sister did this!" said the mother. The mother ran at the younger girl. So the younger girl ran into the forest.

A prince came through the forest. "Why are you here?" he asked the girl. "My mother sent me away," she said. Diamonds fell from her mouth again. She told the prince her story. He loved her, and he married her.

The older girl was not kind to anyone. Her own mother did not want her. So she went far away from home.

## Level A2

A woman lived with her two daughters. The older daughter looked and acted just like her mother. They both thought only of themselves. No one could live with them. The younger daughter was like her father. She was kind and good, and she was very beautiful. The mother loved her older daughter very much. But she did not love the younger one at all.

The younger girl had to do all the hard work. Two times a day, the girl had to get water. She carried a big pitcher to a far fountain and back.

One day, a poor woman came up to her at the fountain. "Please, can I have a drink?" asked the woman. "Of course," said the girl. She cleaned the pitcher and filled it with the clearest water. She held it up, so the woman could drink easily.

This poor woman was really a fairy. She wanted to see how kind the girl was. The fairy smiled at her. "You are beautiful, kind and good. So I give you a present. Every time you speak, a flower or a diamond comes out."

The girl came home a little late. "Why are you so late?" asked her mother. "I am sorry I am late, Mother," said the girl. Two flowers, two pearls and two big diamonds fell from her mouth. "What do I see? How did this happen, my daughter?" said the mother. The girl told her the whole story.

The mother called her older daughter. "Go to the fountain. Be kind to the poor woman there," said the mother. "I don't want to walk all that way!" said the older daughter. "You must go, and you must go now!" said the mother. So the older daughter went. She took the best silver bottle in the house with her.

There, a lady in beautiful clothes came out of the forest. It was the same fairy. "Can I have a drink, please?" asked the lady. "Did I walk all this way to give you water? Get your own water from the fountain!" said the older daughter. The fairy looked at the girl. "You are not kind at all. So every time you speak, a snake or a toad comes out."

The older daughter walked home. "Well, my daughter?" asked the mother. "Well, Mother?" said the older daughter. Two snakes and two toads jumped out of her mouth. "This is your sister's fault!" said the mother. The mother ran at the younger daughter. The poor girl ran away into the forest.

The king's son came through the forest. He saw the beautiful girl all alone. "Why are you here alone?" asked the king's son. "My mother sent me away from home," said the girl. Five or six pearls and as many diamonds fell from her mouth. So she told him her whole story. The king's son fell in love with her. To him, her words were worth more than any money. He took her to his father, the king, and married her.

The older sister was not kind to anyone. Even her own mother did not want her. So she left and went far away from home.

## Level B1

Long ago, a widow lived with her two daughters. The older daughter was just like her mother, both in her looks and in her manners. Both of them were proud and unpleasant, and nobody could bear to live with them. The younger daughter took after her father. She was gentle and polite, and one of the most beautiful girls in the land. People often love those who are like them, so the mother adored her older daughter. She could not stand the younger one.

She made the younger girl eat in the kitchen and work from morning to night. Among her many jobs, the poor girl had to fetch water twice a day. The fountain was a long walk from the house. Each time, she carried back a large pitcher full of water.

One day, while she was standing at the fountain, a poor woman came up to her. "Could I have a drink, please?" asked the woman. "Of course, my good woman," said the girl kindly. She rinsed the pitcher and filled it from the clearest part of the fountain. Then she held it up the whole time, so the woman could drink easily.

But this was no ordinary woman. She was a fairy who was pretending to be a poor village woman. She wanted to see how kind the girl really was. The fairy smiled at her. "You are so beautiful, so good and so polite. For this, I will give you a gift. With every word you speak, a flower or a jewel will fall from your mouth."

At home, her mother scolded her for coming back so late. "I am sorry I took so long, Mother," said the girl. As she spoke, two roses, two pearls and two large diamonds fell from her mouth. "What is this? Pearls and diamonds are falling out of her mouth! How did this happen, my daughter?" cried the mother. The girl told her the whole story, and many diamonds tumbled out as she spoke.

"I really must send my older daughter there," said the mother. She called the older girl over. "Look at what comes out of your sister's mouth when she speaks! Don't you want the same gift? Go to the fountain and give a drink to the poor woman who asks for one." "Me, carry water from the fountain? Never!" said the older girl rudely. "You will go," said the mother, "and you will go this minute!" So the older girl went, and she grumbled all the way. She took with her the finest silver flask in the house.

At the fountain, a lady in splendid clothes came out of the forest. It was the same fairy, but this time she looked like a princess. She wanted to see how rude the older girl could be. The lady came over and asked for a drink. "Do you think I came here to give you water? Of course, I brought this silver flask just for you! Go and drink from the fountain yourself!" said the older girl. The fairy did not lose her temper, but she spoke firmly. "You are not very polite. So with every word you speak, a snake or a toad will fall from your mouth."

As soon as her mother saw her coming, she called out, "Well, my daughter?" "Well, Mother?" replied the rude girl. Two snakes and two toads jumped out of her mouth. "Oh, no! What do I see? This is all her sister's fault, and she will pay for it!" cried the mother. In a rage, she ran toward the younger daughter. The poor girl ran away into the nearby forest.

The king's son was riding home through that forest. He found the beautiful girl there, alone and in tears. "Why are you here all alone, and why are you crying?" asked the prince. "Oh, sir, my mother sent me away from home," she answered. While she spoke, five or six pearls and as many diamonds fell from her mouth. He asked her where they came from, and she told him her whole story.

The prince fell in love with her. He decided that such a gift was worth more than any fortune. So he took her to the palace of the king, his father, and married her.

The older sister, meanwhile, made herself so hated that even her own mother drove her away. She went from door to door, but no one wanted to take her in. At last, she went far away from home.

## Level B2

Once there was a widow who had two daughters. The elder was so much like her mother, in both looks and character, that seeing her was like seeing the mother herself. The two of them were so disagreeable and so proud that it was impossible to live with them. The younger daughter, on the other hand, was the very picture of her late father. She was gentle and courteous, and she was one of the most beautiful girls anyone had ever seen.

People naturally love those who resemble them, so the mother was devoted to her elder daughter. For the younger one, however, she felt a fierce dislike. She made the girl eat in the kitchen, apart from the family, and keep working from dawn till night. Among her many tasks, she had to go twice a day to a fountain a good half league from the house. Each time, she had to carry back a large pitcher full of water.

One day, while she was at the fountain, a poor woman came up and begged her for a drink. "Why, of course, my good woman," said the girl, and she rinsed her pitcher at once. She filled it from the clearest part of the fountain and offered it to the woman. She held the pitcher up the whole time, so that the woman could drink comfortably.

When the woman had finished drinking, she looked at the girl with great kindness. In fact, she was a fairy who had taken the form of a poor village woman. She had come to find out how far the girl's kindness would go. "You are so beautiful, so good and so well-mannered that I cannot help giving you a gift. From now on, a flower or a precious stone will fall from your lips with every word you speak," said the fairy.

When the girl finally reached home with her heavy pitcher, her mother scolded her sharply for coming back so late. "I beg your pardon, Mother, for taking so long," said the poor girl. As she spoke, two roses, two pearls and two large diamonds fell from her mouth. "What on earth is this? Pearls and diamonds seem to be falling out of her mouth! How did this happen, my daughter?" cried her mother in amazement. It was the first time she had ever addressed the girl as her daughter. The girl simply told her everything that had happened, and a great many diamonds tumbled out as she spoke.

"Well, I must certainly send my elder daughter there," said the mother. She called the girl over at once. "Look at what comes out of your sister's mouth when she speaks! Wouldn't you like to be given the same gift? All you have to do is go and fetch water at the fountain. When a poor woman asks you for a drink, give it to her very politely."

"That would be a fine sight, wouldn't it? Me, carrying water from the fountain!" replied the elder girl rudely. "I want you to go," said her mother, "and you will go this very minute." So she went, though she grumbled the whole way, and she took with her the finest silver flask in the house.

No sooner had she reached the fountain than a magnificently dressed lady stepped out of the forest. The lady asked her for a drink. It was the same fairy who had appeared to her sister. This time, however, she had taken the form of a princess, to see how far this girl's rudeness would go. "Do you suppose I came all this way just to give you a drink? I suppose I brought a silver flask on purpose to serve you! Help yourself from the fountain if you want some," said the proud girl.

The fairy showed not the slightest sign of anger, but her answer was firm. "You are not very polite. Since you are so unkind, here is my gift to you. With every word you speak, a snake or a toad will come out of your mouth."

The moment her mother caught sight of her coming home, she called out eagerly, "Well, daughter?" "Well, Mother?" answered the rude girl, and two snakes and two toads dropped from her lips. "Heavens! What do I see? Her sister is to blame for this, and she will pay for it!" cried the mother. She rushed at the younger girl in a fury, and the poor child fled into the nearby forest to escape her.

The king's son, who was riding home through the forest, came upon her there. Seeing how beautiful she was, he asked her what she was doing all alone and why she was weeping. "Alas, sir, my mother has driven me out of the house," she said. As she spoke, he saw five or six pearls and as many diamonds fall from her mouth. He begged her to tell him how such a thing was possible. So she told him her whole story from beginning to end.

The prince fell deeply in love with the gentle girl. He reasoned that such a gift was worth more than any dowry another bride could bring. So he took her to the palace of the king, his father, and married her there.

As for her sister, she made herself so hated that even her own mother drove her out of the house. She wandered from place to place, but no one would take her in. In the end, she went far away from home.

## Level C1

There was once a widow who had two daughters, and the elder was her mother all over again. So closely did she resemble the widow, in face as well as in temper, that to look at the one was to look at the other. Both were so disagreeable and so swollen with pride that nobody could bear to live under the same roof with them. The younger daughter, by contrast, was the very image of her late father, as gentle and courteous as her sister was sour. Besides all this, she was one of the loveliest girls anyone could ever hope to see, though in that house her beauty earned her nothing but resentment.

Since we naturally love whatever resembles us, the mother doted on her elder daughter, while for the younger she nursed a fierce and unrelenting dislike. She made the poor girl take her meals in the kitchen and kept her toiling from the first light of morning until the house was dark again. Among her countless chores was one that never varied from one day to the next. Twice every day she had to walk to a fountain a full half league from the house and carry home a large pitcher brimming with water. It was a long road to travel with so heavy a load, yet she never once failed to make the journey.

One day, as she stood beside the fountain with her pitcher, a poor woman approached her and asked, in a humble voice, whether she might have a little water to drink. "Why, certainly, my good woman," the girl replied, and without a moment's hesitation she rinsed out the pitcher. Then she drew water from the clearest part of the fountain and offered it to the stranger, holding the pitcher up the whole while so that the woman could drink with ease.

When the woman had finished drinking, she looked at the girl with unmistakable warmth, for she was, in truth, no beggar at all. She was a fairy, who had taken on the shape of a poor village woman in order to discover just how far the girl's goodness would stretch. "You are so lovely, so good and so gracious that I cannot help but give you a gift. Henceforth, with every word you utter, either a flower or a precious stone shall fall from your lips," the fairy declared.

The girl got home rather later than usual, and her mother promptly scolded her for having taken so long over a simple errand. "Forgive me, Mother, for being so long," the girl said meekly, and as the words left her, two roses, two pearls and two great diamonds tumbled from her mouth. "What do I see? Pearls and diamonds dropping from her mouth, I do believe! How has this come about, my daughter?" cried the astonished mother. Never before in her life had she called the girl by that name, and it is doubtful whether she even noticed doing so now. The child related, quite simply and without the least boast, all that had happened at the fountain, scattering a small fortune in diamonds as she spoke.

The mother needed no further persuading, for it seemed plain to her that what one daughter had won so easily, the other could surely win as well. "Truly, I must send my elder daughter there," she declared, and she summoned the girl without delay. "Come and see what falls from your sister's mouth whenever she opens it to speak! Wouldn't it please you to have the very same gift for yourself? You need only go to the fountain and fetch water, and when a poor woman asks you for a drink, hand it to her very civilly."

"A pretty sight that would be, me trudging all the way to the fountain with a pitcher!" retorted the elder girl. "I want you to go," said her mother, in a tone that allowed no argument, "and you will go this very minute." Off she went at last, grumbling every step of the way, and she carried with her the handsomest silver flask in the house rather than any humble pitcher.

Scarcely had she reached the fountain when a magnificently dressed lady emerged from the wood and asked her, very graciously, for a drink. This was the very fairy who had appeared to her sister, though no one would have known her now. She had put on the air and the dress of a princess, for she wished to see how far this girl's ill manners would go. "Am I here, then, to serve you water? I suppose I brought a silver flask all this way purely for the pleasure of giving Madam a drink! Help yourself from the fountain, if you have a mind to," replied the proud, ill-bred girl.

The fairy listened to all this without the least trace of anger, and when she answered, her voice was perfectly calm. "You are hardly obliging. Very well: since you are so ungracious, this is the gift I give you in return. With every word you speak, a snake or a toad shall spring from your mouth."

The instant her mother caught sight of her returning, she called out eagerly, "Well, daughter?" "Well, Mother?" answered the rude girl, and out of her mouth came two snakes and two toads. "Heavens above! What is this I see? Her sister is behind this, and she shall pay for it!" cried the mother. Off she ran in a fury toward the younger girl, who fled from the house into the neighboring forest to escape her.

It so happened that the king's son was riding home through that same forest, and he came upon her there, sitting alone and weeping. Struck by her beauty, he asked her what she could be doing all alone in such a place and why she was weeping so bitterly. "Alas, sir, my mother has driven me out of the house," she answered. As she spoke, he watched five or six pearls and as many diamonds fall from her lips, and he begged her to explain how such a marvel could be. So she told him the whole of her adventure, holding nothing back. The prince grew more astonished with every word, for each one brought fresh pearls and diamonds tumbling from her lips.

The king's son, charmed as much by her gentleness as by her beauty, fell deeply in love with her. Reflecting that such a gift was worth more than any dowry another bride might bring him, he took her to the palace of the king his father. There he made her his wife.

Her sister, meanwhile, made herself so hateful that in the end her own mother turned her out of the house. The wretched girl went from door to door in search of shelter, but no one would take her in, and at last she went far away from home.
