# The Clock That Went Backward

Summary: Two cousins inherit a strange old clock whose hands move backward, and it hides a secret.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): clock, time, siege

## Level A1

Harry and Robert were cousins. As boys, they often visited Aunt Gertrude. Her old house had a very old clock. The clock came from Holland. But it was not like other clocks. Its hands moved back, not forward.

Every night, she turned a key in it. "Never let this clock stop," she said. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died. She left the clock to Harry and Robert.

Then Harry and Robert went to Holland. They studied in the city of Leiden. The clock went with them. Their teacher was Professor Van Stopp. One evening, he came to their room. He saw the clock, and his face changed. "I know this clock," said Professor Van Stopp. "I read about it in an old book. It can take people back in time."

"Back to what year?" asked Robert. "To fifteen seventy-four," said Professor Van Stopp. "In that year, Leiden had a siege. Many men from another country stood around it. No food came into the city. The people were very hungry and tired."

All three put their hands on the clock. Professor Van Stopp turned the hands back. Everything went black. Then they stood in old Leiden. The clock stood next to them.

Harry walked to the city wall. A young man came in through a door. He had a letter in his hand. But he was too tired to walk. "Take it to the leader," he said.

Harry ran through the city with the letter. At last, he found the leader. The leader read the letter. "Our friends come soon with food!" he said. "We must wait. We must not give up."

So the people of Leiden waited. A few days later, their friends came. They brought a lot of food. The siege was over.

"Now we go home," said Professor Van Stopp. He turned the hands forward. Everything went black again. Then they were back in their room. "Was it real?" asked Robert. "Yes," said Harry. "We helped Leiden."

## Level A2

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up together. As boys, they spent every summer with their Aunt Gertrude. She lived alone in a big old house. In her front room stood a very old clock. It came from Holland, many years before. But this clock was different from all other clocks. Its hands did not move forward. They moved back.

One evening, he came to visit them. When he saw the clock, his face changed. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I read about it in a very old book. A man in Leiden made it long ago. It can take people back in time."

"Back to what year?" asked Robert. "To fifteen seventy-four," said the old teacher. "In that year, Leiden was under siege. Many men from another country stood around the city for months. Nothing could come into the city, not even food. The people of Leiden became very hungry and tired. Do you want to see it?"

Harry and Robert said yes. The three of them put their hands on the clock. Professor Van Stopp turned the hands back, again and again. The room went black. Then the black went away, and they stood in an old street. The clock stood next to them.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four. The city was very still. No children played in the streets. Harry walked to the city wall. A young man came in through a little door in the wall. He held a letter, but he could not walk any more. "Please take this to the leader," the young man said to Harry.

Harry ran through the streets with the letter. He asked people for the way, and an old woman showed him. At last, he found the leader in a large house. The leader opened the letter and read it. "Our friends come soon with food," he told his people. "We must wait a little longer. We must not give up."

So the people of Leiden waited. A few days later, water came over the land around the city. Their friends came across the water with lots of food. The siege was over, and people ate and laughed in the streets.

"Now it is time to go home," said Professor Van Stopp. The three of them put their hands on the clock again. This time, the old teacher turned the hands forward. The street went black, and then they were back in their room. Robert looked at Harry and asked, "Did that really happen?" Harry smiled. "Yes," he said. "We helped Leiden."

## Level B1

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up together. As boys, they spent every summer with their Aunt Gertrude. She was a quiet woman who lived alone in a large old house. In her front room stood a very old clock that came from Holland long ago. The boys soon noticed something strange about it. Its hands never moved forward like the hands of other clocks; they moved back.

Aunt Gertrude cared for the clock the way a mother cares for a child. Every night she opened a little door in its side and turned a small key. "You must never let this clock stop," she told the boys more than once. Harry and Robert did not understand her, but they remembered her words. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died quietly. She left the old clock to Harry and Robert.

When they were older, the cousins went to Holland to study at the university in Leiden. They took the clock with them and put it in the corner of their room. There it went on ticking backward, just as before. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a kind old man who loved history. One evening he came to visit them. When he saw the clock, his face went pale. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I read about it in a very old book in the university library. A clockmaker of Leiden built it long ago, in the hardest years of this city. It does not only count time backward. It can carry people back in time."

"Back to which year?" asked Robert, who did not believe a word. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor answered. "That was the year of the great siege. An army from another country stood around Leiden for months. Nothing could come in, not even food. The people were very hungry and tired, but they did not give up. My book says that a single letter kept their hope alive." "Will you come with me and see it?" the professor asked the two young men.

Harry ran through the empty streets with the letter in his hand. He did not know the way, so he asked an old woman. She pointed to a large house near the center of the city. Inside, he found the leader of the city, a tired man with a gray face. The leader opened the letter and read it twice. Then, for the first time in many weeks, he smiled. "Our friends will come soon with food," he told the people who stood around him. "We must wait a little longer. We must not give up now."

## Level B2

Harry and his cousin Robert grew up almost like brothers. Every summer of their boyhood was spent at the house of their Aunt Gertrude. She was a quiet, serious woman who lived alone among old furniture and older memories. In her front room stood a tall clock of dark wood, which her family had brought from Holland generations before. The boys found it fascinating, because it did something no other clock in the world seemed to do. Its hands did not move forward around the face; they moved slowly and steadily backward. Sometimes the boys found their aunt sitting in front of it for an hour, silently watching the hands creep back.

Aunt Gertrude treated the clock with a care that bordered on love. Every night before bed, she opened a small door in its side and turned a little brass key. "Whatever happens, never let this clock stop," she told the boys more than once. Neither Harry nor Robert understood what she meant, but neither of them forgot it. Many years later, Aunt Gertrude died peacefully, and in her will she left the old clock to her two nephews.

When they were old enough, the cousins sailed to Holland to study at the famous university in Leiden. They brought the clock with them and set it up in a corner of their room. There it went on ticking backward, exactly as before. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a gentle old scholar who seemed to know everything about the history of the city. One evening he came to their room for a cup of tea. The moment he saw the clock, the color left his face. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice. "I have read about it in a very old book in the university library. It was built by a clockmaker of Leiden in the darkest years this city has ever known." He laid a hand on its wooden case almost tenderly. "Most people think of time as a road that runs in only one direction. This clock does not simply count the hours backward; it can carry people back along that road."

"Back to when?" Robert asked. He clearly did not believe a word of it. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor replied. "That was the year of the great siege. For months an army from another country stood around Leiden, and nothing could get in, not even food. The people were desperately hungry and tired, yet they refused to give up. According to my book, a single letter kept their hope alive at the most important moment." The professor turned to the two young men. "Would you like to see it with your own eyes?"

Harry and Robert exchanged a long look, and then they both nodded. The three of them placed their hands on the old clock, and Professor Van Stopp turned its hands back, around and around. The room grew dark, and the ticking became so loud that it seemed to fill the whole world. Then the darkness lifted, and they found themselves in a narrow street of old brick houses. Robert touched the wall of the nearest house, as if to make sure it was real. The clock stood next to them on the cobblestones, ticking quietly.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four, and the city was terribly still. No children were playing, no one was selling anything in the market, and the people who passed looked thin and exhausted. While the professor and Robert stayed with the clock, Harry walked toward the city wall to see more. There he watched a young man squeeze through a small door in the wall and stumble inside. The stranger was clutching a letter, but he was so weak from his journey that he sank to the ground. "Please take this to the leader of the city," the young man begged Harry. "It is very important, and I cannot walk another step."

Harry ran through the empty streets with the letter held tightly in his hand. He did not know the way, so he asked an old woman for help. She pointed him toward a large house near the center of the city. Inside he found the mayor, the leader of the city, a tired man with a gray face and kind eyes. The mayor broke the seal, read the letter twice, and then, for the first time in many weeks, he smiled. "Our friends will come soon with food," he told the people who had gathered around him. "We must hold on a little longer. We must not give up now."

So the people of Leiden waited, and as they waited their hope came back. Each morning they climbed onto the wall and looked out over the flat land, searching for a sign. A few days later, water began to spread across the fields around the city. Far away, their friends had opened the sea walls to let the sea cover the land. Then their friends arrived across the water in boats that were full of food. The siege was over, and people ate bread in the streets, laughed, cried and held one another.

"It is time for us to go home," said Professor Van Stopp quietly. Once more the three of them laid their hands on the clock, and this time the professor turned its hands forward. The street faded into darkness, and a moment later they were back in the cousins' room. In its corner, the clock went on ticking as if nothing had happened. "Did all of that really happen?" asked Robert, staring at the professor. Harry thought of the young man at the wall and the letter he had carried, and he smiled. "Yes," Harry said. "It happened, and we helped Leiden."

## Level C1

Harry and his cousin Robert were raised almost as brothers, and every summer of their boyhood was spent in the rambling old house of their Aunt Gertrude. She was a reserved, serious woman who lived alone among heavy furniture and heavier memories, and she rarely spoke unless there was something worth saying. In her front room stood a tall clock of dark, polished wood, which her family had carried over from Holland so many generations before that nobody remembered exactly when. To the two boys it was the most fascinating object in the house, for it did something that no other clock in the world appeared to do. Its hands did not travel forward around the face, as every honest clock's hands should; they crept slowly, steadily and quite deliberately backward. More than once the boys came upon their aunt sitting before it in the half-light, her hands folded in her lap, watching the minutes return to wherever minutes come from.

Aunt Gertrude tended the clock with a devotion that bordered on love. Each night, just before she climbed the stairs to bed, she opened a little door in its side and turned a small brass key that she kept on a ribbon. "Whatever else happens, never let this clock stop," she told the boys on more than one occasion, without ever explaining why. Neither Harry nor Robert understood what she meant, yet neither of them could quite forget the strange urgency in her voice. Many years later Aunt Gertrude died peacefully, and her will, to the surprise of the rest of the family, left the old clock to her two nephews.

When they were old enough, the cousins sailed to Holland to study at the ancient university of Leiden, and naturally the clock went with them. They set it up in a corner of their lodgings, where it resumed its backward ticking as calmly as if it had never left home. Their favorite teacher was Professor Van Stopp, a gentle, absent-minded scholar who seemed to carry the whole history of the city in his head. One evening he climbed the stairs to their room for a cup of tea, and the instant his eyes fell on the clock, the color drained from his face. "I know this clock," Professor Van Stopp said in a low voice, as though afraid of waking it. "I have read of it in a very old book in the university library, a book few people have ever bothered to open. It was built by a clockmaker of Leiden during the darkest years this city has ever known." He laid a hand on its wooden case, almost tenderly. "Most people imagine time as a road that runs in only one direction, from yesterday to tomorrow. This clock does not merely count the hours backward, my young friends; it can carry those who touch it back along that road."

"Back to when, exactly?" Robert asked. His tone made it plain that he did not believe a single word. "To fifteen seventy-four," the professor replied, quite unoffended. "To the year of the great siege. For months an army from another country surrounded Leiden, and nothing could pass through its lines, not even a loaf of bread. The people grew desperately hungry and tired, and yet, against all reason, they refused to give up. According to my book, it was a single letter, arriving at the most important moment, that kept their hope alive." He looked from one cousin to the other, and his eyes were bright. "Would you care to see it for yourselves?"

Harry and Robert exchanged a long glance, in which curiosity slowly won out over doubt, and then they both nodded. The three of them placed their hands on the old clock, and Professor Van Stopp began to turn its hands back, around and around, faster and faster. The room grew dark, and the ticking swelled until it seemed to fill not only their ears but the whole of the world. Then, as suddenly as it had come, the darkness lifted, and they found themselves standing in a narrow street of tall brick houses. The clock stood beside them on the cobblestones, ticking away as quietly and patiently as ever. Robert reached out and pressed his palm against the rough wall of the nearest house, as if the bricks might dissolve at his touch.

This was Leiden in fifteen seventy-four, and the silence of the city was the first thing they noticed. No children played in the lanes, no one called out prices in the market, and the few people who passed by were thin, pale and visibly exhausted. While the professor and Robert stayed to guard the clock, Harry wandered toward the city wall, hoping to understand where they had arrived. There he watched a young man squeeze through a small door in the wall and stumble inside, gasping for breath. The stranger was clutching a letter to his chest, but he was so worn out by his journey that his legs gave way, and he sank to the ground. "Please, take this to the leader of the city," the young man begged, pressing the letter into Harry's hands. "It matters more than I can tell you, and I cannot walk another step."

Harry ran through the empty streets with the letter held tightly against him, his footsteps echoing between the silent houses. He had no idea where to go, so he stopped an old woman, who studied him for a moment before pointing toward a large house near the city's center. Inside he found the mayor, the leader of the city, a tired man with a gray face and kind, sleepless eyes. The mayor broke the seal, read the letter once, then again more slowly, and then, for the first time in many weeks, he smiled. "Our friends will come soon with food," he announced to the people who had crowded around him to hear the news. "We must hold on a little longer. Whatever we do, we must not give up now."

So the people of Leiden waited, and as they waited, the hope that had nearly left them crept quietly back. Every morning they climbed onto the wall and gazed out across the flat gray land, searching the horizon for any sign at all. A few days later, water began to spread across the flat fields around the city. Far away, their friends had opened the sea walls and let the sea pour over the land, so that boats could at last reach the city. Then those friends came gliding across the new water in boats piled high with food. The siege was over, and people ate bread in the streets, laughed and wept at the same time, and held one another as if they would never let go.

"I think it is time for us to go home," said Professor Van Stopp gently, once the celebrations had begun. Once more the three of them laid their hands on the clock, and this time the professor turned its hands forward with the same steady patience. The bright street faded into darkness, and a moment later they were back in the cousins' room, with the old clock ticking calmly in its corner. "Did all of that really happen?" asked Robert at last, staring at the professor as though he had never seen him before. Harry thought of the exhausted young man at the wall and of the letter that had passed through his own hands, and he smiled. "Yes," Harry said. "It happened, and in our own small way, we helped Leiden."
