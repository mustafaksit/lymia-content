# Off on a Comet

Summary: A French captain and his loyal helper wake up one morning and see the sun rise in the west.

Glossed key words (A1/A2): comet, balloon, volcano

## Level A1

Hector Servadac lived in Algeria, near the Mediterranean. He was a French captain. Ben Zoof worked for him. The two men were good friends.

It was the last night of the year. That night, the whole land moved. Servadac and Ben Zoof fell down.

In the morning, the sun came up. But it came up in the west! The day was very short, too. Six hours later, it was night again. Ben Zoof jumped over a small wall. He went up very high! "I am very light!" said Ben Zoof. Their land was a small island.

Then a ship came. Servadac went on it, but Ben Zoof stayed. They looked for other lands. But most of the world was gone! They found only a few small places. In one place, they found a little girl. They also saw a big volcano.

Later, they found notes in the water. Each note had a new name: Gallia. Who wrote them? Nobody knew.

Soon the sun was very far away. It was very, very cold. The water turned to ice. Servadac remembered the big volcano. There was fire in the volcano. So all the people lived in it.

The last note named an island. Servadac went there over the ice. He found an old man there. It was Palmyrin Rosette, Servadac's old teacher! Palmyrin Rosette studied the stars. He wrote the notes. Servadac took him back to the volcano.

Palmyrin Rosette told them everything. "A comet came near our world," he said. "It took this land with it. Now we all live on the comet. Its name is Gallia. In two years, it comes back. But it does not stop."

"A balloon can take us home!" said Servadac. So they made a big balloon. Two years later, Gallia came back. Everyone got into the balloon. It went up, and then it came down. They were in Algeria again! It was the first day of the year. Palmyrin Rosette was sad. His comet went away without him. But Servadac and Ben Zoof were very happy. They were home at last!

## Level A2

Hector Servadac was a captain in the French army. He lived in a small house in Algeria, near the Mediterranean. Ben Zoof lived with him and helped him every day. The two men were good friends.

On the last night of December, the whole land moved. The two men fell to the floor. They stayed there until morning.

When Servadac went outside, the sun came up. But it came up in the west! The day was also very short. After only six hours, it was night again. Ben Zoof tried to jump over a small wall. He flew high into the air. "I am as light as a bird!" said Ben Zoof. Servadac felt light too. The two men walked down to the water. Most of the land was gone. Their small piece of Algeria was now an island.

During the trip, they found notes in the sea. The notes were about the stars and had a new name: Gallia. Nobody knew the writer.

Every month, the sun looked smaller. The air became very cold, and the sea turned to ice. "We must go to the volcano," said Servadac. There was fire deep inside it, so it was never cold there. All the people moved into the volcano. They lived there through the long, dark months.

The last note had the name of a small island. Servadac and the count went across the ice to it. In a little house, they found an old man. He was very cold and could not stand up. It was Palmyrin Rosette, Servadac's old teacher! Palmyrin Rosette studied the stars, and he wrote all the notes. They carried him back to the volcano.

When the old man felt better, he explained everything. "A comet came very close to our world," said Palmyrin Rosette. "It took a piece of our world with it. We all live on this comet now. I call it Gallia, and it is very small. So everything feels light here." "Can we ever go home?" asked Servadac. "In two years, Gallia comes back near our world," said Palmyrin Rosette. "But it does not stop. It only comes close for a short time."

After many months, the cold went away. Then Servadac had an idea. "We can make a balloon," he said. "When Gallia comes close, the balloon can take us home." Everyone helped, and they made a big balloon from the ship's sails.

Two years later, Gallia came close to our world. Everyone got into the balloon, and it rose high into the air. Then it came down softly in Algeria, near Servadac's old home. It was the first day of January again. Palmyrin Rosette was not happy, because his comet went away without him. But Servadac and Ben Zoof were happy, because they were home.

## Level B1

Hector Servadac was a young captain in the French army. He lived in Algeria, in a small house near the Mediterranean. His helper was Ben Zoof, a cheerful soldier who loved his captain more than anything. The two men lived a simple life, and they were happy together.

On the last night of December, Servadac was sitting at his table and writing a letter. Ben Zoof was sleeping in the corner of the room. Suddenly the ground began to shake, and a terrible noise filled the air. Both men fell to the floor, and everything went black. They did not wake up until the next morning.

When Servadac woke up, he went outside and looked at the sky. The sun was rising, but it was rising in the west! Later he noticed that the day was much shorter than before. The sun went down only six hours after it came up. Ben Zoof tried to jump over a small wall, and he flew high into the air. "Captain, I am as light as a bird!" he shouted. Servadac felt the same strange lightness in his own body. The two men walked down to the shore and stared at the water. Most of the land around them was gone. Their small part of Algeria was now an island in a strange, empty sea.

A few days later, a ship arrived at the island. It belonged to a Russian count, who was traveling on the Mediterranean with his sailors. Servadac joined the count, while Ben Zoof stayed behind to look after the island. For many weeks they sailed around and searched for other countries. They found almost nothing, only a few small islands where big countries used to be. On one island, they found a little girl who was living alone with her goat. They also saw a huge volcano on a rocky coast far in the south. Later, a small group of Spanish workers joined them as well.

During the trip, the sailors found several notes floating in the sea. Someone had written them and thrown them into the water. The notes were full of numbers about the sun and the stars. Each note also had a new name on it: Gallia. Nobody knew who the writer was.

Month after month, the sun looked smaller and gave less heat. Soon the air became bitterly cold, and the sea turned to ice. Servadac remembered the huge volcano in the south. Deep inside it, a river of fire kept the rock warm. So all the people moved into the volcano and made their rooms in its warm tunnels. There they lived through the long, dark winter.

The last note named a small island, and Servadac and the count decided to go there. They built a light sled with a sail. The wind pushed it quickly across the frozen sea. On the island they found a tiny hut, and inside it lay an old man. He was very cold and weak, but he was still alive. Servadac knew him at once, because it was Palmyrin Rosette, his old teacher! Palmyrin Rosette studied the stars, and he was the writer of the notes. They wrapped him in warm coats and carried him back to the volcano.

When the old man felt better, he explained everything to them. "A comet came very close to the Earth on that December night," said Palmyrin Rosette. "It carried away a piece of the Earth, with some air and some sea. We are all living on that comet now, and I gave it a name: Gallia. It is much smaller than the Earth, so everything feels light here." "Will we ever see the Earth again?" asked Servadac. "Yes," said Palmyrin Rosette. "In two years, Gallia will come back near the Earth. But it will not stop there. It will pass close for only a short time."

Slowly the months passed, and the sun grew bigger and warmer again. The ice melted, and the people came out of the volcano into the light. Servadac thought hard about the old man's words, and he had an idea. "We will make a balloon," he said. "When Gallia passes close to the Earth, the balloon will carry us home." Everyone worked together, and they made a large balloon from the ship's sails.

Two years after that strange night, Gallia came close to the Earth again. Everyone climbed into the balloon, and it rose high above the comet. Then it came softly down in a field in Algeria, near Servadac's old home. It was the first day of January. Palmyrin Rosette watched his comet disappear into space, and he looked very sad. But Servadac and Ben Zoof were full of joy, because they were home at last.

## Level B2

Hector Servadac was a captain in the French army, and his post was in Algeria, on the shore of the Mediterranean. He was a cheerful, rather careless young man who preferred writing poems to studying maps. He lived in a simple house near the sea with his orderly, Ben Zoof. Ben Zoof was a loyal soldier who would have followed his captain to the end of the world. Neither man could have guessed how far that journey would really take them.

On the last night of December, Servadac sat at his table with a poem, while Ben Zoof slept in the corner. Without warning, the ground lifted under them and a deafening roar filled the air. Both men were thrown to the floor, and everything went black. They lay there without moving until the morning.

When Servadac stepped outside, he could not believe his eyes. The sun was rising, but it was rising in the west instead of the east. The day was strangely short, too, because the sun set only six hours after it had come up. Ben Zoof tried to jump over a small wall and flew high into the air, far higher than any man should. "Captain, I am as light as a bird!" he shouted from above. Servadac noticed that his own body felt light, that the air was thin, and that water boiled much faster than usual. The two men hurried down to the shore and stared at the sea in silence. The land around them had vanished, and their small piece of Algeria had become an island.

A few days later, a ship appeared on the horizon and dropped anchor near the island. It belonged to a Russian count, who had been sailing on the Mediterranean with his crew. Servadac went aboard to join the search, while Ben Zoof proudly stayed behind to guard the island. For many weeks the ship sailed in every direction, but whole countries seemed to have disappeared. Where great coasts had once stood, the travelers found only a few small, rocky islands. On one of them, they discovered a little girl who was living alone with her goat, and she sailed on with them. On a large rock in the west, a group of English soldiers politely thanked them but chose to stay where they were. Far in the south, the travelers also passed a huge volcano on a rocky coast. Later, a small group of Spanish workers joined the little community as well.

During the voyage, the sailors pulled several notes out of the sea. Each one had been written by an unknown hand and thrown into the water. The notes were full of numbers about distances, the sun and the stars. Each note also carried a name nobody had heard before: Gallia. Whoever the writer was, he clearly understood what had happened to them.

Month after month, the sun shrank in the sky and gave less heat. At last the air became bitterly cold, and the sea froze into a single sheet of ice. Servadac remembered the huge volcano in the south, so he led everyone there. Deep inside the mountain, a slow river of fire kept the rock warm. The people made their rooms in its tunnels and lived there through the long, dark winter. Ben Zoof, who could never stay sad for long, kept everyone cheerful with his stories and songs.

The last note had named a small island, and Servadac and the count decided to search it. They built a light sled with a sail, and the wind pushed it swiftly across the frozen sea. On the island they found a tiny hut, and inside it lay an old man who was very cold and weak. Servadac knew him at once, because it was Palmyrin Rosette, his old teacher. Palmyrin Rosette was a scientist who had spent his life studying the stars, and he was the writer of the notes. He was also a difficult, impatient man, and he had never been fond of his former student. They wrapped him in warm coats and carried him back to the volcano.

When the old man had recovered, he explained everything with great pride. "I saw this comet coming long before anyone else," said Palmyrin Rosette. "On the last night of December, it brushed against the Earth. It carried away a piece of the Earth, with some air and some sea. We are all living on the comet now, and I named it Gallia. It is much smaller than the Earth, and that is why everything feels so light here." "Will we ever see the Earth again?" asked Servadac. "In two years, Gallia will come back to the same place and pass close to the Earth," said Palmyrin Rosette. "But it will not stop, and it will be close for only a moment." To the old scientist, this was not bad news, because he had no wish to leave his comet.

Slowly the months passed, and the sun grew larger and warmer again. The ice melted, and the people came out of the volcano into the light. Servadac thought hard about the old man's numbers, and he had an idea. "If we are in the air when Gallia passes, the air of the Earth may hold us," he said. "We will make a balloon and let it carry us home." Everyone worked together, and they sewed a large balloon from the ship's sails.

Two years after that strange night, Gallia came close to the Earth again, exactly as the old man had said. Everyone climbed into the balloon, and it rose high above the comet. For a few moments, the travelers felt the wind change around them. Then the balloon came softly down in a field in Algeria, near Servadac's old home. It was the first day of January. Palmyrin Rosette watched his comet disappear into space, and he looked very sad. But Servadac and Ben Zoof were full of joy, because they were home at last. Later, few people believed their story, yet the two friends never forgot their long winter on the comet.

## Level C1

Hector Servadac, a captain in the French army, had been posted to Algeria, where his small house looked out over the bright water of the Mediterranean. He was a good-natured, rather careless young officer who would sooner spend an evening polishing a poem than poring over a map. His orderly, Ben Zoof, was a loyal soldier of boundless cheerfulness, convinced that no captain in the whole army could compare with his own. Neither of them, on that mild winter evening, could have imagined how far from home their quiet routine was about to carry them.

On the last night of December, Servadac sat at his table struggling with the final lines of a poem, while Ben Zoof snored peacefully in the corner. Without the slightest warning, the ground heaved beneath them, a roar louder than any thunder filled the air, and both men were flung to the floor. Everything went black, and they lay there, senseless and still, until the first light of morning crept through the window.

What Servadac saw when he stepped outside made him doubt his own eyes, for the sun was rising, calmly and unmistakably, in the west. Nor was that the only puzzle, since the day proved absurdly short: barely six hours after it had appeared, the sun slipped below the horizon again. Ben Zoof, meaning only to hop over a small wall, sailed high into the air and came down laughing, as if he had become an acrobat overnight. "Captain, I am as light as a bird!" he called out, half delighted and half alarmed. Servadac, too, felt strangely weightless; the air seemed thin in his chest, and when Ben Zoof set a pot on the fire, the water boiled almost at once. Hurrying down to the shore, the two men found the coastline unrecognizable, for the land beyond their little corner of Algeria had simply vanished, leaving them on an island.

A few days later, a ship appeared on the horizon and dropped anchor off the island, much to the relief of its two lonely inhabitants. It belonged to a Russian count who had been cruising the Mediterranean with his crew, and who was every bit as bewildered as they were. Servadac went aboard to join the search, while Ben Zoof, swelling with importance, remained behind as governor of the island. For weeks the ship sailed in every direction, yet wherever a coast should have risen from the sea, there was only open water or a few scattered, barren rocks. On one of them the travelers discovered a little girl living alone with her goat, and she came aboard as calmly as if she had been expecting them. On a great rock in the west, a company of English soldiers received them with perfect courtesy, declined every offer of help, and carried on as though nothing had changed. Far in the south, the voyagers passed a huge volcano rising from a rocky coast, and Servadac took careful note of its position. Later, a small band of Spanish workers joined the little community as well, so that it numbered about thirty souls.

It was during this voyage that the sailors began fishing curious notes out of the sea, each sealed against the water and plainly thrown there on purpose. The notes were crowded with figures concerning distances, the sun and the stars, set down in a hand that was confident to the point of rudeness. Each one bore a name that none of them had ever heard: Gallia. Whoever their author might be, he evidently understood their situation far better than they did, and Servadac longed to meet him.

As the weeks became months, the sun dwindled in the sky until it seemed hardly larger than a coin, and its warmth faded with it. The air grew bitterly cold, and one still night the whole sea froze into a single, gleaming sheet of ice. Remembering the volcano in the south, Servadac led everyone there, and they found that a slow river of fire deep within the mountain kept its rocky passages comfortably warm. In those tunnels they made their homes and waited out the long, dark winter, with Ben Zoof's songs and stories warming them nearly as much as the mountain itself.

The last of the notes had named a small island, and Servadac and the count resolved to cross the frozen sea in search of its writer. They built a light sled, fitted it with a sail, and let the wind drive them across the ice at a speed that left them breathless. On the island stood a tiny hut, and inside it, wrapped in every blanket he owned, lay an old man too cold and weak to rise. Servadac recognized him at once, with a mixture of joy and dismay, as Palmyrin Rosette, the teacher whose lessons he had once found so tiresome. Palmyrin Rosette had devoted his whole life to the stars, and it was he, of course, who had written the notes. He was also impatient, sharp-tongued and quick to take offense, and he had never had a high opinion of his former pupil. Nevertheless, they bundled him in warm coats and carried him back to the volcano, grumbling all the way.

Once he had recovered, the old scientist explained their situation with undisguised pride, as if he had arranged the whole affair himself. "I observed this comet long before anyone else," said Palmyrin Rosette. "On the last night of December, it brushed against the Earth, and it tore away a piece of the Earth, together with some of its air and its sea. We are all passengers on the comet now, and I have named it Gallia. Since it is much smaller than the Earth, it pulls on us far more gently, which is why you all go bouncing about like children." "And will we ever see the Earth again?" Servadac asked. "In precisely two years, Gallia will return to the very same point and pass close to the Earth once more," said Palmyrin Rosette. "It will not stop, however, and the meeting will last no more than a moment." To the old man this was hardly bad news, since he could think of nothing he wanted less than to leave his comet.

Slowly the months went by, and the sun grew larger and kinder. At last the ice softened, and the people came blinking out of the volcano into the light. Servadac turned the old man's figures over in his mind for many evenings before an idea finally took shape. "If we are already floating in the air when Gallia passes, the air of the Earth may hold us and let the comet go on without us," he said. "We shall build a balloon and trust it to carry us home." Everyone set to work, and with patient stitching they turned the ship's sails into a great balloon.

Two years after that strange night, exactly as the old man had foretold, Gallia swept close to the Earth once more. The whole company climbed into the balloon, which rose high above the comet while the wind roared and swirled around them. Then, almost gently, the balloon came drifting down into a field in Algeria, not far from Servadac's old home. It was the first day of January, and the new year had only just begun. Palmyrin Rosette stood apart, watching his comet shrink into the depths of space, and he looked like a man who had lost the only friend he ever valued. Servadac and Ben Zoof, by contrast, could hardly stop smiling, for after two long years among the stars they were home at last. Few people would ever believe their story, but that troubled the two friends far less than one might expect.
