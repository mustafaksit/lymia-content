# Pinocchio

Summary: A naughty wooden puppet runs away from school, meets tricksters and monsters, and dreams of becoming a real boy.

## Level A1

Geppetto was old and very poor. He made a puppet from a talking log. He called the puppet Pinocchio. "Now I have a son," he said.

But Pinocchio was a bad puppet. He ran away and played all day. The Talking Cricket spoke to him. "Be good to your father." Pinocchio threw a hammer at it.

Geppetto sold his coat in the cold. He bought a school book for Pinocchio. Pinocchio sold it for a puppet show. Fire-Eater owned the show. He wanted to burn Pinocchio. Then he felt sorry and gave him gold.

Then Pinocchio met a fox and a cat. They wanted his gold. At night, they tied him to a tree. A kind Fairy saved him. "Where is your gold?" she asked. "I lost it," said Pinocchio. But it was in his pocket. His nose grew long! With every lie, it grew more. The Fairy laughed and made it small again.

Later the fox and cat came back. "Plant your gold in our field," they said. Pinocchio did, but the gold was soon gone.

Pinocchio went to school at last. "Be good," said the Fairy. "Then you can be a real boy." But his friend Candlewick had a plan. "Come to the Land of Toys!" There was no school there, only games. Soon the boys had donkey ears! They were little donkeys now.

A man threw Pinocchio into the sea. In the water, he was a puppet again. Then a huge fish ate him. Inside the fish sat Geppetto! "I came to find you," he cried. The fish slept with its mouth open. Pinocchio swam out with his father.

Pinocchio worked hard for his father. He did not tell lies now. One morning, he was a real boy! "I am not a puppet now!" he said.

## Level A2

An old carpenter once found a strange piece of wood. When he hit it with his axe, the wood cried out! He was afraid, so he gave it to his friend Geppetto. Geppetto made a wooden puppet from it. He named the puppet Pinocchio. Before he finished, the puppet laughed at him.

Pinocchio was very naughty. As soon as he could walk, he ran out of the house. At home, the Talking Cricket gave him some advice. "Go to school and listen to your father." Pinocchio got angry and threw a hammer at it.

It was winter, but Geppetto sold his only coat. With the money, he bought a school book for his son. On the way to school, Pinocchio heard music. He sold his book and bought a ticket to a puppet show. The owner, Fire-Eater, had a long black beard. He needed wood for his fire, so he took Pinocchio. But Pinocchio cried about his poor father. Fire-Eater sneezed, and his heart became soft. He gave Pinocchio five gold coins for Geppetto.

On the road home, Pinocchio met a fox and a cat. "Come with us to a magic field," said the fox. "Plant your coins there, and a money tree grows." That night, two robbers in black ran after Pinocchio. They looked very much like the fox and the cat. They caught him and tied him to a tall tree.

A beautiful Fairy saw him from her window and saved him. "Where are your gold coins?" she asked. "I lost them," said Pinocchio, but they were in his pocket. His nose grew longer. He told another lie, and it grew again. Soon he could not turn around. The Fairy laughed, and some birds made his nose short again.

Later, the fox and the cat found him again. He planted his coins in their field and waited. When he came back, the coins were gone. Pinocchio went back to the Fairy. He went to school and worked hard for a whole year. "Tomorrow you can be a real boy," she told him.

But that evening he met his lazy friend Candlewick. "Come with me to the Land of Toys," he said. "Nobody goes to school there, and every day is a holiday." For five months the boys played from morning to night. Then one morning, Pinocchio touched his head and felt long ears. Soon he and Candlewick were donkeys!

A man bought Pinocchio and threw him into the sea. In the water, he became a wooden puppet again. A giant fish swam up and swallowed him. At a little table inside sat Geppetto with a candle! "I went to sea to look for you," he cried.

The fish slept with its mouth open. Pinocchio took his father on his back and swam out. Every day, Pinocchio worked for a farmer. In return, he got milk for Geppetto. When the Fairy was sick, he sent her all his money. In the morning, he woke up as a real boy! An old wooden puppet sat in the corner. "Goodbye, little puppet," said Pinocchio with a smile.

## Level B1

An old carpenter once found a piece of wood that could talk. Frightened, he gave it to his poor neighbor, Geppetto. Geppetto carved a puppet from the wood and called it Pinocchio. While he was working, the eyes began to stare at him and the mouth laughed. As soon as Pinocchio had legs, he ran out into the street.

Back at home, he met the Talking Cricket. "Children who run away from their fathers are never happy," it warned. Pinocchio grew angry and threw a hammer at the Cricket. Geppetto sold his only coat to buy his son a spelling book. "Now you can go to school like other boys," he said. But on the way, Pinocchio heard music from a puppet theater. He sold the book and bought a ticket.

The owner of the theater was a huge man called Fire-Eater. He needed wood for his dinner fire, and he decided to burn Pinocchio. But whenever Fire-Eater felt sorry for someone, he sneezed. When Pinocchio cried about his poor father, the big man began to sneeze. In the end he gave the puppet five gold coins for Geppetto.

On the road, Pinocchio met a fox and a cat. The fox walked on three legs, and the cat pretended to be blind. "We know a magic field where money grows on trees," said the fox. Pinocchio forgot about his poor father and followed them to an inn. At midnight the two animals left, and he had to pay for their dinner.

In the dark woods, two robbers in black sacks jumped out at him. Pinocchio hid the coins under his tongue and ran. The robbers caught him at last and tied him high in an oak tree. Nearby lived a Fairy with turquoise hair, and she saw everything. She sent a bird to bring the poor puppet down.

"Where are your gold coins now?" the Fairy asked kindly. "I lost them," Pinocchio answered, but they were in his pocket. At once his nose grew two inches longer. With every new lie, it grew more, until he could not turn around. "A lie always shows, and yours shows on your face," the Fairy laughed. Then a thousand woodpeckers came and made his nose short again.

Later, the fox and the cat took him to their magic field. He buried his coins there and went away to wait. When he came back, he found only an empty hole. A parrot in a tree laughed at him for believing such a silly story. Pinocchio returned to the Fairy, and she became a mother to him. He went to school for a whole year and worked hard. "Tomorrow you will become a real boy," she promised one evening.

That same evening, Pinocchio met his best friend, Candlewick. Candlewick, the laziest boy in the school, was waiting for a coach. "I am going to the Land of Toys," he said. "There, every week has six Thursdays and one Sunday, and nobody studies." Pinocchio said no twice, but the third time he jumped on a donkey.

For five months the boys played and never opened a book. Then one morning Pinocchio woke up with long, hairy donkey ears. Soon both friends were walking on four legs. The coach driver sold Pinocchio to a circus, where he hurt his leg. His next owner wanted his skin for a drum and threw him into the sea. Fish in the sea ate the donkey skin away, and a wooden puppet came up instead.

Suddenly a terrible dogfish opened its mouth and swallowed him. Far inside, an old man was sitting at a table with a candle. It was Geppetto, and he cried with joy when he saw his son. Months before, he went to sea to look for Pinocchio, and the dogfish swallowed him. The dogfish slept with its mouth open, so they waited for the right moment. Pinocchio carried his father over the huge tongue and swam to the shore.

For months, he worked hard to get milk for his weak father. When he heard the Fairy was sick and poor, he sent her all his savings. That night the Fairy kissed him in a dream. When he woke up, he was not a puppet anymore but a real boy. Geppetto was healthy again, and an old wooden puppet sat in the corner. Pinocchio looked at it and laughed, glad to be free of it at last.

## Level B2

An old carpenter once found an ordinary log and decided to make a table leg from it. As soon as his axe touched the wood, a tiny voice begged him to stop. Terrified, he gave the log to his neighbor Geppetto, a poor old man. Geppetto planned to carve a puppet that could dance, so that he could earn his living with it. He named it Pinocchio before it even had a face. While he worked, the eyes stared at him and the unfinished mouth laughed.

The moment Pinocchio had feet, he ran out of the door and down the street. A policeman caught him, but the crowd took the puppet's side, and Geppetto spent the night in prison. At home, Pinocchio found the Talking Cricket climbing slowly up the wall. It told him that runaway boys always come to a bad end. Pinocchio told it to be quiet, and when it refused, he threw a hammer at it.

That night, hungry and cold, he fell asleep with his feet on a pan of hot coals. By morning his wooden feet had burned away, so Geppetto made him a new pair. Then, although it was the middle of winter, he sold his only coat to buy his son a spelling book. Pinocchio promised to go straight to school and make his father proud. Yet on the way he heard pipes and drums from a great puppet theater. Within a few minutes he had sold the book and bought a ticket.

Inside, the wooden actors recognized him and pulled him onto the stage, so the show fell into chaos. Their master, Fire-Eater, was an enormous man whose black beard reached down to the floor. His dinner was roasting, but he had run out of firewood. He ordered his men to throw the new puppet on the flames. Fortunately, whenever Fire-Eater felt pity, he sneezed. When Pinocchio wept and spoke about his poor father, the giant sneezed loudly and changed his mind. He even gave the puppet five gold coins and told him to take them home at once.

Pinocchio had not gone far when he met a fox, lame in one leg, and a cat, blind in both eyes. Both were pretending, of course, and they could see the gold clearly. The fox described a field where a buried coin would grow overnight into a tree covered in money. Pinocchio believed him, and they stopped at an inn, where the fox and the cat ate an enormous supper. When the puppet woke at midnight, they had disappeared, and he had to pay the bill himself.

Out in the dark countryside, two figures in black sacks leapt at him and demanded his money. He hid the coins under his tongue and ran, but at last they caught him. They tied him to a high branch of a great oak and left him swinging in the wind. From a little white house nearby, a girl with turquoise hair saw everything. She was in fact a Fairy, and she sent a falcon to set him free.

Later the Fairy asked him what had happened to his gold coins. He said that he had lost them, although they were in his pocket, and at once his nose grew two inches. With each new lie it grew longer, until he could no longer turn around in the room. The Fairy laughed and told him that a liar can never hide, because his lies show on his face. Then she let in a flock of woodpeckers, which pecked his nose back to its normal size.

Soon the fox and the cat found him again and led him to their magical field. He buried his four remaining coins there and watered them as he had been told. When he returned, he found only an empty hole and a parrot laughing at him. He reported the theft to the judge, but the judge, an old gorilla, sent the honest victim to prison. Four months later the emperor freed all the criminals. The guard released Pinocchio only after he insisted that he was a criminal too.

After more adventures, Pinocchio found the Fairy again, now grown into a woman, and she agreed to be his mother. He worked hard at school, and by the end of the year he was the best student in his class. Pleased with him, the Fairy promised that the next day he would become a real boy.

That evening he met Candlewick, the laziest boy in the school, who was waiting for a coach. He was going to the Land of Toys, a country with no schools, no teachers, and no books. There, he explained, every week had six Thursdays and one Sunday. Pinocchio refused twice, but the third time he jumped on a donkey and rode away with the others. The coach was pulled by twelve pairs of donkeys and driven by a fat little man with an oily smile.

For five months the two friends played from morning till night. Then one morning Pinocchio found that his ears had grown long and hairy. Candlewick had the same problem, and soon both boys were down on all fours, braying like donkeys. The coach driver had been collecting lazy boys for exactly this purpose, and he sold them at the market. Pinocchio was trained to dance in a circus until he fell and injured his leg. He was then sold to a man who wanted his skin to make a drum. The man threw him into the sea on a rope, but hungry fish ate the donkey away. When the rope came up, a wooden puppet was hanging on the end.

Pinocchio swam happily away until the terrible dogfish swallowed him in one gulp. In the darkness of its belly he noticed a faint light. There, at a small table, sat Geppetto, eating raw fish by the light of a candle. The old man had been swallowed two years before while he was searching the sea for his son. Since the dogfish slept with its mouth wide open, they crept quietly over its tongue. Pinocchio swam with his father on his back, and a friendly tuna carried them to the shore.

Pinocchio worked hard turning a water wheel to buy milk for his weak father. In the farmer's stable he found a worn-out donkey and recognized his old friend Candlewick. He stayed with the poor animal until it closed its eyes for the last time. When he heard that the Fairy was ill, he sent her every coin he had saved. That night she visited him in a dream and kissed him. In the morning he woke up as a real boy, with Geppetto healthy and smiling beside him. In the corner leaned the old wooden puppet, and Pinocchio looked at it with relief.

## Level C1

Long before it learned to walk, Pinocchio was nothing more than an ordinary log, the kind people toss on the stove without a second thought. It came into the workshop of an old carpenter who meant to turn it into a table leg. At the first blow of his axe, however, a thin voice begged for mercy, and at the touch of his plane it giggled as if tickled. Badly shaken, the carpenter was only too glad to give the troublesome log to his neighbor Geppetto.

Geppetto was a poor, quick-tempered old man whose only room had a fire painted on the wall instead of a real one. His plan was simple: he would carve a marvelous puppet, take it around the world, and earn his bread with its tricks. No sooner had he carved the eyes than they began to stare at him, and the nose kept stretching however often he cut it back. The mouth laughed at him before it was even finished.

Once he had legs, the puppet bolted through the door and raced down the street, leaving his maker panting behind him. A policeman caught him by the nose, which was conveniently long, but the crowd decided that the old man must be a tyrant at home. So it was Geppetto, not the runaway, who spent that night in prison. At home a large cricket, which claimed to have lived in that room for a hundred years, offered Pinocchio some advice he had no wish to hear. Boys who disobey their parents and flee from home, it warned, never come to any good. The puppet's answer was a wooden hammer, and after that the Talking Cricket said nothing more.

Chilled and hungry, he fell asleep with his feet propped on a pan of glowing coals, and by morning they had burned away to nothing. Geppetto, released at dawn, gave him the pears he had saved for his own breakfast, carved him new feet, and listened to a flood of promises. To help him keep them, the old man went out into the snow without his coat and came back with a spelling book. Pinocchio remembered this sacrifice for roughly as long as it took him to hear the sound of a drum. Within the hour the book had been exchanged for four pennies and a ticket to a puppet theater.

The wooden actors recognized their brother instantly, abandoned the play, and carried him in triumph across the stage. Their master, Fire-Eater, arrived like a thunderstorm, with a black beard that trailed across the boards behind him. Since his roast was not quite done and his firewood had run out, he sent for the new puppet to feed the flames. Yet this monster had a secret softness: pity did not make him weep but sneeze, and the tale of an old father shivering without his coat set him sneezing like a cannon. Before the evening was over, the puppet was walking home with five gold coins for Geppetto.

He met the fox and the cat at the first bend in the road, the one limping on three legs and the other feeling its way as though blind. Neither was as unfortunate as it appeared, and both noticed the glint of gold long before Pinocchio thought of hiding it. The fox spoke of a field in a town for fools, where a coin buried at night would by morning become a tree heavy with money. It was the sort of offer only a puppet with no experience of the world could believe, and Pinocchio believed every word. At an inn, the cat, complaining of a weak stomach, ate thirty-five fish, and the fox, on a strict diet, managed a hare and several chickens. Then, while the puppet slept, the two of them slipped away, kindly leaving him the bill.

Out on the dark road, two figures in black coal sacks leapt from the bushes, and their voices sounded oddly familiar. Pinocchio hid the coins under his tongue and ran until his wooden legs could carry him no further. The robbers caught him at last, tied him to a high branch of a great oak, and went off to wait for him to give up his gold. From a little white house nearby, a child with turquoise hair, in truth a Fairy more than a thousand years old, had watched it all. She sent a falcon to free him and a poodle dressed as a coachman to fetch him in her carriage.

Asked about his money, Pinocchio discovered how dangerous a simple question can be. He had lost the coins, he said, and his nose promptly grew two inches; he had swallowed them, he added, and it grew until he could not turn round. The Fairy laughed, explaining that some lies trip you up while others announce themselves in the middle of your face. She left him to reflect on this for half an hour before letting in a flock of woodpeckers, which tapped the nose back to a respectable length.

Even this lesson did not last, and the fox and the cat had little trouble steering him to their famous field. What he found after burying his coins there was a hole in the ground and a parrot laughing so hard it nearly fell off its branch. Outraged, he took his case to the local judge, an elderly ape with gold-rimmed glasses, who listened with sympathy and then sent him to prison for being robbed. Months later, when the emperor pardoned every criminal, the jailer released Pinocchio only after he swore he was a criminal as well.

Further misadventures followed, and from a cliff he watched Geppetto's tiny boat vanish among the waves as the old man sailed off in search of him. When at last he found the Fairy again, she had grown into a woman, old enough to be his mother. She agreed to be exactly that, on the condition that he went to school and told the truth. To everyone's surprise, including his own, he kept his word for nearly a year and finished as the best pupil in his class. As a reward, the Fairy announced that the next day he would stop being a puppet and become a real boy.

That very evening he ran into Candlewick, a thin, lazy boy named for his long, narrow body, who was waiting for a coach. It was bound for the Land of Toys, a country without schools or teachers, where every week contained six Thursdays and a Sunday. Pinocchio said no once, and then again, and the third time he found himself riding a donkey behind the coach. The driver, a little man as round and soft as a lump of butter, smiled sweetly at every boy who climbed aboard.

Five months passed in a whirl of games and idleness, and not a single book was opened in all that time. Then one morning Pinocchio put his hand to his head and found two ears as long as a pair of brushes. He ran to Candlewick, who had hidden the same ears under an enormous cotton cap, and their laughter turned, mid-breath, into braying. The little driver sold his harvest of donkeys at the market, which was, of course, the whole point of his kindness. Pinocchio danced in a circus until a fall left him lame, and he was sold to a man who wanted his skin for a drum. Lowered into the sea on a rope, he came up an hour later as a wooden puppet, for the fish had eaten the donkey away.

Laughing, he swam out into the waves and straight into the path of the terrible dogfish, a monster longer than a kilometer. Deep in its belly, where the air smelled of fish and darkness, a faint light was shining, and beside it, at a small table, sat Geppetto. The dogfish had swallowed him two years before, together with his boat, and his candles were almost gone. Because the old monster suffered from asthma and slept with its mouth open, father and son crept across its tongue and slipped into the night sea. With Geppetto clinging to his shoulders, Pinocchio swam until his strength was gone, and a friendly tuna carried them both to the beach.

On the road inland they passed the fox and the cat, now truly lame and blind, and Pinocchio walked on without giving them a penny. From that day the puppet worked, turning a farmer's water wheel for a glass of milk for his father. In the farmer's stable he found a worn-out donkey, knew at once that it was Candlewick, and held his friend's head until the end came. When a snail brought news that the Fairy lay ill and penniless, he sent her everything he had saved. That night she appeared in his dreams and forgave him. He woke in a bright room with new clothes on the chair, no longer made of wood, while Geppetto, healthy again, was already at work. Against the wall leaned an old puppet, its head hanging, and Pinocchio studied it with astonishment, very glad to be rid of it.
