# The Snow Queen

Summary: A brave girl travels across the frozen north to find her friend and bring him home.

## Level A1

Kai and Gerda lived in a big town. Their homes stood side by side. They had red roses by the window. The two children were best friends.

Far away, a bad troll made a mirror. Good things looked ugly in it. Then the mirror broke into tiny pieces. The pieces flew all over the world.

A small splinter flew into Kai's eye. Another piece went into his heart. Now his heart was cold like ice. "The roses are ugly!" he said. He laughed at Gerda and ran away.

In winter, Kai played in the snow. A big white sled came by. A tall lady in white sat in it. She was the Snow Queen. Kai tied his little sled to hers. They went far from the town. She kissed him, and he forgot Gerda.

Gerda cried for a long time. Then she put on her red shoes. She went out to look for Kai. A kind crow helped her. A prince and princess gave her boots. In a dark forest, robbers stopped her. Their daughter was wild but kind. She had an old reindeer. "Take Gerda to the Snow Queen," she said.

The reindeer ran north with Gerda. They came to Lapland and then to Finland. A wise woman smiled at Gerda. "Her heart is her power," she said.

The Snow Queen had a palace of ice. Kai sat there all alone. He was blue with cold. His cold hands moved flat pieces of ice. He wanted to make a word with them.

Gerda ran into the cold palace. She saw Kai and held him tight. Her hot tears fell on his chest. The ice in his heart melted. Then Kai cried too. His tears washed the splinter out. "Gerda!" he said. "Where are we?" The ice pieces danced and made the word.

Kai and Gerda went home together. The roses were red at the window. The two friends were big now. But in their hearts, they were still children.

## Level A2

Long ago, a wicked troll made a magic mirror. In this mirror, good and beautiful things looked small and ugly. One day it fell from the sky and broke. Millions of tiny pieces flew around the world.

In a big town, two children lived in two old houses. Their families grew roses in wooden boxes between the windows. The boy was Kai, and the girl was Gerda. They loved each other like brother and sister.

One summer afternoon, Kai suddenly cried out. "Something hit my heart, and something is in my eye!" Two tiny splinters of the mirror were in him. Soon his heart was as hard as ice. He broke the roses and laughed at Gerda's tears.

That winter, Kai went to the town square with his sled. He tied his sled to a big white sleigh. They raced out of the town, faster and faster. The driver was a tall, beautiful lady, all in white. It was the Snow Queen. She kissed Kai, and he forgot the cold. After a second kiss, he forgot Gerda too.

Kai did not come home, and nobody found him. In spring, Gerda put on her red shoes and left home. A little boat carried her down the river. It stopped near an old woman's beautiful garden. The woman knew some magic, and she wanted Gerda to stay. But Gerda saw a rose on the woman's hat. She remembered Kai and ran away.

A crow told her about a clever young prince. "Maybe the prince is Kai!" said Gerda. But the prince was not Kai, and Gerda cried. The kind prince and princess gave her boots and a golden carriage.

In a dark forest, robbers stopped the carriage. Their wild daughter wanted Gerda for a friend. That night, some birds spoke to Gerda. "We saw Kai in the Snow Queen's sleigh," they said. The robber girl had an old reindeer, and he knew the way. In the morning, she let them go.

The reindeer ran day and night. They stopped in Lapland and then in Finland. "Can you make Gerda very strong?" the reindeer asked a wise woman. The woman shook her head. "Her power is already in her heart," she said.

Gerda went on alone, without boots or gloves. The Snow Queen's palace had walls of snow. Inside, Kai sat alone on a lake of ice. He was blue with cold, but he did not feel it. He moved flat pieces of ice and tried to make a word. "Make the word 'forever', and you are free," the queen told him.

Gerda ran to Kai and put her arms around him. Her warm tears fell on his chest and went into his heart. The ice in his heart melted. Kai began to cry too, and his tears washed the splinter out. The pieces of ice danced with joy and made the word.

Kai and Gerda walked home together. At home, the roses were in flower at the window. Kai and Gerda were not small now. But they were still children in their hearts.

## Level B1

Long ago, there was a wicked troll who loved to cause trouble. He made a magic mirror. Everything good and beautiful looked ugly in it. The troll's students carried the mirror all over the world and laughed. At last, they flew up to the sky with it, but it slipped from their hands. It broke into millions of pieces, and the wind carried them everywhere. A person who got one in the eye saw only the bad side of things. A person who got one in the heart soon had a heart of ice.

In a big town, two poor children lived in houses that stood side by side. Their windows almost touched. Between them, the families grew roses in boxes. The boy was Kai and the girl was Gerda, and they loved each other dearly. In winter, Kai's grandmother told them about the Snow Queen, who flew with the snowflakes.

The next summer, the children were looking at a picture book when Kai cried out. "Something hit my heart, and something is in my eye!" Two tiny splinters of the mirror were inside him, in his eye and his heart. From that moment, Kai changed. He pulled up the roses and laughed at Gerda when she cried. He said that snowflakes were much better than real flowers.

That winter, Kai took his sled to the big square. A large white sleigh drove in, and Kai tied his sled to the back of it. The sleigh raced out through the town gate, faster and faster. At last, it stopped, and the driver stood up. She was a tall, shining lady, and her coat and hat were pure snow. It was the Snow Queen. Her kiss was colder than ice. After the second kiss, Kai forgot his home.

In spring, Gerda put on her new red shoes and went down to the river. She stood in a little boat, and the boat drifted away with her. It carried her to the garden of an old woman who knew some magic. The woman wanted to keep Gerda, so she made her roses disappear. But one day Gerda noticed a painted rose on the old woman's hat. She remembered Kai at once and ran out of the garden.

Gerda met a crow on the road. The crow told her about a clever young man who married the princess of that land. "Maybe it is Kai!" Gerda cried. At night, the crow took her up the back stairs of the palace. But the young man was not Kai, and Gerda began to cry. The prince and princess were kind. They gave her warm boots and a golden carriage.

The carriage was passing through a dark forest when robbers attacked it. They pulled Gerda out, but the robbers' wild daughter wanted her as a friend. That night, some birds told Gerda that they saw Kai in the Snow Queen's sleigh. "She was going to Lapland," said an old reindeer that stood near them. In the morning, the robber girl untied the reindeer and lifted Gerda onto his back. "Take her to Lapland, and look after her," she said.

In Lapland, an old woman wrote a message on a dry fish and sent them on. In Finland, the reindeer asked a wise woman for help. "Please make Gerda as strong as twelve men!" he said. The wise woman shook her head. "Look how people and animals help her," she said. "Her power comes from her pure, kind heart."

The reindeer left Gerda near the palace and ran back, crying. Gerda had no boots and no gloves, and huge snowflakes rushed at her. She prayed, and her warm breath became small angels who protected her. Inside the palace, Kai was alone, blue with cold, but he did not feel it. He was moving flat pieces of ice and trying to make a word. The queen told him, "Make the word 'forever', and you will be your own master." But he could never find the right pieces.

Gerda saw him and threw her arms around him. He sat still and stiff, and she began to cry. Her hot tears fell on his chest, went into his heart and melted the ice. Kai looked at her and burst into tears, and the splinter came out of his eye. "Gerda, where were you?" he asked. "And where am I?" The pieces of ice danced with joy and lay down in the shape of the word.

They walked out of the palace hand in hand. The reindeer was waiting for them. On the way home they met the robber girl, who was riding out into the world. When they came into their town, it was summer. They climbed the stairs to grandmother's room and saw that they were no longer small. But they were still children in their hearts, and the roses were blooming at the window.

## Level B2

There was once a wicked troll who made a mirror with a terrible power. Everything good or beautiful shrank to almost nothing in it. Everything ugly grew larger and worse. His pupils carried the mirror through every land and laughed at what it showed them. At last, they tried to fly up to heaven with it. But the mirror shook so hard that it slipped from their hands. It shattered into millions of pieces, and the wind blew them all over the world. Whoever got a splinter in the eye saw only what was wrong, and a splinter in the heart turned it to ice.

In a crowded town lived two poor children, Kai and Gerda, who loved each other like brother and sister. Their families rented attic rooms in houses whose roofs almost touched. Two little rose trees grew in boxes between their windows. On winter evenings Kai's grandmother told them about the Snow Queen. She flew through the streets at night and painted frost on the windows.

One summer day, as they were looking at a picture book, Kai suddenly cried out. Something had struck him in the heart, and something had flown into his eye. In fact two splinters of the troll's mirror were inside him. His heart was already turning to ice. He tore the roses from the box, made fun of his grandmother and laughed at Gerda's tears. Snowflakes, he said, were far more perfect than any real flower.

That winter he took his sled to the big square, where the boldest boys tied their sleds to farmers' carts. A large white sleigh drove in, and Kai quickly fastened his little sled to the back of it. The sleigh circled the square twice and then rushed out of the town gate. When it finally stopped, the driver stood up. She was a tall lady, and her fur coat and cap were pure snow. It was the Snow Queen, and her kiss on his forehead was colder than ice. With a second kiss he forgot Gerda, his grandmother and everyone at home.

People said that Kai had drowned in the river, but in spring the sunshine told Gerda he was alive. She put on her new red shoes and offered them to the river, and a drifting boat carried her away. It brought her to the garden of an old woman who knew a little magic. As the woman combed her hair with a golden comb, Gerda slowly forgot Kai. To keep it that way, the old woman made every rose bush sink into the earth. But she forgot the roses painted on her own hat, and when Gerda saw them, she remembered everything. She ran out of the garden and found that summer was over and autumn had come.

Resting in the snow, she met a crow. He told her about a clever young man who had married the princess. The princess had promised to marry only a man who could talk as well as she could. "That must be Kai!" cried Gerda. At night a tame crow from the palace led her up the back stairs. Gerda held up her lamp, but the sleeping young man was not Kai, and she burst into tears. The prince and princess were kind, and in the morning they gave her boots, a muff and a golden coach.

In a dark forest the golden coach shone like a flame, and robbers soon attacked it. They dragged Gerda out, and an old robber woman was reaching for her knife when her wild daughter bit her ear. "She shall play with me," said the robber girl. That night wood pigeons told Gerda that they had seen Kai in the Snow Queen's sleigh, probably on its way to Lapland. The robber girl's old reindeer, who was tied to the wall, said that Lapland was his home.

In the morning, while her mother was asleep, the robber girl untied the reindeer and lifted Gerda onto his back. She gave her a pair of huge mittens, two loaves and a ham, and told the reindeer to look after her. The reindeer ran as fast as he could, through forests and over marshes, under the flashing northern lights.

In Lapland an old woman wrote a message on a dried fish and sent them to a wise woman in Finland. The reindeer begged this woman to make Gerda as strong as twelve men. "I cannot give her more power than she already has," the wise woman replied. "Don't you see how people and animals help her, and how far she has walked in bare feet?" "Her strength is in her heart, because she is a good and innocent child." Kai, she explained, thought he was happy, but only because of the splinters in his eye and heart.

The reindeer left Gerda by a bush with red berries and ran back with tears on his face. Without boots or mittens, she faced an army of living snowflakes that raced toward her across the ground. She began to pray, and her breath turned into little angels with helmets and spears. They broke the snowflakes into pieces, warmed her hands and feet, and she walked on without fear.

The palace had walls of drifting snow and windows of cutting wind. Its hundred halls were empty and freezing. In the largest hall lay a frozen lake, cracked into a thousand pieces. The Snow Queen called it the Mirror of Reason. Kai sat there, blue with cold, but he did not notice it because his heart was a lump of ice. He was moving flat pieces of ice into patterns, trying to spell a word. The queen had promised that if he spelled "Eternity", he would be his own master and own the whole world. Then she had flown away to the warm countries, leaving him alone.

At that moment Gerda came through the great gate and ran to Kai. "Kai, dear Kai, I have found you at last!" she cried, holding him tight. But he sat still and cold, so she wept. Her hot tears reached his heart and melted the ice. When she sang their old rose song, Kai burst into tears, and the splinter was washed out of his eye. "Gerda!" he cried. "Where have you been, and where have I been?" The pieces of ice danced for joy. When they lay down, they spelled the very word the queen had asked for.

Hand in hand they walked out of the palace, and the reindeer was waiting to carry them south. Near the forest they met the robber girl, riding out into the world. When they reached home, they climbed the stairs to grandmother's room and noticed that they had grown up. The roses were blooming at the window, and it was summer, but in their hearts they were still children.

## Level C1

There was once a wicked troll who made a mirror in which everything good or lovely shrank to nothing, while everything mean grew larger. His pupils carried it through every land, and at last they resolved to fly up and mock the angels themselves. The higher they rose, the more the mirror shook with laughter, until it slipped from their fingers and shattered into hundreds of millions of fragments, which the wind scattered across the world. Whoever caught one in the eye saw only what was crooked, and a splinter in the heart turned it into a lump of ice.

In a crowded town lived two poor children, Kai and Gerda, who loved each other as dearly as any brother and sister. Their families rented attic rooms in houses whose roofs almost touched, and in boxes along the gutter between them grew two little rose trees. On winter evenings Kai's grandmother told them about the Snow Queen, who flew through the streets at night and breathed flowers of frost onto the windows. Once, through a hole melted in the frosted glass, Kai saw a woman of glittering ice beckon to him.

The following summer, as the two of them sat over a picture book, Kai cried out that something had stabbed his heart and flown into his eye. Two splinters from the troll's mirror had found him, and before long his heart would be no warmer than a lump of ice. "Why are you crying?" he snapped at Gerda. He tore the blossoms from the rose tree, mimicked his grandmother behind her back, and insisted that snowflakes under a glass were finer than any real flower.

That winter he carried his sled to the great square, where the boldest boys liked to hitch rides behind the farmers' carts. A large white sleigh drove in, its driver wrapped in shaggy white fur, and Kai quickly tied his little sled behind it. It circled the square twice and swept out through the town gate, into snow so thick that he could not see his own hand. When at last it halted, the driver rose, a tall and dazzling lady whose fur and cap were nothing but snow, and Kai knew her at once. It was the Snow Queen, and her kiss on his forehead sent a cold sharper than ice straight down to his half-frozen heart. A second kiss made him forget Gerda, his grandmother and everyone at home, and now she seemed to him perfect.

The whole street believed Kai had drowned in the river, but in spring the sunshine and the swallows told Gerda he was alive. Early one morning she offered her new red shoes to the river in exchange for her friend, and a drifting boat carried her away. It brought her to the cherry orchard of an old woman in a broad sun hat, no wicked witch but a lonely one who longed for a little girl. As she combed Gerda's curls with a golden comb, the child slowly forgot Kai, and to keep it so, the woman made every rose bush sink into the earth. She forgot, however, the roses painted on her own hat, and the sight of them brought everything back. Where Gerda's tears fell, a bush rose again in full bloom and assured her that Kai was not among the dead. She ran out barefoot and found that the summer was long gone.

Resting in the snow, she met a crow who had heard of a clever young man with squeaking boots who had won the hand of a princess. The princess, so clever that she had read every newspaper in the world and forgotten them again, had wanted a husband who could talk well. That night a tame crow led Gerda up the back stairs of the palace, while dreams of horses and huntsmen rushed past like shadows on the walls. Gerda held up her lamp to the sleeping young man, but when he turned, it was not Kai at all, and she burst into tears. Far from being angry, the prince and princess heard her story and sent her on her way in a coach of pure gold.

The coach glittered like a torch in the dark forest, drawing robbers to it as surely as a candle draws moths. They dragged Gerda out, and an old robber woman had already drawn her knife when her own wild daughter bit her on the ear. "She shall play with me and sleep in my bed," the robber girl announced, and nobody dared to argue. Wood pigeons in the rafters cooed that they had seen Kai in the Snow Queen's sleigh, heading, they believed, for Lapland. An old reindeer tied to the wall sighed that Lapland was his true home, a glorious country of shining snow where one could run free.

Next morning the robber girl untied the reindeer and lifted Gerda onto his back. She pushed her mother's huge mittens onto Gerda's hands, added two loaves and a ham, and told the reindeer to run his fastest. Away he leaped, through forests and over marshes, while the northern lights flickered red overhead like a fire in the sky.

In Lapland an old woman, who had no paper, scribbled a few words on a dried cod and sent them on to a wiser woman in Finland. The reindeer begged this second woman for a drink that would give Gerda the strength of twelve men. She laid fresh ice on his head and explained that no power she could grant was greater than the one Gerda already had. "Can't you see how men and animals serve her, and how far she has come on her bare feet?" she asked. Her strength lay in her innocent heart, the woman said, and Kai believed himself perfectly happy only because of the splinters in his eye and his heart.

The reindeer set Gerda down by a bush heavy with red berries and ran back with large tears rolling down his face. Without boots or mittens, she stood in that terrible country while an army of snowflakes came racing along the ground, shaped like hideous porcupines and knotted snakes. Gerda began to pray, and her breath came out like smoke, thickening into little bright angels with helmets and spears. They struck the snowflakes into a hundred pieces, and she walked on toward the palace without fear.

The palace had walls of drifting snow and windows of cutting wind, and its hundred halls, lit by the northern lights, were vast, empty and bitterly cold. In the largest of them lay a frozen lake, cracked into a thousand identical pieces, which the Snow Queen called the Mirror of Reason. There sat Kai, blue and almost black with cold, though he did not notice it, since her kisses had taken away his shivering. He was arranging sharp, flat pieces of ice in what he called the game of reason, and every pattern seemed to him splendid and important. She had promised that if he could spell the word Eternity, he would be his own master, and she would give him the whole world and a new pair of skates. Then she flew off to look into the volcanoes of the warm countries, leaving him alone.

It was at this moment that Gerda stepped through the great gate, and the biting winds dropped as though they had fallen asleep. She flew to Kai and held him tight, crying out, "Kai, dear little Kai, I have found you at last!" He sat perfectly still, so she wept hot tears that soaked through to his heart, thawed the lump of ice and consumed the splinter. When she sang the old rose song from their window boxes, he burst into tears and wept until the second splinter washed out of his eye. "Gerda, dear Gerda, where have you been all this time, and where have I been?" he cried, as if waking from a long sleep. Even the pieces of ice danced for joy, and when they lay down again, they spelled exactly the word the Snow Queen had demanded. So Kai had become his own master after all, though the whole world and the new skates no longer interested him in the least.

Hand in hand they walked out of the palace. Wherever they went, the winds grew calm, and the sun broke through. The reindeer carried them home past the women of Finland and Lapland, and near the forest they met the robber girl, riding out to see the world. She looked Kai up and down and told him frankly that she wondered whether he deserved someone running to the end of the world for him.

Spring came with them as they walked, and the church bells were ringing when they recognized the towers of their own town. Only as they passed through grandmother's low doorway did they notice that they had become grown-up people. The roses were blooming in at the open window, grandmother sat reading in the sunshine, and the cold, empty splendor of the Snow Queen had faded like a heavy dream. So the two of them sat there, grown up and yet children at heart, and it was summer, warm and blessed summer.
