# Robin Hood and the Golden Arrow

Summary: The sheriff sets a clever trap with a golden arrow, but Robin Hood has a plan too.

## Level A1

Robin Hood lived in the forest of Sherwood. He was the best archer in the land. His friends lived there with him. Little John was his best friend. Robin and his friends always wore green.

The sheriff of Nottingham hated Robin. He wanted to catch him. So he made a clever plan. He called for a big shooting match. The prize was an arrow of gold. "Robin can't miss a match," the sheriff said.

Robin heard about the match. "I want that golden arrow," he said. "It is a trap," said Little John. Robin only laughed. "Then I must look different," Robin said. He put on old, torn clothes. He put a patch over one eye. Now he looked like a poor beggar.

Many archers came to Nottingham. The sheriff sat on a high seat. He looked for men in green. But he did not see Robin. He saw only a poor one-eyed man.

The match began. At the end, only three archers remained. Gilbert shot first. His arrow went near the middle. Adam shot next, and he did well too. Then the poor man stepped up. He pulled the string and let go. His arrow hit the very middle! The people shouted and cheered.

The sheriff gave him the golden arrow. "You shoot well," the sheriff said. "Come and work for me," he added. "No, I work for nobody," said the man. Then he walked away.

Back in Sherwood, Robin took off the patch. He was happy, but a little sad too. "The sheriff doesn't know the truth," he said. Little John smiled. "Let's tell him," he said.

That night the sheriff sat at dinner. Suddenly an arrow flew in through the window. A small letter was on it. "Thanks for the gold arrow! Robin Hood." The sheriff jumped up, red and angry. In Sherwood, Robin and his friends sang.

## Level A2

Robin Hood lived in the forest of Sherwood with his merry men. Nobody in the land could shoot a bow better than Robin. He took money from greedy rich men and gave it away.

The sheriff of Nottingham hated Robin more than anyone. He tried to catch him many times, but he always failed. At last, he sat in his big house and thought hard. "Robin is proud of his bow," he said to himself. "A shooting match can bring him out of the forest," he thought. So he sent men to every town with the news. He planned a great match at Nottingham. The prize was a shining arrow of gold.

The news soon reached Sherwood. Robin jumped up. "Get ready, lads," he said. "Let's win that arrow," he added. But one of his men shook his head. "Be careful," he said. "The match is a trap, master," he went on. "You are right," said Robin. "But I don't want people to call us cowards," he went on. So they decided to go in disguise.

Robin's men always wore green, so this time they wore other colors. Some dressed like farmers, and some dressed like beggars. Robin chose old red rags full of holes. He wore a patch over one eye and had a dirty face. "Nobody can tell it is you now!" laughed Little John.

On the day of the match, Nottingham was full of people. The sheriff sat on a high seat with his fine friends. "Where are the men in green?" he asked his guards. "We can't see them, sir," they said. The sheriff frowned, and then the match began.

The archers shot again and again. In the end, only three stayed in the match. Gilbert was a famous archer. Adam of the Dell was very good too. The third was the poor stranger with one eye. Gilbert shot, and his arrow landed close to the middle. Adam's arrow landed close too, but not as close. Then the stranger drew his bow. His arrow flew straight and hit the very center. The crowd shouted with joy.

The sheriff gave the golden arrow to the stranger. "What is your name, good archer?" he asked. "I am only a poor traveler," the stranger said. "Work for me, and I can pay you well," said the sheriff. "No, thank you," said the stranger. "I am my own master," he added. He walked away into the crowd.

That evening, Robin sat with his men in Sherwood. He looked at the golden arrow. "I am glad, but I am sad too," he said. "The sheriff thinks a beggar won his prize," he explained. Little John laughed. "Leave it to me," he said.

The sheriff sat at dinner in his big hall. Suddenly an arrow flew in through the open window. It landed on the table in front of him. A small letter hung from it. It said thank you for the golden arrow. At the bottom was the name "Robin Hood." The sheriff's face turned red, and he shouted with anger. Far away, Little John put down his bow and smiled.

## Level B1

In the great forest of Sherwood lived Robin Hood and his band of merry men. They wore green clothes that matched the leaves, and they lived under the open sky. Robin was the finest archer in the land, and everybody knew his name. He took gold from rich lords who cheated the poor. Then he gave it to families in need.

The sheriff of Nottingham tried many times to catch Robin, but he always failed. Every time, Robin escaped and laughed at him from the trees. One evening the sheriff was walking up and down his hall, thinking hard. Suddenly he stopped and smiled. "Robin Hood is proud of his bow," he said. "He will never miss a chance to show his skill," he added. The next morning, riders carried his message to every town and village. The sheriff was holding a great shooting match at Nottingham. The best archer was going to win an arrow of pure gold.

When the news reached Sherwood, Robin was delighted. "Get your bows ready, lads, and we will bring that golden arrow home," he cried. But one of his men, who was just back from town, looked worried. "Listen, master," he said, "everyone in town says the match is a trap." "The sheriff's guards will be waiting for you," he warned. Robin was quiet for a moment. "You are wise, but I won't let people call me afraid," Robin said at last. "So we will go, but in a way the sheriff won't expect," he added.

So the merry men left their green clothes in the forest. Some of them dressed as farmers, and others dressed as beggars or travelling monks. Robin put on a coat of old red rags that was full of holes. He tied a patch over one eye and rubbed dirt on his face. When he stepped out, Little John and Will Scarlet could hardly stop laughing. "Nobody will know you now," said Will Scarlet. "You look like the poorest man in the country," he added.

On the day of the match, the town was full of color and noise. People sat on long wooden seats around a wide green field. At one end stood the targets, and the sheriff sat high above everyone. He was searching the crowd with sharp eyes. "Do you see any men in green?" he asked his guard. "No, sir, not one," the guard answered. "Then the coward has stayed at home," said the sheriff, and he looked disappointed.

The trumpets sounded, and the archers began to shoot. One by one, the weaker archers dropped out. Soon only three archers remained on the field. The first was Gilbert, a tall man and one of the best archers in the country. The second was Adam of the Dell, who also had a great name. The third was the ragged stranger with the patch over his eye.

Gilbert shot first, and his arrow landed very near the center. The crowd cheered loudly for him. Next came Adam, and his arrow landed close, but not as close as Gilbert's. Then the stranger stepped forward and calmly drew his bow. For a moment nobody breathed. His arrow flew across the field and hit the exact center of the target. The people jumped to their feet and shouted until they were hoarse.

The sheriff called the stranger to his seat and gave him the golden arrow. "You are a fine archer," he said. "Come and serve me, and I will pay you well," he went on. The stranger shook his head. "No man in this land will ever be my master," he answered. Then he turned away, and the crowd soon swallowed him.

That night, back under the trees of Sherwood, Robin threw away his patch. His men were cheering, but Robin was not fully happy. "The sheriff thinks that a beggar won his prize," he said. "I want him to know the truth," he added. Little John picked up his bow and smiled. "Leave that to me, master," he said.

At that moment the sheriff was sitting at a great dinner in his hall. He was still angry about the match. Suddenly an arrow came through the window and struck the table in front of him. It carried a small piece of paper. The sheriff opened it with shaking hands. In a few short lines, Robin Hood thanked him for the golden arrow. The sheriff's face went purple, and he roared with anger. Far away in Sherwood, Little John walked back to his friends, and they laughed until morning.

## Level B2

Deep in the forest of Sherwood, where the sunlight fell through the oaks in pieces, lived Robin Hood and his outlaws. They dressed in green, ate the king's deer, and sang around their fires at night. Robin was the finest archer anyone had ever seen. He used his skill to take from greedy lords and give to people who had nothing. To the poor he was a hero, but to the powerful he was a thorn in the side.

No one hated him more than the sheriff of Nottingham. Again and again the sheriff had sent men into the forest. Again and again they had come back empty-handed, or worse, laughed at. At last, he rode all the way to the king to ask for help. The king, however, was in no mood to listen. "What use is a sheriff who cannot catch one man in a wood?" he said coldly. "Deal with the outlaw yourself, or I will find someone who can," the king added. The sheriff rode home in a black temper, and for days he could think of nothing else.

Then one night an idea came to him. "Robin Hood is as proud as a peacock," he thought. "If I hold a shooting match, he will not be able to stay away," he told himself. The very next morning, messengers set out for every market and village. A great match would be held at Nottingham, they announced, and the winner would receive an arrow of solid gold.

When the news reached Sherwood, Robin leapt to his feet. "Now that is a prize worth winning!" he cried. "Get your bows ready, lads, for we are going to Nottingham," he called to his men. One of his men, however, had been in town the day before, and he had heard some worrying talk. "Master, the whole town is saying the match is a trap," he warned. "The sheriff wants nothing more than to see you walk through his gates," he added. Robin listened carefully and then smiled. "You have done well to tell me," Robin said. "But if we stay at home, people will call Robin Hood a coward, and that I will never allow," he went on. "We will go, but not as the sheriff expects," he decided.

So the green coats were left in the forest. Some of the men dressed as farmers, others as travelling tinkers, and a few as monks or beggars. They planned to walk into town in twos and threes so that nobody would notice them. Robin himself put on a tattered red coat, patched and torn in a dozen places. He tied a patch over one eye, rubbed dirt into his beard, and bent his back like an old man. Little John looked at him and roared with laughter. "If your own mother saw you now, she would give you a penny and walk on," he said.

On the morning of the match, Nottingham was alive with noise and color. Crowds filled the benches around a long green field, and at the far end stood the targets. The sheriff sat on a raised platform, dressed in his finest clothes, but his eyes were busy. He searched every face in the crowd, looking for a flash of green. "Do you see any of them?" he whispered to his captain. "Not one, my lord," the captain replied. "Then the coward is hiding in his forest," the sheriff muttered, and he could not hide his disappointment.

The trumpets sounded, and the archers began. Round after round, the weaker men dropped out, until only ten remained, and then only three. The first was Gilbert, whose skill was famous across the whole country. The second was Adam of the Dell, another archer with a great name. The third was the ragged stranger with one eye, whom nobody knew.

Gilbert went first, and his arrow struck just beside the center. A great cheer rose from the crowd. Adam shot next, and his arrow landed close, though not quite as close as Gilbert's. Finally, the stranger stepped forward, took his time, and drew the string back to his ear. The whole field fell silent. His arrow flew like a bird and buried itself in the exact center of the target. For a moment nobody made a sound, and then the crowd exploded with cheers.

The sheriff called the stranger to the platform and handed him the golden arrow. "I have never seen better shooting," he admitted. "Join my service, and I will pay you more than any man in Nottingham earns," he went on. The stranger bowed, but only a little. "Thank you, my lord, but no man alive will ever be my master," he said. Before the sheriff could reply, he had slipped back into the crowd and vanished.

That evening, under the great oak in Sherwood, Robin pulled off his patch and held up the prize. His men cheered, but Robin's smile soon faded. "The sheriff believes some poor beggar has taken his arrow," he said. "It annoys me that he will never know the truth," Robin added. Little John picked up his bow and a scrap of paper. "Then let us make sure he knows," he said.

That same night, the sheriff was sitting at a grand feast in his hall, still sour about the match. Suddenly something flew in through the open window and struck the table in front of him with a sharp crack. It was an arrow, and a small rolled letter was tied to its shaft. With trembling fingers, the sheriff unrolled it and read a short, cheerful rhyme. It thanked him kindly for his golden gift and was signed by Robin Hood of Sherwood. The sheriff's face turned from red to purple, and he shouted until the dishes shook. Out in the darkness, Little John lowered his bow and slipped quietly back into the trees.

## Level C1

In the days when the forest of Sherwood still stretched for miles beneath its ancient oaks, there lived within it a band of outlaws whose leader was Robin Hood. They wore green so that the leaves might hide them, they feasted on venison that belonged, strictly speaking, to the king, and they spent their evenings singing beside the fire. Robin himself was held to be the finest archer in all the land, a man who could split a willow wand at a distance most marksmen would not even attempt. What he took from fat abbots and greedy lords he gave away to widows and hungry farmers, which made him beloved in every cottage and loathed in every castle.

Of all those who loathed him, none did so with greater energy than the sheriff of Nottingham. Time after time he had sent armed men into the greenwood, and time after time they had returned with nothing to show but torn clothes and bruised pride. At last, swallowing his own dignity, he rode all the way to the king's court to beg for help. The king, who had troubles enough of his own, listened with growing impatience. "Are you a sheriff or a frightened shepherd?" he demanded at last. "Go home and deal with this outlaw yourself, or I shall find a man who can," he added.

The sheriff rode home through the rain with his cheeks burning, turning the insult over and over in his mind. For several days he barely ate, and his servants learned to walk softly past his door. Then, one evening, as he sat glaring into the fire, a thought struck him so sweetly that he laughed aloud. "The fellow is vain," he murmured. "He cannot bear for anyone to be called a better archer than himself, and that vanity shall be the bait in my trap," he added, smiling into the flames. Within the week, heralds were riding to every market town in the shire, crying out that a grand shooting match would be held at Nottingham. The prize, they announced, was to be an arrow whose shaft was silver and whose head was beaten gold.

News travels quickly in the greenwood, and it was not long before it reached Robin under his great oak. His eyes lit up at once. "Now there is a prize fit for a merry man," he declared, reaching for his bow. "Make ready, lads, for we go to Nottingham, and I mean to bring that arrow home," he told them. One of the band, however, a young man who had spent the previous day drinking at an inn in town, held up his hand. "Master, think twice," he said, "for at the inn they talk of nothing else, and every man of them says the match is a snare laid for you alone." "The sheriff has ordered his guards to watch for anyone dressed in green," he added. Robin heard him out in silence, stroking his beard. "You were right to warn me, and I thank you," Robin said at length. "Yet if we skulk among the trees, the country will say a sheriff frightened off Robin Hood, and that song I will not have sung," he went on. "We shall go, then, but as the sheriff least expects," he concluded.

And so the green coats were folded away, and the outlaws became, for a day, quite other men. Some dressed as tinkers with pots clanking at their backs, some as plain farmers, and one or two as barefoot friars with their hoods drawn low. They agreed to enter the town by different roads and in small groups, so that no watchful eye might count them. As for Robin, he chose a coat of faded scarlet rags, so patched and torn that it seemed held together more by habit than by thread. Over one eye he tied a dirty patch, his beard he smeared with mud, and he practiced a stooping walk until he looked twenty years older. Will Scarlet took one look at him and had to sit down on a log to laugh. "Were you to beg at your own door, your own men would toss you a crust and send you on your way," he gasped.

The morning of the match dawned bright, and by mid-morning Nottingham was a sea of noise and color. Benches had been raised along both sides of a broad green meadow, and at its far end stood the targets, painted in bright rings. Above everything, on a raised stand hung with cloth, sat the sheriff in his richest robes, with his lady at his side. Yet he paid little attention to the crowd's merriment, for his eyes were busy elsewhere. Row by row he searched the benches, peering at every face, looking for the flash of green that would tell him his trap had worked. "Well?" he asked his captain in a low voice. "Not a single one of them, my lord," the captain admitted. "Then the braggart has lost his nerve," said the sheriff, and he settled back in his seat, more disappointed than he cared to show.

At the sound of the trumpets the contest began. One after another the archers stepped up, and one after another the weaker hands fell away, until only ten remained. From ten the number fell to three, and the whole crowd leaned forward to see them. There was Gilbert, a lean, cool-headed man whose arrows were spoken of in every county, and there was Adam of the Dell, hardly less famous. And there, to everybody's amusement, was the ragged one-eyed beggar, whose name no one could tell.

Gilbert shot first, and his arrow struck so near the center that a hand's breadth could not have fitted between them. The crowd roared its approval. Adam followed, and his arrow also found the inner ring, though it stood a shade farther out than Gilbert's. Then the beggar shuffled forward, and a ripple of laughter ran along the benches. It did not last. Straightening his bent back, he drew the string to his ear in one easy movement, held it for a heartbeat, and let fly. The arrow hissed across the meadow and lodged in the very center of the target, so true that it seemed to have grown there. For a moment the field was utterly silent; then the noise that broke out was enough to send the rooks screaming from the trees.

The sheriff, much impressed, beckoned the stranger up to the stand and placed the golden arrow in his hands. "I have never seen a finer shot in all my years," he said, almost warmly. "Leave your rags and enter my service, and you shall have a good coat, good meat and good wages," he went on. The beggar bowed, not very low. "I thank you, my lord, but I was born my own master, and I mean to die so," he said. Before the sheriff could decide whether to be offended, the man had melted back into the crowd, and no one could afterwards say which way he had gone.

That night, beneath the great oak in Sherwood, Robin tore off his patch and held up the arrow so that it glittered in the firelight. His men cheered until the woods rang, yet before long their leader fell quiet. "It is a fine prize," he said, turning it in his fingers, "but half the pleasure is gone." "The sheriff sits in his hall believing some wandering beggar has robbed him, and he will never know it was I," Robin added ruefully. Little John, who had been sitting a little apart, rose and took up his bow. "Give me a scrap of parchment and leave the rest to me," he said.

At that very hour the sheriff was presiding over a great feast in his hall, picking at his meat with little appetite. Without warning, something flashed through the open window and struck quivering into the table, an inch from his plate. It was a gray goose arrow, and a strip of parchment was tied around its shaft. Pale and then crimson, the sheriff unrolled it and read a short verse, wishing him every blessing for having handed his golden prize to Robin Hood of merry Sherwood. His roar of fury could be heard in the street, and more than one of his guests had to bite a napkin to keep from laughing. Far off in the darkness, Little John unstrung his bow, whistled a tune under his breath, and strolled home through the trees to his friends.
