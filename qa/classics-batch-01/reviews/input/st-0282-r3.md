# Rumpelstiltskin

Summary: A miller's daughter must spin straw into gold, and a strange little man offers to help.

## Level A1

A poor miller had a beautiful daughter. One day, he told the king a lie. "My girl can spin straw into gold!" "Bring her to me," said the king.

The king took her to a room. It was full of straw. "Make it gold by morning," he said. The girl cried.

Then a tiny man came in. "Why are you sad?" he asked. "I can't make gold," she said. "What do I get?" asked the little man. She gave him her necklace. The little man sat at the spinning wheel. By morning, all the straw was gold.

The king wanted more gold. The next night, the little man came again. This time, she gave him her ring. Again, all the straw turned to gold. Next, the king showed her a huge room. "Spin it, and be my queen!" he said.

The little man came for the third time. "I have nothing more," said the girl. "Then give me your first child," he said. Sadly, she said yes.

The king married the girl. A year later, the queen had a baby. Then one night, the little man came back. "Give me the child now!" he said. The queen cried and cried. The little man felt sorry. "Find my name in three days," he said. "Then you can keep the baby."

The queen sent a helper to find names. The next day, the little man came. "Is it Caspar? Is it Melchior?" she asked. "No!" laughed the little man. On the second day, she tried new names. "Is it Ribsofbeef? Or Muttonchops?"

On the third day, the helper came back. "I saw a little house," said the helper. "A tiny man danced around a fire there." "He sang his name in his song." "His name is Rumpelstiltskin!"

That evening, the little man came again. "Is it Rumpelstiltskin?" said the queen. The little man went red. "Somebody told you!" he shouted. Then he ran away. Nobody saw him again.

## Level A2

There once was a poor miller with a beautiful daughter. One day he met the king and wanted to seem important. "My daughter can spin straw into gold!" he said. The king loved gold more than anything. "Bring her to my castle tomorrow," he said.

The king led the girl to a little room full of straw. "Spin all this into gold before morning," he said. Then he locked the door. The poor girl had no idea how to make gold. She sat down in the straw and cried.

Suddenly the door opened, and a tiny man walked in. "Why are you crying?" he asked. "I must spin this straw into gold, but I can't," she said. "What can you give me for my help?" asked the little man. "You can have my necklace," said the girl. The little man sat down at the spinning wheel. He worked all night, and by morning the straw was gold.

The king's eyes shone, but he wanted more gold. The next night he took her to a bigger room. Again the little man came, and she gave him her ring. By sunrise the whole room was bright with gold. On the third night the king took her to the biggest room. "Spin all this, and you can be my wife," he said.

The little man came for the third time. "I have nothing left," the girl said sadly. "Then promise me your first child," he said. She did not know what else to do, so she promised.

The king kept his word, and the girl became queen. A year later she had a lovely baby. She was very happy, and she forgot the little man. But one night he stood in her room again. "Now give me the child," he said. "You promised." The queen offered him all the gold in the kingdom. "No. A living child means more to me," he said. She cried and cried, and at last he felt sorry for her. "I give you three days," he said. "Find my name, and you can keep your child."

The queen sent a messenger across the country to collect names. The next day she tried Caspar, Melchior, Balthazar and many more. "No, it is not my name," said the little man each time. On the second day she tried strange names. "Are you Ribsofbeef? Or Muttonchops? Or Lacelegs?" "Wrong again!" he laughed.

On the third day the messenger came back. "I saw a small house high on a mountain," he said. "A fire burned in front of it." "A funny little man hopped around it on one leg." "He sang a song about the queen's baby." "In his song, he said his own name: Rumpelstiltskin!"

Later that day the little man came in. "Well, my queen, what is my name?" he asked. "Is your name Rumpelstiltskin?" asked the queen. The little man's face turned red with anger. "The devil told you! The devil told you!" he screamed. He stamped his foot hard on the floor. Then he ran out of the castle in a rage. Nobody ever saw him again.

## Level B1

There was once a poor miller who lived by a river with his beautiful daughter. One day the king rode past the mill and stopped to talk with him. Wanting to impress him, the miller said the first silly thing that came into his head. "My daughter is so clever that she can spin straw into gold." The king, who loved gold more than anything, was suddenly very interested. "Bring her to my castle tomorrow, and we will see," he said.

The next day the king led the girl into a small room full of straw. A spinning wheel was standing in the middle. "All this straw must be gold by morning," said the king. "Fail, and you will be in serious trouble." He locked the door behind him and left her alone. She had no idea how to do such a thing, so she began to cry.

Just then the door opened, and a tiny man stepped into the room. "Good evening, miller's daughter," he said. "Why are you crying so hard?" "I must spin this straw into gold, and I don't know how," she said. "What will you give me for doing it?" he asked. The girl offered him her necklace. The little man sat down at the wheel and began to spin. With every turn, bright gold thread came out instead of straw. By morning the whole room was shining with gold.

When the king came in at sunrise, he could hardly believe his eyes. But the gold only made him hungry for more. That evening he took the girl to a larger room, which held even more straw. Once again she cried, and once again the door opened. This time she gave the little man the ring from her finger. He spun until the sun came up, and every piece of straw turned to gold.

The king was delighted, but he still was not satisfied. On the third night he led her to the biggest room in the castle. "Spin all of this tonight," he said, "and tomorrow you will become my queen." As soon as the king was gone, the little man appeared again. "What will you give me now?" he asked. "I have nothing left to give," said the girl. "Then promise me your first child when you are queen," he said. The girl felt cold all over, but she saw no other way, so she promised. At once the little man spun all the straw in the last room into gold.

The king found everything as he wished, and he married the miller's daughter. A year later the young queen had a beautiful baby. In all her happiness, she forgot the little man completely. Then one evening he suddenly appeared in her room. "Now give me what you promised," he said.

The queen was terrified, and she offered him all the treasures of the kingdom instead. "No," said the little man. "Something living means more to me than gold." The queen cried so bitterly that the little man felt sorry for her. "Very well," he said. "I will give you three days." "Tell me my name before then, and you can keep your child."

The queen lay awake all night, thinking of every name that she could remember. She also sent a messenger across the land to collect unusual names. The next day she began with Caspar, then Melchior, then Balthazar. She went through her whole list, but after each name he shook his head. On the second day she tried the strangest names that the messenger brought back. "Is your name Ribsofbeef?" she asked. "Or Muttonchops, or Lacelegs?" "No, that is not my name," the little man answered each time, laughing.

On the third day the messenger returned with a strange story. "I was walking over a high mountain near a forest when I saw a tiny house." "A fire was burning in front of it, and a little man was jumping around it." "He was hopping on one leg and singing at the top of his voice." Then the messenger sang the little man's song for the queen. "Tonight I dance beside my fire, and soon the queen's child will be mine!" "Nobody can guess my name, for I am Rumpelstiltskin!" You can imagine how happy the queen was when she heard that name.

Later that day the little man walked in, looking very pleased with himself. "Well, my queen, what is my name?" he asked. First the queen pretended to guess a few ordinary names. "No, no, no!" he cried each time, rubbing his hands together. "Then perhaps your name is Rumpelstiltskin?" she said slowly. "The devil told you that! The devil told you that!" he shouted. He stamped his foot so hard that the floor shook. Then he ran out of the castle in a terrible rage. From that day on, nobody ever saw him again.

## Level B2

In a valley beside a fast little river there lived a miller who was poor in everything except words. He had a daughter, and she was the most beautiful girl for miles around. One afternoon the king happened to stop at the mill, and the miller, eager to seem important, began to boast. "My daughter is not only lovely," he said, "she can also spin straw into pure gold." It was a foolish thing to say, but the king, who loved gold above all things, took him at his word. "Now that is a talent worth seeing," he replied. "If she is as clever as you say, bring her to my castle tomorrow."

The next morning the miller's daughter was led into a small room in the castle, piled high with straw. In the middle stood a spinning wheel, and next to it a reel for winding the thread. "Get to work," said the king. "If all this straw has not become gold by sunrise, things will go very badly for you." Then he locked the door himself and carried the key away. The poor girl sat among the straw and stared at the wheel. She had not the faintest idea how straw could become gold. The longer she thought about it, the more frightened she grew, until at last she burst into tears.

All at once the door swung open, and in walked a little man no taller than a child. "Good evening, young lady," he said. "Why are you crying so bitterly?" "Because I have to spin this straw into gold," she sobbed, "and I haven't the slightest idea how." "And what would you give me if I did it for you?" asked the little man. "My necklace," said the girl, and she took it off at once. The little man slipped the necklace into his pocket and sat down at the wheel. Three quick turns, and the reel was full of shining thread. Then he put on another reel, and another, and he kept spinning until dawn, when every last straw had turned to gold.

At sunrise the king unlocked the door, and his eyes grew wide at the sight. Yet the gold did not satisfy him; if anything, it only made him hungrier for more. That evening he had the girl taken to a much larger room, which was packed with straw up to the ceiling. "This must be gold by morning too," he told her, "and do not disappoint me." Alone again, she wept, and once more the door opened as if by magic. "What will you give me this time?" asked the little man. "The ring from my finger," she said, and she handed it to him. The wheel hummed all night long, and by morning the larger room glittered from wall to wall.

The king was delighted, yet still not content. On the third evening he led her into the largest room of all, heaped with even more straw than before. "Spin all of this tonight," he said, "and if you succeed, you shall be my wife." He reasoned that even though she was only a miller's daughter, he would never find a richer bride anywhere. As soon as she was alone, the little man appeared for the third time. "What will you give me now?" he asked. "I have nothing left," she replied in despair. "Then promise me your first child, once you are queen," he said. The girl hesitated, but nobody could say what the future would bring, and she saw no other way out of her trouble. So she gave her promise, and the little man spun the straw into gold once more.

When the king came in the morning and found everything as he had wished, he kept his word and married her. And so the miller's beautiful daughter became a queen. A year later she brought a lovely child into the world. In her happiness she gave no thought at all to the little man. Then, one evening, he stepped suddenly into her room. "Now give me what you promised," he said.

The horrified queen offered him all the riches of the kingdom if only he would leave her child alone. "No," said the little man. "Something living is dearer to me than all the treasure in the world." Then the queen began to weep so pitifully that even his hard little heart was touched. "I will give you three days," he said at last. "If by then you can tell me my name, you may keep your child."

The queen lay awake all night, going over every name she had ever heard. She also sent a messenger far and wide to gather any names he could find, however strange they might be. When the little man returned the next day, she started with Caspar, Melchior and Balthazar. After that she recited her whole long list, one name after another. But to every single name he answered, "That is not my name." On the second day she tried the oddest names that the messenger had collected on his travels. "Perhaps your name is Ribsofbeef?" she asked. "Or Muttonchops? Or Lacelegs?" "That is not my name either," he replied each time with a grin.

On the third day the messenger came back, tired and dusty from the road. "I have not found a single new name," he said. "But on a high mountain at the edge of a forest, I saw a little house." "In front of it a fire was burning, and around the fire a ridiculous little man was hopping on one leg." "As he hopped, he was singing a song at the top of his voice." Then the messenger repeated the little man's song, word for word. "Tonight I dance beside my fire, tomorrow I bake, and soon the queen's own child is mine!" "Nobody in the wide world can guess my secret, for my name is Rumpelstiltskin!" You can imagine how glad the queen was when she heard that name.

Soon after that, the little man came in, rubbing his hands with delight. "Well, my lady queen," he said, "what is my name?" First she pretended to guess a few ordinary names, one after another. "No!" he cried each time, grinning. "Then perhaps," she said slowly, "your name is Rumpelstiltskin?" "The devil told you that! The devil told you that!" screamed the little man. He stamped his foot so hard that the whole room shook. Then he rushed out of the castle in a furious rage, and nobody ever saw him again.

## Level C1

In a narrow valley, where a swift little river turned the wheel of a mill, there lived a miller who owned almost nothing but a beautiful daughter. His flour was coarse, and his roof leaked, but his tongue ran faster than his sense, and when it came to talking, he could make a puddle sound like a lake. It so happened that the king, riding home from a hunt, stopped one afternoon to water his horse at the mill. The miller, seized by the wish to appear more than a dusty tradesman, began to boast. "Your Majesty may not believe it," he said, puffing out his chest, "but my daughter is so gifted that she can spin plain straw into gold." Had he been a wiser man, he would have bitten his tongue and said no more; as it was, he simply beamed, delighted with the effect of his words. The king, whose love of gold was the one sincere passion of his life, did not laugh. "That is a gift worth seeing," he said. "Send her to my castle tomorrow, and we shall discover how much of what you say is true."

The next morning the girl was taken up the hill and shown into a small, windowless room, piled from floor to rafters with straw. In the middle stood a spinning wheel, and next to it an empty reel, waiting for thread that could not possibly exist. "You have until sunrise," said the king pleasantly, as though he were discussing the weather. "If this straw has not become gold by then, you will wish your father had kept his mouth shut." He turned the key in the lock himself and carried it away in his pocket. For a long while the girl sat among the straw, turning a single stalk between her fingers, as if looking at it hard enough might reveal some secret. It revealed nothing, of course, and as the candle burned lower her fear grew larger, until at last she put her face in her hands and wept.

It was then that the door, which she had heard the king lock with her own ears, opened without a sound. In stepped a little man, no taller than a child of six, with old, bright eyes that seemed to take in the room, the straw and the girl in a single glance. "Good evening, miller's daughter," he said. "What is all this crying about?" "I am supposed to spin this straw into gold," she said between sobs, "and I have no more idea how to do it than a cow does." "And what would you give me, if I spun it for you?" he asked. Without a moment's hesitation she unclasped the necklace at her throat and held it out to him. He weighed it in his palm, slipped it into his pocket, and sat down at the wheel as if he had been born there. Three quick turns, and the reel was full of thread that shone like the sun on water. He put on a second reel, and a third, and so the night went on, until dawn found every last stalk of straw transformed.

When the king unlocked the door at sunrise, he stood speechless on the threshold, his eyes moving from one gleaming heap to the next. Yet his joy lasted barely longer than his surprise, for gold, to a greedy heart, is less a meal than an appetite. By evening he had the girl led to a much larger room, where the straw reached the beams, and he told her, smiling, that she would do the same again by morning. Left alone, she wept as before, and as before the door opened and the little man came in. "What will you give me this time?" he asked, tilting his head. "The ring from my finger," she said, and she pulled it off and dropped it into his waiting hand. All night the wheel hummed its soft, tireless song, and when the king came in at daybreak, the larger room blazed with gold from wall to wall.

Delighted though he was, the king was not yet satisfied. On the third evening he led her into the largest room in the castle, where the straw was heaped as high as hay in a barn loft. "Spin all of this tonight," he said, "and tomorrow you shall be my queen." A miller's daughter, he reflected, was hardly the bride his ancestors would have chosen, but a richer wife than this one could not be found in the whole wide world. No sooner had his footsteps faded than the little man stood before her for the third time. "And what will you give me now?" he asked. "I have nothing left," she said, spreading her empty hands. "Then promise me this," he said softly. "When you are queen, you will give me your first child." A chill ran through her, but she reasoned, as frightened people do, that the future was a long way off and that nobody could say what it might bring. Seeing no other way out, she gave her word, and once more the wheel sang until morning.

The king, finding everything exactly as he had hoped, kept his side of the bargain. And so the miller's daughter, who had come to the castle as little more than a prisoner, now walked its halls as a royal bride. A year later she brought a beautiful child into the world, and in the warmth of her new happiness the little man and his bargain slipped entirely from her mind. Then one evening, as she sat alone with the baby in her lap, he stepped out of the shadows of her room. "Now give me what you promised," he said.

The queen, white with terror, offered him everything the kingdom could provide: chests of gold, jewels, lands, anything at all, if only he would leave her child alone. "No," said the little man. "Something living is dearer to me than all the treasure in the world." At that the queen began to weep so bitterly and so hopelessly that even he, who seemed to feel very little, was moved to a kind of pity. "Very well," he said. "I will give you three days. If by the end of them you know my name, you may keep your child."

She spent the whole night going over every name she had ever heard, from the grand names of kings to the plain ones of farmhands. Before dawn, she had sent a messenger riding out across the land to gather any others he could find. When the little man appeared the next day, she began with Caspar, Melchior and Balthazar, and went on through her entire list, name after name, until her voice grew hoarse. To each one he gave the same cheerful answer: "That is not my name." On the second day she tried the strangest names that the messenger had sent back, names so odd that she could hardly say them with a straight face. "Could it be Ribsofbeef?" she asked. "Or Muttonchops? Or perhaps Lacelegs?" "That is not my name either," he replied each time, enjoying himself enormously.

On the third day the messenger came back, his boots gray with dust and his face full of news. "I have not found a single new name, Your Majesty," he said, "but as I climbed a high mountain at the edge of a forest, I came upon a tiny house." "A fire was burning in front of it, and round the fire the most ridiculous little man was hopping about on one leg and singing at the top of his voice." The messenger then repeated the song as well as he could remember it. "Tonight I dance beside my fire, tomorrow I bake, and soon the queen's own child is mine!" "Let them search the wide world over, for nobody will ever guess that my name is Rumpelstiltskin!" It is not hard to imagine with what joy the queen heard that name, nor how carefully she repeated it to herself until the little man arrived.

He came in that evening rubbing his hands, already, it seemed, feeling the weight of the child in his arms. "Well, my lady queen," he said, "what is my name?" "Is it Melchior?" she asked, frowning as if in thought. "No," he said, with a grin that showed every one of his small teeth. "Then is it Muttonchops?" she asked. "No!" he laughed. "Then perhaps," said the queen, very slowly, "your name is Rumpelstiltskin?" For a moment the little man stood frozen, and then his face went from red to purple. "The devil told you that! The devil told you that!" he shrieked, and he stamped his foot so hard that the floorboards cracked. Then he ran out of the castle in a blind fury, and from that day on, nobody in the kingdom ever saw him again.
