# Mowgli's Brothers

Summary: A family of wolves takes in a lost baby boy, but a hungry tiger wants him back.

## Level A1

The Seeonee hills were warm that night. A father wolf woke up in his cave. Raksha, the mother wolf, lay with their cubs. Then Tabaqui the jackal came to the cave. "Shere Khan hunts here tonight," he said.

Soon the wolves heard an angry cry. The big tiger missed his dinner. Then something moved in the bushes. It was a little baby boy! He had no clothes. He looked up and laughed. The father wolf carried him inside.

Shere Khan pushed his head into the cave. "Give me the man's cub!" he shouted. Raksha jumped up. "He is my cub now," she said. "Go away from my home!" The angry tiger left. Raksha called the boy Mowgli, the little frog.

Later, the wolves met at Council Rock. Akela, the old gray wolf, led the pack. Shere Khan asked for the boy again. Then Baloo, the sleepy brown bear, stood up. "I can teach this cub," he said. Bagheera, the black panther, spoke next. "Keep the boy, and take my fat bull." The wolves wanted the meat, so Mowgli stayed.

Mowgli grew up with his wolf brothers. Baloo taught him the rules of the jungle. Bagheera taught him to climb trees.

Years later, Akela was old and slow. Shere Khan made friends with the young wolves. "Bring the red flower," Bagheera told Mowgli. The red flower was fire.

Mowgli ran to the village at night. He took some fire in a pot. That same night, Akela missed a deer. The young wolves wanted a new leader.

At Council Rock, Shere Khan smiled. "The boy is mine now," he said. Mowgli threw the fire pot on the grass. He waved a burning stick at Shere Khan. The tiger cried out and ran away.

Then water ran down Mowgli's face. "What is this?" he asked. "These are tears," said Bagheera. "Only men cry like this." Mowgli hugged Raksha goodbye. He walked down the hill to the men.

## Level A2

It was a hot evening in the Seeonee hills. A father wolf woke up after his long day of sleep. Raksha, the mother wolf, lay beside their four cubs. Suddenly Tabaqui the jackal appeared at the mouth of the cave. "Shere Khan, the great tiger, hunts in your hills tonight," he said. Then he ran off into the dark.

Soon the wolves heard an angry cry below the hill. "The tiger missed his dinner," said the father wolf. Then the bushes moved, and a tiny baby stepped out. He had no clothes, and he laughed up at the wolf. The father wolf carried him gently into the cave. The boy pushed in among the warm cubs. "Look, he is not afraid at all," said Raksha.

Then Shere Khan pushed his head into the mouth of the cave. "Give me the man's cub," he said. Raksha jumped forward with burning green eyes. "This cub is mine, and he stays with me!" The tiger was too big for the cave, so he backed out. "This is not the end!" he shouted from the trees. Raksha named the boy Mowgli, the little frog.

When the moon was full, the wolves met at Council Rock. Akela, the old gray leader, lay on top of the stone. From the dark, Shere Khan asked for the boy again. By wolf law, two animals must speak for a strange cub. Baloo, the sleepy brown bear, stood up first. "Let him live, and I can teach him," he said. Next came Bagheera, the black panther, with his soft voice. "Take my fat bull, and give the boy a home." The wolves were hungry, so they agreed.

Mowgli grew up with his four wolf brothers. Baloo taught him the calls of every animal. Bagheera showed him the trees and the safe paths. But years later, Akela grew old. Shere Khan made friends with the young wolves. "Bring back the red flower from the village," Bagheera told Mowgli. The red flower was fire, and every animal feared it.

That night Mowgli ran down to the farms and huts. He took a small pot of burning wood from a boy. At the same time, Akela tried to catch a deer and failed. The young wolves laughed at their old leader.

At the next meeting, Shere Khan walked onto the Rock. "The man's cub is mine now," he said. Mowgli stood up and threw the pot on the dry grass. Bright flames jumped up, and the wolves moved back. He took a burning branch and hit the tiger. Shere Khan cried like a kitten and ran into the night. "Leave Akela alone," Mowgli told the young wolves.

Then something wet ran down his face. "Bagheera, what is this?" he asked. "Men cry tears," said the panther, "and now you are a man." Mowgli said goodbye to Raksha and the father wolf. Then he walked alone down the hill to live with men.

## Level B1

It was seven o'clock on a hot evening in the Seeonee hills. A father wolf woke up from his day's rest and stretched his tired legs. Raksha, the mother wolf, was lying beside their four cubs at the back of the cave.

Just then, a small shape appeared at the entrance. It was Tabaqui, who ate scraps and loved to carry bad news. "Shere Khan has moved his hunting ground to these hills," Tabaqui said. "He will hunt here for the next moon." Then he slipped away into the bushes, pleased with the trouble he had made. The father wolf was angry, because the jungle law did not allow this.

Soon they heard a great cry from the valley below. "The fool jumped at a woodcutters' campfire and burned his feet," said the father wolf. Then he stopped, because something was climbing up the hill toward the cave. In front of him stood a tiny baby boy with no clothes at all. The child looked up into the wolf's face and laughed.

"A man's cub!" said Raksha. "Bring him to me." The father wolf carried the baby inside so gently that his teeth left no mark. The boy pushed his way in among the cubs to get close to the warm fur. "How small he is, and how brave," Raksha said softly.

Suddenly the moonlight vanished from the door. Shere Khan had pushed his great square head and shoulders into the entrance. "I followed a man's cub here," he said. "Give him to me." Then Raksha sprang forward, and her eyes burned like two green moons. "The cub is mine," she said. "He will live and run with the pack. Now go away, lame one, before I make you sorry!" Shere Khan knew that he could not fight inside such a narrow cave. He pulled back his head, growling, and shouted that the cub would be his one day. Raksha looked at the baby and gave him a name: Mowgli, the little frog.

When the moon was full, the wolves met on Council Rock. Akela, the great gray leader of the pack, lay on top of the stone. "Look closely, wolves," Akela called again and again. From behind the rocks, Shere Khan demanded the man's cub. The law said that two animals, not his parents, had to speak for a new cub. Baloo, the sleepy brown bear who taught the young wolves, stood up first. "This little man will do us no harm," he said. "Let him run with the pack." Then Bagheera, the black panther whom everyone feared, stepped softly into the light. "I have no right to speak here," he said in his gentle voice. "But I can offer a fat bull, which I killed not far from this rock." The hungry young wolves agreed at once and ran off to eat.

So Mowgli grew up with the wolves. The father wolf taught him the ways of the jungle, and Baloo taught him the law. Bagheera showed him how to climb trees and find honey. He also told Mowgli never to touch village cattle, because a bull paid for his life. Mowgli grew strong, and no wolf could look into his eyes for long.

Akela, meanwhile, was growing old and weak. Shere Khan was making friends with the young wolves and filling their heads with lies. "Little Brother, they will turn against you," Bagheera warned. "Go down to the village and take some of the red flower." He meant fire, which every animal in the jungle feared. That night Mowgli watched a farmer's child feeding a fire in a small clay pot. He took the pot from the boy and ran back into the darkness. On the same night, Akela missed his jump at a big deer.

The next evening the pack met again, and Shere Khan spoke boldly. "The old wolf failed, so now you must give me the man's cub." Mowgli stood up with the pot in his hands. He threw the hot coals onto the dry grass, and flames jumped up. The whole Council moved back in terror. Mowgli pushed a dead branch into the fire until it was burning brightly. He struck Shere Khan with it, and the tiger ran howling into the night. "Akela will go free," he told the young wolves. "Nobody here will hurt him."

After that, Mowgli felt something wet on his cheeks, and he began to cry. "What is it, Bagheera?" he asked. "Am I going to die?" "No, Little Brother, those are only tears, and men cry often," said the panther. "Now I know that you are a man and not a cub anymore," he added. Mowgli went to the cave and cried with Raksha and his four brothers. At sunrise, he walked down the hill to find the people of the village.

## Level B2

On a warm evening in the Seeonee hills, a father wolf woke from his long day's sleep and stretched. Raksha, the mother wolf, lay beside their four cubs. The father wolf was just preparing to go out and hunt when a narrow shadow crossed the doorway. It belonged to Tabaqui, who lived on scraps, told tales, and was despised by every wolf in the hills.

Tabaqui greeted them with false politeness, his eyes searching the cave for bones. Shere Khan, the great tiger, had moved his hunting ground from the Waingunga river to these very hills. The father wolf was furious, since the law of the jungle forbade a tiger to change his ground without warning. Worse still, Shere Khan was hunting men, and a man-killing tiger would sooner or later bring hunters with guns and torches. Satisfied with the trouble he had caused, Tabaqui slipped away into the bushes.

Before long, an angry cry rose from the valley. "He has missed," said the father wolf with a short laugh. "The fool jumped at a woodcutters' fire and burned his feet, and now his dinner has escaped." Hearing a rustle, the father wolf sprang, but he checked himself in mid-air and landed in astonishment. Standing before him, holding on to a low branch, was a naked brown baby who could barely walk. The child looked straight up into the wolf's face and laughed.

"Is that a man's cub?" Raksha asked. "I have never seen one, so bring it here." The father wolf lifted the baby in his jaws so carefully that not a single tooth scratched the skin. Set down among the cubs, the little boy wriggled his way into the warm fur and made himself at home. "How little he is, and how bold," Raksha murmured.

Suddenly the moonlight was blocked out of the doorway. Shere Khan had forced his great head and shoulders into the entrance, but the cave was too narrow for him. "A man's cub came this way," he growled. "It is my prey, so give it to me." Then Raksha rose and faced the tiger, her eyes like two green moons in the darkness. "The man's cub is mine," she declared. "Nobody will kill him, for he will live to run and hunt with the pack. And one day, you lame old bully, this cub will hunt you down!" Backing out, Shere Khan warned them that the cub would be his in the end. Raksha then turned to the child and named him Mowgli, the little frog.

According to the law, every cub had to be shown to the pack before he could run with them. So when the moon was full, the wolves gathered on Council Rock. Akela, the great gray wolf who led the pack, lay stretched out on his rock and called, "Look closely, wolves!" When it was Mowgli's turn, the boy sat in the moonlight and played with some pebbles. From the shadows, Shere Khan roared that the man's cub belonged to him. If anyone disputed a cub's right, two members of the pack besides his parents had to speak for him.

First came Baloo, the sleepy brown bear, who was welcome at the Council because he taught the cubs the law. "Let the man's cub run with the pack, and I myself will teach him," he rumbled. Then Bagheera, the black panther, stepped forward and spoke in a soft, gentle voice. He admitted that he had no right to speak at the Council. "But the law also says that a cub's life may be bought for a price," he went on. "A fat bull lies freshly killed not half a mile from here, and it is yours if you accept the man's cub." The young wolves, always hungry, agreed at once, and Mowgli was accepted into the pack. Akela added that a man's cub might be useful to them one day.

Mowgli grew up among the wolves, and the father wolf taught him the meaning of every sound in the forest. Baloo taught him the law and the special words that let him speak to birds, snakes and hunting animals. Bagheera warned him never to touch the cattle of the village, because his own life had been paid for with a bull. One day the panther showed Mowgli a bare patch under his chin, the mark of a collar. Bagheera had been born in a king's cage among men, and he had broken the lock to escape. Mowgli also noticed that no wolf could hold his gaze for long, and Bagheera explained why. "You are a man," he said, "and the wolves are afraid of you."

Meanwhile, Akela was growing old, and Shere Khan was flattering the young wolves with gifts of meat. "Little Brother, when Akela misses his kill, they will turn against him and you," Bagheera said. "Go to the huts in the valley and bring back some of the Red Flower." By the Red Flower he meant fire, which no creature in the jungle dared to touch. That night Mowgli crept down to the village and watched a boy feeding a fire in a small clay pot. Snatching the pot, he raced back into the hills. On the same night, Akela leaped at a large deer and missed for the first time in his life.

At the next Council, Akela lay beside his rock instead of on it, and Shere Khan was allowed to speak. "The man's cub belongs to me," the tiger demanded. "He is a man, and he has no place among wolves." Several wolves shouted their agreement, and Bagheera reminded them angrily that the boy had been paid for. At that moment Mowgli stood up, the fire pot in his hands. "You listen to a tiger's lies," he told them, "so I am leaving you to go to my own people." He flung the pot to the ground, the red coals lit the dry moss, and the whole Council drew back in terror. Pushing a dead branch into the flames, Mowgli whirled it above his head. He grabbed Shere Khan by the fur of his chin and beat him with the branch until the tiger fled. The young wolves who had sided with the tiger ran after him, and Akela was left in peace.

Then water ran down Mowgli's face. "What is this, Bagheera?" he cried. "Am I dying?" "No, Little Brother, that is only weeping, and men do it all the time," the panther replied gently. "Now I am sure you are a man, so let the tears come," he said. Mowgli went back to the cave and wept against Raksha's side, while his four brothers howled with sadness. "Come back to us soon," Raksha told him. At dawn, Mowgli walked down the hillside alone toward the villages of his own kind.

## Level C1

The heat of the day was only beginning to lift from the Seeonee hills when the father wolf woke, scratched himself, and yawned until his jaws creaked. Beside him, Raksha lay with her gray nose laid across four squirming cubs, while the moon poured a pale light through the mouth of the cave.

Just then a ragged shadow slid across the threshold: Tabaqui the scrap-eater, a creature who lived on leftovers and gossip and was more feared for his tongue than for his teeth. He began by praising the cubs, which every wolf knows to be bad luck, and only then delivered his real errand. Shere Khan, the great tiger, had abandoned his old hunting grounds by the Waingunga and intended to hunt among these hills for the coming moon. The father wolf's anger was immediate, for the law of the jungle forbade any beast to change his ground without fair warning. Worse, Shere Khan had taken to stalking men, and a tiger who hunted men would sooner or later bring men with fire, drums and guns into the jungle.

Soon a long, whining snarl drifted up from the valley, the sound of a tiger who had missed his prey. "The fool has jumped into a woodcutters' campfire," the father wolf remarked with contempt, "and scorched his feet into the bargain." Even as he spoke, the bushes below the cave began to stir, and he gathered himself for a leap. What happened next was something few wolves had ever managed: he checked himself in mid-spring and dropped back to earth, utterly astonished. Clutching a low branch for balance stood a naked brown child, barely old enough to walk, who gazed up into the wolf's face and laughed.

Raksha had never seen a man's cub, and she asked her mate to bring it closer. He carried the child in his mouth with such care that not a tooth so much as grazed the soft skin, and laid it down among the cubs. At once the baby elbowed his way into the warm heap of fur, as though he had been born there. "How small, and how utterly without fear," Raksha said, watching him with a softness she rarely showed.

Then the moonlight was suddenly blotted out. Shere Khan had thrust his broad head and shoulders into the opening, though the cave was far too narrow for the rest of him. "Hand over the man's cub," he demanded, his voice booming around the stone walls. "It is my quarry, and I want it." The father wolf replied, with a coolness he did not entirely feel, that wolves took orders from the leader of their own pack and from no tiger. Springing forward, her eyes blazing like twin green moons in the dark, Raksha told him that the cub was hers and would live to run with the pack. "And one day, Lame One, brave hunter of helpless babies, he will be the one who hunts you!" Shere Khan, who knew better than to fight in such a space, withdrew with a growl, promising that the matter was far from finished. When he had gone, Raksha looked down at the child and gave him his name: Mowgli, the Frog.

The law decreed that every cub must be presented to the pack before he might run with it, and so, at the full of the moon, the wolves gathered on Council Rock. Akela, the great gray wolf who led the pack by strength and cunning, lay stretched upon his stone, calling out the ancient summons: "Look closely, wolves!" When Mowgli was pushed into the circle, he sat in the moonlight and played contentedly with a handful of pebbles, untroubled by the hundred eyes upon him. Out of the darkness rumbled the voice of Shere Khan, insisting that the cub was his and asking what business free wolves had with a man's child. Under the law, should anyone challenge a cub's right, at least two members of the pack other than his parents were required to speak for him. For a terrible moment nobody moved.

Then Baloo rose on his hind legs, the sleepy brown bear whom the Council tolerated because he taught the wolf cubs the law of the jungle. "This little man means nobody any harm," he grunted, in the plain way of someone who has never learned to make speeches. "I will speak for him, and I will teach him myself." A second voice followed, low and silky and dangerously gentle, and Bagheera stepped into the ring. Black as midnight and as patient as a shadow, he was a hunter whose path no animal in the jungle would willingly cross. He admitted he had no right to speak at a wolf council, but the law allowed a cub's life to be bought at a price. "There is a bull, newly killed, lying not half a mile from here," he purred. "It is yours, if you will take the man's cub according to the law." The young wolves, whose stomachs always spoke louder than their judgment, agreed with indecent haste and streamed off downhill toward the carcass. Akela, gazing after them, observed quietly that a man's cub might prove useful one day, and that a wise leader looked ahead.

So Mowgli grew up among the wolves, learning from the father wolf the meaning of every rustle, every scent and every change of wind. Baloo drilled him patiently in the law and in the words that would keep him safe among birds, snakes and hunters alike. From Bagheera he learned to climb like a monkey, to find wild honey, and to doze away the hottest hours along a branch. The panther also warned him, more than once, never to touch the cattle of the village, since his own life had been bought with a bull. Once, Bagheera showed him a bald patch beneath his chin where a collar had rubbed, for he too had been born among men, in a king's cages. It was from Bagheera, too, that Mowgli learned why no wolf could meet his stare for long: whatever he believed himself to be, he was a man, and the wolves knew it.

Years passed, and Akela grew old and slow, while Shere Khan, never one to forget a grudge, quietly made friends among the young wolves, feeding them scraps and flattery in equal measure. "When Akela misses his kill, as one day he must, they will turn on him and then on you," Bagheera warned. "Go down to the huts of men and bring back the Red Flower." He meant fire, the one thing every creature of the jungle feared and none dared to name. That very night Mowgli crept to the edge of the village and watched a farmer's boy tending the glowing coals in a little earthen pot. Waiting until the boy turned away, he seized the pot and carried it back into the dark like a living thing. In the same darkness, Akela sprang at a great buck and, for the first time in his long life, missed.

At the next Council, the old leader lay beside his rock rather than upon it, a sign to every wolf that his rule was over. Shere Khan, grown bold, now spoke openly and claimed the man's cub as his rightful prey. Some wolves argued that the boy had broken no law; others, louder and younger, howled that a man had no place among them. Bagheera reminded them scornfully that the cub had been paid for, but the pack no longer listened. It was at this point that Mowgli rose, the fire pot in his hands, and looked at them as no wolf had ever looked at another. "You have called me a man so often," he said, "that I believe it, and I will go to my own people. But there is something I must settle first." He flung the pot to the ground, and as the coals set the dry moss ablaze, the whole Council shrank back from the leaping flames. Thrusting a dead branch into the blaze, Mowgli strode to Shere Khan, gripped the fur beneath his chin, and beat him with the blazing wood until the tiger fled. He chased the tiger's followers away with burning sticks and told the rest that Akela was to live as he pleased.

When it was over, something caught in Mowgli's throat, and to his bewilderment he began to sob, the tears running down his face. "What is happening to me, Bagheera?" he asked. "Am I dying?" "No, Little Brother," the panther said gently, "it is only weeping, a thing that men do and wolves never can. I have no more doubt about what you are; so let it come, Mowgli, and do not be ashamed of it." He went first to the cave, where he cried against Raksha's side while his four brothers howled mournfully around him. "Do not stay away too long, my small bare-skinned son," Raksha said, "for no cub of mine was ever dearer to me than you." Then, as dawn broke gray over the Seeonee hills, Mowgli went down the hillside alone toward the smoke of the village and a people he had never known.
