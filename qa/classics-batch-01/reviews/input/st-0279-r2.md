# Robinson Crusoe

Summary: A young sailor survives a shipwreck and must learn to live alone on a wild island.

## Level A1

Robinson Crusoe lived in York, in England. He loved the sea. His father said, "Stay home." But Robinson went to sea. Years later, he had a farm in Brazil. Then he sailed on a big ship again.

A terrible storm came. The sailors got into a small boat. Then a wave turned the boat over. At last, he reached an island. He was alive, but his friends were gone.

The next morning, the sea was calm. He saw the ship on some rocks. Robinson swam out to it. He found food, tools, and guns. He made a raft from wood. He took many things to the island.

Robinson built a home near a big rock. He put a strong fence around it. He made a tall wooden post. Every day, he cut a mark in it. So he knew the day and the year.

There were wild goats on the island. Robinson caught some young goats. They gave him milk. He also had a parrot, Poll. Robinson taught Poll some words. "Poor Robinson Crusoe!" the bird said.

Years went by, and no ships came. Then he found a footprint in the sand. It was the print of a man's foot. Robinson was very afraid. He ran home and hid. Who was on his island?

Many years later, boats came to the beach. Some men brought a prisoner with them. Suddenly the prisoner ran away. Two men ran after him. Robinson jumped out and helped him. His gun scared the two men away.

It was a Friday. So Robinson called his new friend Friday. He taught Friday English. Friday helped him every day. Robinson was not alone now.

At last, a ship came to the island. The captain needed help, and they helped him. Then Robinson and Friday sailed away together. He was there for twenty-eight years. He went home with his friend.

## Level A2

Robinson Crusoe was born in the city of York, in England. His father wanted him to stay at home. But the young man dreamed only of the sea. He ran away and became a sailor. Later, he bought a farm in Brazil. Then he joined a ship for a long trading trip.

Twelve days later, a terrible storm hit the ship. Then the ship hit the sand and stopped. The sailors jumped into a small boat. But a huge wave turned it over. Finally, the waves threw Robinson onto a beach. Nobody else came out of the sea.

That night, he slept in a tree. In the morning, he saw the ship near the shore. Robinson swam to it and found bread, rice, and cheese. He made a raft from pieces of wood. Over many days, he carried food, tools, and guns to the island. He also saved a dog and two cats.

Next, he built a strong fence near a high rock. He put his tent inside it and dug a cave behind it. On the beach, he put up a big wooden post. Every day he cut a small mark on it. With this post, he never lost count of the days.

The island had many wild goats. Robinson caught some young ones, so soon he had milk and cheese. He planted a few old seeds and later made bread. He also caught a young parrot and named it Poll. Robinson talked to the bird every day. Then, one afternoon, Poll called out, "Poor Robinson Crusoe! Where are you?" Robinson almost fell over in surprise.

Many years passed, and Robinson often felt lonely. He made pots and clothes from goat skins. One day, he walked along the beach and suddenly stopped. There, in the sand, was a footprint. It was the print of a man's bare foot. Robinson looked everywhere, but he saw nobody. He ran home and did not sleep that night. For a long time, he lived in fear.

Years later, he saw smoke on the beach. A group of men sat around a fire near their canoes. They had two prisoners with them. Suddenly, one prisoner broke free and ran toward Robinson's hill. Two men ran after him. Robinson hurried down and stood between them. He knocked the first man down. The noise of his gun frightened the second.

The prisoner shook with fear, but Robinson smiled at him. It was a Friday, so Robinson called him Friday. Robinson taught Friday English, and Friday learned very fast. Friday worked hard and helped Robinson with the goats and the fields. They became true friends.

One morning, Friday ran in with big news. "A ship! A ship!" he shouted. Some sailors on the ship were against their captain. Robinson and Friday helped the captain, and he got his ship back. So after twenty-eight years, Robinson finally left the island. Friday sailed with him, and the two friends went to England together.

## Level B1

Robinson Crusoe was born in the old city of York, in England. His father, a careful man, wanted his son to study law. But Robinson could think only about ships and faraway lands. At nineteen, he ran away to sea without his father's blessing. In time, he settled in Brazil and became the owner of a sugar farm. The farm was doing well, yet Robinson soon grew restless again. When some neighbors planned a trading voyage, he agreed to join them.

Not long after they left Brazil, a terrible storm began. For twelve days the wind threw the ship across the sea. Then the ship struck a sandbank and stuck fast. The men climbed into a small boat, but a giant wave turned it over. Again and again, the sea pulled Robinson under the water. At last, it threw him onto a beach, where he held on to a rock. He was alive, but none of his companions came out of the sea.

That first night he slept in a tree, holding a short stick. By morning the storm was over, and the ship sat on rocks nearby. He swam out to it and found that much of the food was dry. He tied some pieces of wood together and made a raft. Over the next two weeks, he made eleven trips to the ship. He brought back bread, rice, cheese, tools, guns, sails, and rope. Even the ship's dog and two cats came with him. Then one night another storm came, and the ship was gone.

Now Robinson needed a safe home. He chose a flat place in front of a steep rock. There he built a high fence and put up a tent. Behind the tent, he dug a cave, which became his storeroom. He was afraid of losing track of time. So he set up a large wooden post on the shore. Every day he cut a notch into it, and every seventh notch was longer. This simple post was his calendar.

Little by little, he learned to live on the island. He hunted wild goats, and later he kept young ones in a field. Soon he had milk and cheese whenever he wanted. Some old seeds that he threw away grew into barley and rice. After a few years, he was baking his own bread. He made pots from clay and sewed clothes from goat skins. In the evenings, he read his Bible.

His best companion in those years was a parrot named Poll. Robinson taught the bird to speak, and it soon learned his name. One afternoon, while he was sleeping after a long walk, a voice woke him. "Poor Robinson Crusoe! Where are you?" it cried. He jumped up in fear, and then he laughed, because it was only Poll.

Fifteen years passed in this way. Then one day, as he was walking along the beach, Robinson stopped in shock. In the sand in front of him was a single footprint. It was the print of a man's bare foot. He looked around and listened, but he saw no one. He ran home like a frightened animal and did not sleep all night. For two years he lived in fear, and he planted trees to hide his home.

Many years later, early one morning, he saw five canoes on the beach. A group of men was dancing around a fire. They had two prisoners with them. Suddenly one of the prisoners broke away and ran toward Robinson's hill. Two men ran after him. Robinson rushed down and knocked the first man over. Then he frightened the second one away with his gun.

The young man was shaking with fear, but he soon understood that he was safe. It was a Friday, so Robinson decided to call him Friday. Friday was clever, kind, and full of good humor. He learned English quickly, and he asked hundreds of questions. Robinson also taught him to cook goat meat, and Friday soon loved it. He worked hard beside Robinson and helped him with the goats and the fields. For the first time in twenty-five years, Robinson had someone to talk to. The two men became true friends.

About three years later, Friday came running with news. "Look! A ship!" he shouted. An English ship was lying near the shore, but something was wrong. Some sailors were fighting against their captain because they wanted the ship. With Robinson's plan and Friday's help, the captain won back his ship. So after twenty-eight years on the island, Robinson Crusoe sailed away at last. His good friend Friday stood beside him on the ship.

## Level B2

Robinson Crusoe was born in 1632 in the city of York, the third son of a respectable family. His father begged him to stay at home and enjoy a calm, ordinary life. Robinson promised to obey, but the sea called to him more loudly than any advice. At nineteen, he boarded a ship without even telling his parents, and a storm on that very first voyage almost drowned him. Most young men would have gone home, but Robinson went to sea again.

Over the following years his luck rose and fell like the waves. He was captured by pirates and spent two years as a prisoner before he escaped in a small fishing boat. A kind captain picked him up at sea and carried him safely to Brazil. There he bought land, planted sugar and tobacco, and slowly became a wealthy man. When some neighboring farmers planned a trading voyage across the ocean, he offered to sail with it.

Within two weeks a violent storm caught them. For twelve days the wind drove the ship wherever it pleased, and the crew expected to die at any moment. Early one morning the ship struck a sandbank and stuck fast. Fearing that she would break apart, the eleven men still on board lowered the boat and rowed toward the shore. A wave as tall as a mountain rose behind them and turned the boat over in an instant. Robinson was carried forward, dragged back, and thrown forward again. At last, he managed to cling to a rock and then scramble up onto the grass. Of all the ship's company, he alone had survived.

He spent that first night in a tree, afraid of wild animals. By morning the storm was over, and the tide had carried the ship much closer to the shore. At low tide he swam out to her. He made a raft from spare masts and planks and loaded it with food, tools, guns, gunpowder, and a barrel of water. Over the next thirteen days he made eleven trips, bringing back sails, ropes, clothes, and even the ship's dog and two cats. That night another storm blew up, and in the morning the wreck had vanished.

Next he searched for a safe place to live. He chose a grassy spot in front of a steep rock, close to fresh water. Around it, he built a fence of strong wooden posts, so tall that no animal could climb over it. Behind his tent he dug a cave into the rock and used it as a storeroom. Afraid that he might lose track of time, he set up a large wooden post on the beach. On it, he carved the date of his arrival, the thirtieth of September 1659. Every day he cut a notch in the post, every Sunday a longer one, and every month a longer one still.

During his first year he fell ill with a fever and lay alone for days, shaking and too weak to fetch water. He opened the Bible he had brought from the ship and began, for the first time in years, to pray. When he recovered, his island began to feel less like a prison.

Little by little he became farmer, hunter, and craftsman all at once. He caught young wild goats and kept them in fenced fields, so he always had milk and cheese. One day he noticed green shoots near his fence. He was amazed, until he remembered shaking out an old bag of chicken feed on that very spot. Within four years those few grains gave him enough barley for real bread. He made pots from clay and sewed clothes and an umbrella from goat skins. Once he spent five months carving a huge canoe from a tree trunk. Only when it was finished did he realize it was far too heavy to drag to the water.

His dearest companion in those years was a parrot, which he named Poll and patiently taught to speak. One afternoon, after a long and tiring journey around the island, he fell asleep in the shade. He woke to a voice calling his name: "Poor Robinson Crusoe! Where are you?" Then he saw Poll on the fence, proudly repeating the words Robinson had taught it.

Fifteen years passed in this way. Then, one day around noon, as he walked along the shore toward his boat, Robinson stopped as if struck by lightning. In the sand before him was the clear print of a man's bare foot, with every toe and the heel plainly visible. There was only one footprint, and somehow that was more frightening than a hundred would have been. He hurried home, looking back every few steps, and did not sleep that night. For years afterward he lived in fear, strengthening his walls and planting trees until his home was hidden from the sea.

Later he discovered that visitors sometimes came to the far side of the island in canoes, bringing prisoners with them. These were men who meant to kill their prisoners, and the thought filled Robinson with horror. In his twenty-fifth year on the island, five canoes landed on the beach near his home. He watched a group of men dancing around a fire, with two prisoners beside them. Suddenly one of the prisoners broke free and ran straight toward Robinson's hill. Only two of the men chased him. Robinson ran down the hill, knocked the first man down with his gun, and fired at the second. The others, too far away to see what had happened, later paddled off without them.

The young man Robinson had saved was trembling, unsure whether his rescuer was friend or enemy. Robinson smiled, spoke gently, and offered him bread, raisins, and water. Because it was a Friday, he gave him the name Friday. Friday was quick, cheerful, and honest, and he learned English with remarkable speed. Robinson also taught him to cook, to plant barley, and to care for the goats. In return, Friday proved a tireless helper, and he handled a canoe far better than Robinson ever could. After so many silent years, Robinson found that he had a true friend.

Three years later, Friday came running into their home, shouting that a ship was anchored off the coast. It was an English ship, but something was badly wrong. Its sailors had turned against their captain and planned to leave him and two loyal men on the island. Robinson and Friday freed the captain, and with a clever plan the small group won back the ship without a long fight. On the nineteenth of December 1686, after twenty-eight years, two months, and nineteen days, Robinson Crusoe finally left his island. He took his goatskin cap, his umbrella, and Poll. Friday stood beside him at the rail, eager to see England for the first time.

## Level C1

Robinson Crusoe came into the world in 1632 in the city of York, the third son of a merchant who had grown comfortable through hard work and caution. His father, by now confined to his chair with gout, pleaded with him to stay at home. Robinson listened, wept, promised to obey, and within a year had slipped away to sea without so much as a goodbye. A storm on his first voyage made him swear never to sail again, but the oath lasted only as long as the weather.

What followed was a youth spent at the mercy of fortune. Captured by pirates on a trading voyage, he spent two years as a prisoner before escaping in a small fishing boat. A generous captain picked him up at sea and carried him to Brazil. There he bought land, planted tobacco and sugar, and within four years was well on his way to becoming a wealthy man. Most men would have been content; Robinson, true to his restless nature, was not. When his neighbors proposed a trading voyage across the ocean, he agreed to take charge of it.

Twelve days out, a hurricane fell upon them with such violence that for nearly two weeks the crew could do nothing but let the wind carry them where it would. Then, at dawn, a sailor cried, "Land!" and almost in the same breath the ship ran onto a bank of sand and stuck, the sea breaking over her in white sheets. Fearing she would break apart, the eleven survivors crowded into the ship's boat and rowed toward a shore they could barely see. A wave like a moving mountain overtook them a mile out and turned the boat over before anyone could cry out.

The sea buried him, lifted him, flung him forward, and dragged him back, and twice he was certain he would drown. At last a wave dashed him against a rock, knocking the breath from his body. He clung there until the water drew back, then scrambled up the beach and collapsed on the grass, beyond the reach of the tide. Of all the ship's company, he alone had been spared, and the joy of being alive was quickly followed by the terror of being alone.

He spent the night in a tree, armed with nothing but a short stick, and woke to find the storm gone and the sea as calm as a pond. Overnight the tide had lifted the ship off the sand and carried her within a mile of the shore. Swimming out at low tide, he discovered, to his great relief, that the ship's food was dry and much of her cargo undamaged. From spare masts and planks he built a raft, and over thirteen days he made eleven trips. He ferried ashore bread and rice, cheese and dried meat, tools and nails, guns and powder, sails and rope. The ship's dog swam after him, and two cats rode on the raft. In a drawer he found a heap of coins and laughed aloud at their uselessness, then took them all the same. The next night a storm swept the wreck away.

He chose a level piece of ground at the foot of a steep rock, sheltered from the midday sun and within sight of the sea. Around it, he drove a half circle of sharpened posts into the earth, entering and leaving by a ladder that he drew up behind him. Behind his tent he dug into the rock, and the hollow became his storeroom, kitchen, and workshop. Fearing that without books or company he would lose all sense of time, he set up a great wooden post on the shore and carved on it the date of his landing. Each day he cut a notch along its side, each Sunday a longer one, and each month a longer one still.

The first year tested him more than any storm had done. A violent fever laid him low for weeks, and in his delirium he dreamed of a fiery figure reproaching him for his wasted life. Too weak to fetch water, he reached for the Bible he had carried from the ship and, for the first time since boyhood, prayed. The fever passed, and with it something in him changed: he began to see the island less as a prison than as a strange kind of gift.

Over the years that followed he became, by trial and many errors, a farmer, a hunter, a potter, a tailor, and a carpenter. He hunted the wild goats that roamed the hills, but powder was precious, so he began to catch the young ones alive. One morning green stalks appeared beside his wall, and he almost called it a miracle before remembering the old bag of chicken feed he had shaken out there. Sown and resown with great patience, those few grains at last gave him enough to bake real bread. His most ambitious project, a canoe carved from a single enormous tree, cost him five months of labor. Only when it was finished did he admit that no man alone could ever drag it to the sea.

Of all his companions, none gave him more pleasure than Poll, the parrot he had caught as a chick and taught, with endless patience, to talk. One afternoon he fell asleep in the shade after a long journey around the coast. A voice woke him, calling his name again and again: "Poor Robinson Crusoe! Where are you?" He leaped up in terror, half convinced that his mind had finally given way. Then he saw Poll on the hedge, cheerfully repeating the very words of self-pity that he himself had so often spoken aloud.

Fifteen years passed in this fashion, until one day, walking toward his boat along a part of the shore he seldom visited, he stopped as if struck by lightning. There in the sand lay the print of a man's naked foot, every toe and the curve of the heel perfectly clear. He listened and looked about him, but there was no sound, no person, and no second print. A single footprint, he later admitted, frightened him more than a crowd of strangers would have done, for his imagination peopled the empty beach with every danger it could invent. He fled home like a hunted animal, looking over his shoulder at every bush, and for two years afterward he hardly dared fire a gun for fear of being heard.

In time, he learned that people from other lands did come, now and then, to the far side of the island, bringing prisoners whom they meant to kill. The chance he needed arrived in his twenty-fifth year on the island, when five canoes landed on his own side of the shore. Through his telescope he watched a group of men dancing around a fire, two prisoners waiting nearby. Without warning one of the prisoners broke away and raced along the sand toward the very hill where Robinson lay hidden, with only two of the others in pursuit. Robinson understood at once that his moment had come. He ran down by a shorter path and placed himself between the runaway and his pursuers. The first he knocked to the ground; the second fled at the roar of his gun.

The young man approached slowly, trembling, unsure whether this bearded stranger in goatskins meant to save him or harm him. Robinson smiled, beckoned, and offered him bread, raisins, and water, and slowly the fear left the young man's face. Since the day was a Friday, Robinson named him Friday. Friday proved quick, warm-hearted, and endlessly curious, and within a year the two of them could talk about almost anything in English. Robinson taught him to cook, to sow barley, and to milk the goats, and Friday soon preferred roasted goat to anything he had known. In the water, however, the roles reversed: Friday handled a canoe with an ease Robinson could never match, and together they built a boat sturdy enough for the open sea. For the first time in a quarter of a century, Robinson's days were full of conversation, and his island no longer felt like a prison.

One morning Friday burst in crying that a boat was coming, and from the hilltop Robinson saw an English ship lying at anchor off the coast. Something, however, was badly wrong: her crew had risen against their captain and meant to leave him and two loyal companions on the island. Working in secret, Robinson and Friday freed the prisoners, and with patience, cunning, and a good deal of bluff, the small band won back the ship without a pitched battle. The grateful captain, who could hardly believe this wild figure in goatskins was an Englishman, offered to carry them home.

And so, on the nineteenth of December 1686, after twenty-eight years, two months, and nineteen days, Robinson Crusoe left his island. He carried away as keepsakes his great goatskin cap, his umbrella, and Poll, along with the coins he had once laughed at in the wreck. At the rail beside him stood Friday, gazing at the wide sea with the same eager curiosity that had once driven a young man from York away from home.
