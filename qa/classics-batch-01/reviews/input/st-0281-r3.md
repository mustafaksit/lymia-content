# The Pied Piper of Hamelin

Summary: A town full of rats makes a deal with a strange piper and then breaks it.

## Level A1

Hamelin was a small town by a river. The name of the river was the Weser. One summer, many rats came to the town. They ran in the houses and the streets. The rats ate the bread and the cheese. They even slept in the men's hats. Nobody could sleep at night.

The people ran to the Mayor. "Do something!" they shouted. But the Mayor had no plan.

A tall, thin man came into the room. His coat was half red and half yellow. He had a long pipe in his hand. "I am the Piper," he said. "I can take your rats away." "Pay me a thousand coins." "We can pay fifty thousand!" cried the Mayor.

The Piper played a strange little song. Rats came out of every house. They all ran after the Piper. He walked down to the Weser. The rats jumped into the deep water. The river took them all away.

The people rang the bells and danced. Then the Piper came back. "Now give me my thousand coins," he said.

The Mayor only laughed. "The rats are in the river," he said. "Why pay so much? Take fifty." "You made a promise," said the Piper. "Play your songs!" said the Mayor. "I don't care."

The Piper played a sweet, happy song. Now the children came out of the houses. They ran and laughed and clapped. They all followed the Piper down the street. The Mayor and his men stood like stones.

He walked to a hill, the Koppelberg. A door opened in the hill. The Piper went in, and the children too. Then the door closed. The children never came back.

Only one boy stayed outside. He had a bad leg. "I was too slow," he said sadly. "Now I am alone." Nobody played music in that street again.

## Level A2

Long ago, Hamelin stood beside the Weser. It was a busy town in the land of Brunswick. But one summer, the town had a terrible problem. Rats came, thousands and thousands of them.

They fought the dogs and chased the cats. They ate the cheese and drank the soup from the pots. Some even made nests in the men's best hats. Nobody could sleep at night.

At last, the angry people ran to the town hall. "Our Mayor does nothing!" they shouted. "Find an answer, or you must go!" The Mayor and his men sat inside and worried. "What can we do?" the Mayor asked.

Just then, there was a soft knock at the door. A strange man walked into the room. He was tall and thin, with sharp blue eyes. His long coat was half yellow and half red. A long pipe hung around his neck. "People call me the Piper," he said. "With my music, I can lead your rats away." "My price is a thousand gold coins." "A thousand?" cried the Mayor. "We can give you fifty thousand!"

The Piper stepped out into the street and smiled. He put the pipe to his lips and began to play. Then rats poured out of every house. There were brown rats and black rats, fat rats and thin rats. The Piper walked down to the Weser. The rats ran after him and jumped into the deep water. The river carried them all away.

The church bells rang all over Hamelin. People laughed and danced in the streets. Then the Piper came back to the town hall. "Please give me my coins now," he said.

The Mayor and his men did not want to pay. "The rats are in the river," laughed the Mayor. "Why pay so much?" "Take fifty coins, my friend." The Piper's face grew dark. "You made a promise," he said quietly. "I have another song for people like you." "Play your silly pipe all day!" laughed the Mayor.

Once more the Piper stepped into the street. This time he played the sweetest music in the world. Doors opened, and small feet ran over the stones. All the children of Hamelin came out, laughing and clapping. They danced after the Piper down the street. The Mayor and his men stood still, like stones. They could not move.

The Piper did not go to the river this time. He walked toward a big hill, the Koppelberg. "He can't cross the hill," the parents thought. "Soon he must stop." But at the hill, a door opened in its side. The Piper went inside, and the children followed him. Then the door closed, and the hill was quiet. The children never came home again.

Only one boy stayed outside. He had a bad leg and could not keep up. "The Piper told us about a wonderful land," he said sadly. "There, nobody is ever sick." "But I was too slow, and now I am alone." The Mayor sent men with gold to find the Piper. But nobody found him. From then on, nobody played music in the Piper's street.

## Level B1

Hamelin was a pretty town in Brunswick, on the banks of the wide river Weser. Its people were comfortable and quite proud of their clean streets. But one terrible summer, long ago, an army of rats arrived. Nobody knew where they came from, and soon they were everywhere.

They fought the dogs and chased the cats out of the kitchens. They climbed into the babies' beds and ate the cheese straight from the shelves. Some licked soup from the cooks' spoons or made nests in the men's best hats. At night their squeaking was so loud that nobody could hear a word.

In the end, the people marched angrily to the town hall. "Our Mayor is a fool, and his council is no better!" they shouted. "They sit in their fine robes while we suffer!" "Get rid of the rats, or we will throw you all out!" Inside, the Mayor and his council were sitting around a long table without a plan. "I am tired of thinking," the Mayor sighed, rubbing his head.

At that moment, someone tapped gently on the door. "Come in!" called the Mayor, and a very strange figure walked into the room. He was tall and thin, with sharp blue eyes and long, light hair. His long coat was half yellow and half red. A thin pipe hung around his neck. "I am the Piper," said the stranger with a bow. "I play a secret tune, and every creature that hears it follows me." "In the east I once saved a great king from a huge cloud of flies." "For a thousand gold coins, I will take away all your rats." "A thousand?" cried the Mayor. "We will give you fifty thousand!"

The Piper stepped into the street and played three high, sweet notes. Suddenly a sound like distant thunder came from every house. Out tumbled the rats: great rats and small rats, brown, black and gray, young and old. Whole families of them ran after the Piper, street after street. He led them down to the Weser, and the rats jumped into the water. Not one of them came out again, except a single strong rat. It swam across to the far bank and later told its friends a strange story. "The music sounded like apples and cheese and butter," it said, "and I could not stop!"

All the bells of Hamelin rang until the church towers shook. "Get long sticks and break up the old nests!" ordered the Mayor happily. Just then the Piper appeared in the market square. "First, please, my thousand gold coins," he said politely.

The Mayor's face turned pale, and so did the faces of his council. They were planning to spend that money on wine and big dinners. "My good man," said the Mayor with a wink. "We all saw the rats go into the river." "They are not coming back, are they?" "Our talk about a thousand coins was only a joke." "Here are fifty coins, so you can buy yourself a nice drink." The Piper stopped smiling. "I have no time for games," he said coldly. "Keep your word, or you will hear a different tune." "Are you threatening me?" shouted the Mayor. "Play until your pipe breaks!"

Once more the Piper stepped into the street and lifted his long pipe. This time the music was softer and sweeter than any sound in the world. Then came a happy noise of running feet, clapping hands and laughing voices. Out of every door the little boys and girls of Hamelin came running. With bright faces and shining hair, they danced after the Piper. The Mayor and his council stood like stones and could not move or call out.

The parents were terrified when the Piper turned toward the river. But he walked west instead, toward the steep side of the Koppelberg. "He can never climb that hill," they told each other. "He must stop there, and then our children will come home." Yet at the hill, a great door opened in its side, like magic. The Piper went in, and every child followed him into the dark. Then the door closed behind them, and the hillside was silent.

Only one boy did not go through the door. He had a bad leg and was not able to dance as fast as the others. "The Piper was taking us to a beautiful land," he said sadly. "He told us about sweeter fruit, brighter birds and no pain at all." "But the door closed before I got there, and now I am alone."

The Mayor sent people in every direction, offering silver and gold. It was too late, and the children never returned. Nobody in Hamelin ever saw them again. From that day on, nobody played music in the street where the children disappeared.

## Level B2

On the banks of the Weser, in the old duchy of Brunswick, stood the town of Hamelin. It was a pleasant place with steep roofs, a busy market and a deep, wide river washing its southern wall. In the summer of 1376, however, Hamelin was struck by a disaster nobody could have imagined. Rats had arrived, and not in their dozens but in their thousands.

They fought the dogs, chased the cats out of the kitchens and climbed into babies' cradles. They ate the cheese straight from the barrels and licked the soup from the cooks' spoons. Some broke open the barrels of salted fish, while others built their nests inside the men's Sunday hats. When the women tried to talk to each other, they could hardly be heard above the endless squeaking.

At last the townspeople lost patience and crowded into the square in front of the town hall. "Our Mayor is useless," someone shouted, "and our council is even worse!" "They wear fine fur robes and eat well, but they can't solve the one problem that matters!" "If they don't find a cure for these rats, we'll send all of them away!" Inside the council room, the Mayor and his men had been arguing for an hour without reaching any decision. "I'd sell my robe for a good idea," the Mayor groaned, holding his aching head.

At that very moment there was a gentle tapping at the door. The Mayor jumped, half afraid that it was another rat, and nervously told the visitor to enter. The strangest figure he had ever seen stepped into the room. The man was tall and thin, with sharp blue eyes, light hair and a smooth face without a beard. His long coat was divided down the middle, one half bright yellow and the other bright red. Around his neck hung a slim pipe, and his fingers kept moving as if they wanted to play it.

"Please forgive me for interrupting," said the stranger with a polite bow. "People call me the Piper, and I have a secret skill." "With my music, I can draw after me any living creature, whether it crawls, swims, flies or runs." "Last summer I freed a great ruler in the east from a cloud of biting flies." "After that, I rid another country of its bats." "If I free Hamelin from its rats, will you pay me a thousand gold coins?" "A thousand?" cried the Mayor and his council together. "We'll give you fifty thousand!"

The Piper stepped out into the street, smiling to himself as if he knew a secret. He raised the pipe to his lips and blew three thin, high notes. Immediately a low rumbling began, as though an army were marching through the town. Then the rats came pouring out of every doorway, cellar and drain. Great rats and small rats, brown, black and gray, fathers, mothers, uncles and cousins all ran after the music. From street to street the Piper led them, until he reached the Weser. There the rats plunged into the river after him and were swept away by the current. Only one rat, as strong as a soldier, managed to swim across to the far bank. Later he told his relatives that the music had sounded like apples being crushed and barrels of butter being opened.

In Hamelin the church bells rang until the towers shook. "Get long poles," the Mayor shouted, "and poke out every nest, so that not a single trace of them is left!" Just then the Piper appeared in the marketplace, his coat bright against the gray stones. "First," he said quietly, "if you please, my thousand gold coins."

The Mayor turned pale, and so did every member of the council. They had already planned to spend the money on fine wine and long dinners. Besides, a thousand coins seemed a ridiculous price for a few minutes of music. "My dear fellow," said the Mayor with a knowing wink, "we saw the rats drown with our own eyes." "What is dead can't come back to life, so surely you understood that we were only joking." "Here are fifty coins, which is very generous, and you can buy yourself something nice to drink."

The Piper's smile disappeared. "I have no time to waste," he said. "A ruler in the east is waiting for me, and his kitchens are full of scorpions." "I didn't bargain with him, and I won't bargain with you." "Keep your promise, or you'll hear me play a very different tune." "Are you threatening us?" roared the Mayor. "Play until your fingers ache, for all I care!"

Once again the Piper stepped into the street and put the long pipe to his lips. This time he played three notes so soft and sweet that no musician had ever drawn such a sound from any instrument. At once the town filled with the sound of small shoes on stone, clapping hands and excited laughter. Out of every house the children came running, the boys and girls of Hamelin with rosy cheeks and bright hair. Skipping and dancing, they followed the music down the street. The Mayor and his council stood as still as statues, unable to move a step or shout a single word.

The parents watched in horror as the Piper turned towards the Weser, where the rats had disappeared. But at the last moment he turned west and headed for the Koppelberg, the great hill above the town. "He can't possibly cross that hill," people whispered with relief. "He'll have to stop, and our children will come back to us." Yet when the Piper reached the slope, a wide door suddenly opened in the mountainside. In went the Piper, and in went the children after him. When the last of them was inside, the door shut fast. The hillside was as smooth and silent as before.

Only one child was left behind: a little boy who had a bad leg and could not keep up. For years afterwards he missed his friends and complained that the town was dull. "The Piper promised us a joyful land close by," he used to say. "There the fruit was sweeter, the bees had no stings, and nobody ever felt pain." "I was about to see it when the music stopped and the door closed, leaving me alone outside."

The Mayor sent messengers in every direction, promising silver and gold to anyone who could bring the children home. But it was useless, and the boys and girls were never seen again. The street through which they danced was named after the Piper, and from that day no music was played there. The people also wrote the story on a stone pillar and painted it on a church window.

## Level C1

Hamelin, a town of steep gables and prosperous merchants in the duchy of Brunswick, lay beside the Weser, a river deep and broad enough to wash the foot of its walls. Few places could have seemed safer or more comfortable, which made what happened in the summer of 1376 all the more humiliating. The rats came, and once they had come, there was no getting rid of them.

They fought the dogs and won; they hunted the cats through the kitchens; they climbed into cradles and nibbled at whatever they found there. Cheese vanished from the barrels, soup was licked from the very spoons of the cooks, and the barrels of salted fish were gnawed open one by one. Nests appeared in the men's Sunday hats, and the women, gossiping at their doors, could no longer hear one another above the shrill, ceaseless squeaking.

Patience, even in a town as patient as Hamelin, has its limits. One morning the citizens crowded into the square before the town hall and made their feelings plain. "We pay for a Mayor who wears fur and dines on the best, and a council who do the same," they shouted, "and what do we get for it?" "Rouse yourselves and find a cure, or we will turn the whole lot of you out of office!" Inside, the Mayor and his council had been talking in circles for an hour, each of them hoping someone else would think of something. "I would gladly give my gown for a single good idea," the Mayor muttered, pressing his fingers against his aching forehead.

It was at that moment that someone tapped softly at the council door. The Mayor started, half convinced it was a rat, and it was in a voice smaller than he would have liked that he called for the visitor to come in. What came in was the oddest figure any of them had ever seen. He was tall and thin, with keen blue eyes, pale hair and a smooth face that seemed to be on the point of smiling. His long coat was cut down the middle, half of it bright yellow and half bright red, and from a ribbon at his neck hung a slender pipe. Though he stood perfectly still, his fingers kept straying towards it, as if they could hardly wait to begin.

"Forgive the interruption," he said with a graceful bow. "I am known as the Piper, and I possess a certain secret." "By means of a tune that only I can play, I can draw after me any creature that creeps, swims, flies or runs." "Not long ago I rid a great ruler in the east of a plague of gnats, and soon afterwards I cleared a whole country of its bats." "Should I free your town of its rats, would you pay me a thousand gold coins?" "A thousand?" cried the Mayor and the council in one breath. "We'd pay you fifty thousand!"

The Piper stepped out into the street with the faint smile of a man who knows exactly what is about to happen. He set the pipe to his lips and blew three thin, piercing notes, and almost at once a murmur began to rise from every corner of the town. It grew into a rumble, the rumble into a roar, and out of cellars, doorways and drains the rats came tumbling. Great rats and small rats, lean rats and stout ones, gray, brown and black, grandfathers and grandchildren, whole families moved together after the music. Street by street the Piper led them on until he reached the Weser, where they flung themselves into the water and were swept away. Only one rat, as stout as a soldier, struggled across to the far bank. Later, among his own kind, he described the music as the sound of apples being pressed and butter barrels being opened, the promise of a world made entirely of food.

Back in Hamelin the bells were rung until the steeples rocked. The Mayor, suddenly brave again, called for long poles to poke out the nests and for builders to block up the holes, so that not a trace of the invaders would remain. In the middle of this happy bustle the Piper appeared once more in the marketplace, his coat bright against the gray stones. "First, if you please," he said, "my thousand gold coins."

A thousand coins: the words fell on the council like cold water. Already, in their minds, the money had become fine wine and long dinners, and handing it to a wandering musician in a ridiculous coat seemed nothing short of absurd. "My dear fellow," said the Mayor, winking as if they shared a joke, "we all saw the rats go down into the river." "What is drowned stays drowned, and not one of those rats is going to climb back out of the Weser." "You can hardly have taken our little joke about the money seriously." "Here are fifty coins, which is more than generous, and you may drink our health with them."

The smile went out of the Piper's face as a candle goes out in a draft. "I have no time for this," he said. "A ruler in the east has sent for me, for his kitchens are crawling with scorpions." "I did not haggle with him, and I shall not haggle with you." "Keep your word, or you will find that I know other tunes besides the one you heard this morning." "Threats, from a vagabond in a coat of two colors?" roared the Mayor. "Pipe away until your fingers ache, for all I care!"

Without another word the Piper stepped back into the street and lifted the pipe to his lips. The three notes he played this time were softer than any that had ever been drawn from an instrument, and so sweet that the air itself seemed to listen. There followed a sound the town knew well and had never heard like this: the patter of small shoes on stone, the clapping of hands, and laughter breaking out at every door. Out came the children of Hamelin, every boy and every girl, cheeks flushed and eyes shining, skipping and dancing after the music. The Mayor and his council stood as rooted as the posts in the market, unable to stir a foot or utter a sound.

When the Piper turned towards the Weser, a cry of horror went up from the watching parents. He passed the river by, however, and set off westwards towards the Koppelberg, the great hill that rose above the town. "He can never cross that," people told one another, their relief almost as sharp as their fear had been. "He will have to stop at the foot of it, and then we shall have our children back." But as the Piper reached the slope, a wide door opened in the mountainside, as smoothly as if the rock had always been waiting for him. In went the Piper, and in went the children after him, and when the last of them had passed inside, the door closed and left the hillside blank and silent.

Not quite all of them had gone, for one boy with a lame leg had been unable to keep up. For the rest of his life he limped through Hamelin missing his playmates and finding the town unbearably dull. "He promised us a joyful land just beyond the hill," he would say, "where the fruit was sweeter, the bees had lost their stings, and no one was ever lame or ill." "I had almost reached the door when the music stopped, and I was left standing outside, alone."

The Mayor, wiser too late, sent messengers to the east, west, north and south, promising to whoever found the Piper as much silver and gold as he cared to ask. None of them ever came back with news, and the children of Hamelin were never seen again. The street along which they had danced was given the Piper's name, and no musician has been allowed to play there since. The whole story was carved into a pillar and painted on a window of the great church, so that the town could never pretend it had not happened. Travelers say that far beyond the mountains there lives a people whose customs puzzle their neighbors. They claim, it is said, that their ancestors once came up out of the earth.
