# Gulliver in Lilliput

Summary: A shipwrecked doctor wakes up on a strange beach, tied down by hundreds of tiny people.

## Level A1

Gulliver was a doctor on a ship. On a long trip, a big storm came. The ship hit a rock and broke. Gulliver swam and swam in the dark sea. At last, his feet touched sand. He lay down on the grass and slept.

In the morning, he could not move. Hundreds of thin ropes held him down. Something walked on his leg. It was a very small man! He was not as tall as a finger. Forty more little men came after him. They shot tiny arrows at his hands.

Gulliver put a finger to his mouth. "I am very hungry," he said. The little people brought bread and meat. They brought small barrels of wine too. He ate ten tiny loaves at once. This land was Lilliput.

The Emperor of Lilliput came to see him. Gulliver was always kind and gentle. Children played games in his hair. People called him the Man-Mountain. Soon the Emperor let him walk free.

Lilliput had an enemy across the sea. It was the island of Blefuscu. Blefuscu had many warships. One night, Gulliver walked into the sea. The water came up to his chest. He put a hook on every ship. Then he pulled all the ships to Lilliput.

The Emperor was very happy. But then he wanted more. "Bring me all of Blefuscu!" he said. "Its people must be my slaves." Gulliver said no. "They are free people," he said. The Emperor was angry with him.

Gulliver had a friend at court. The friend came to him late at night. "Your enemies want to hurt you," he said. "You must go now." So Gulliver walked to Blefuscu.

There, he found a real boat. It was a boat for a big man! He fixed it and sailed away. After two days, a big ship found him. At last, Gulliver came home to England. In his pocket were tiny cows and sheep.

## Level A2

Lemuel Gulliver was a ship's doctor from England. In 1699, he went to sea on the Antelope. A terrible storm hit the ship, and it broke on a rock. Gulliver swam for hours in the dark water. At last, his feet touched sand. He lay down on the soft grass and slept.

When he woke up, he could not move. Thin ropes held his arms and legs to the ground. Then something small walked up his body. It was a tiny man with a bow and arrow! He was not as tall as a finger. Forty more little soldiers came behind him.

Gulliver gave a great shout, and they ran away. Soon they came back and shot arrows at him. The arrows felt like needles in his hands. So he decided to lie still and wait. He put a finger to his mouth again and again. The little people climbed up ladders with baskets of food. He ate their tiny loaves three at a time.

The country was Lilliput, and its people were about six inches tall. Many small horses pulled Gulliver on a big cart to Mildendo. There the soldiers put chains on his leg. The Emperor came to see the giant. Teachers visited every day, and Gulliver learned their language.

Gulliver was always gentle with the little people. Children played in his hair. Everyone called him the Man-Mountain. But Flimnap, the Emperor's treasurer, did not like him. Gulliver ate too much food, and it cost a lot! After some months, the Emperor set him free. But Gulliver must help Lilliput in war.

Lilliput and Blefuscu were at war, and the reason was eggs! In Lilliput, people broke their eggs at the small end. Many people in Blefuscu broke them at the big end. Now Blefuscu had a big fleet of warships.

Gulliver made fifty strong hooks and fifty long ropes. At night, he walked through the sea to Blefuscu. The enemy sailors saw him and jumped into the water. They shot arrows at his face, but his glasses saved his eyes. He put a hook on each ship. Then he cut the anchor ropes with his knife. He pulled the whole fleet home to Lilliput.

The people of Lilliput cheered for him. But the Emperor wanted more. "Go back and bring me their other ships," he said. "Blefuscu's people must be my slaves." Gulliver said no. "I can't make a free people into slaves," he answered. The Emperor never forgot this.

Late one night, Reldresal, a friend at court, came to his house. "Your enemies want to punish you badly," he said. "You must leave soon." Gulliver crossed the sea to Blefuscu, and its Emperor welcomed him.

A few days later, he saw something far out in the water. It was a real boat, big enough for him! Gulliver mended it with help from the people of Blefuscu. Then he sailed away with some tiny cows and sheep. An English ship found him at sea and took him home. In England, he showed his little animals to everyone.

## Level B1

Lemuel Gulliver was an English doctor who worked on ships. In 1699, he joined the crew of the Antelope and sailed for the South Seas. For some weeks the voyage went well. Then a violent storm drove the ship onto a rock. It broke apart in minutes. Gulliver swam for his life until his feet finally touched the bottom. Tired, he lay down on the soft grass and slept.

When he opened his eyes, he could not move. Hundreds of thin strings were holding his arms, legs and hair. Soon he felt something moving along his left leg and up onto his chest. Looking down, he saw a tiny human, less than six inches tall. He was holding a bow and arrow, and forty others were following him.

Gulliver gave a roar of surprise, and they all ran away. When he pulled one arm free, they fired a storm of arrows at him. The arrows stung like needles, so he decided to lie quietly and wait. Soon an official stood on a platform beside his head and made a long speech. Gulliver did not understand a word. Instead, he pointed to his mouth to show his hunger. The people brought baskets of meat and bread. They also gave him wine that had a sleeping medicine in it.

While he slept, five hundred workers built a huge wooden cart. Using ropes and machines, nine hundred strong men lifted him onto it. Then fifteen hundred horses pulled him to Mildendo, the capital of Lilliput. There they locked his leg to an old temple with tiny chains.

The Emperor of Lilliput came to look at his giant prisoner. He was a little taller than his people, which made them respect him. Every day teachers came, and Gulliver slowly learned their language. Two officers searched his pockets and made a careful list. They described his watch as a noisy machine. They thought it was probably his god, because he looked at it so often.

Gulliver was always gentle, and soon nobody was afraid of him. Children played hide-and-seek in his hair. People called him the Man-Mountain. At last the Emperor agreed to set him free under certain conditions. He promised to help Lilliput against its enemies and to watch where he stepped. Not everyone was happy, however. Flimnap, the Emperor's treasurer, complained about the huge cost of feeding him.

Reldresal, a secretary and a good friend, told Gulliver about a long war. Years before, an emperor ordered everyone to break their eggs at the small end. Many people refused and escaped to the island of Blefuscu. Since then the two empires had fought many wars. Now Blefuscu was preparing a great fleet to attack Lilliput.

Gulliver had a plan. He made fifty strong hooks and tied each one to a long cable. That night he walked into the sea and headed toward Blefuscu. In the middle, the water was deep, so he swam for a short distance. The enemy sailors saw him and jumped into the water in terror. Thousands of arrows flew at him, but his glasses protected his eyes. He fixed a hook to every ship and cut the anchor cables with his knife. Then he pulled the whole fleet back to Lilliput.

The Emperor greeted him and gave him the highest title in the land. However, the Emperor wanted much more. "Go back and bring me the rest of their ships," he ordered. "I will rule Blefuscu, and its people will be my slaves." Gulliver refused. "I will never help to turn a free and brave people into slaves," he said. From that moment, the Emperor's love for him began to turn cold.

Flimnap and his friends at court began to plot against Gulliver. They called him a traitor and planned a cruel punishment. One night Reldresal visited him secretly and warned him about the danger. "You must go before they act," he whispered. The next morning Gulliver walked across the sea to Blefuscu. The Emperor there welcomed him kindly.

A few days later, he noticed something floating far out in the water. It was a real boat, and it was the right size for him! With help from the people of Blefuscu, he repaired it and made sails. He set off with food, water and a few tiny cows and sheep. After two days, an English ship picked him up. Back in England, he showed his little animals to visitors.

## Level B2

Lemuel Gulliver, a ship's surgeon from England, had always found it hard to stay at home for long. In the spring of 1699 he joined the crew of the Antelope, which was bound for the South Seas. For months the voyage went smoothly, until a violent storm drove the ship off course. In thick fog, the Antelope struck a rock and split in two. Gulliver and five companions escaped in a small boat, but a sudden wave turned it over. He never saw the others again. Pushed by wind and tide, Gulliver swam until his strength was almost gone. Then, to his great relief, his feet touched the bottom. He waded to the shore, found no sign of people, and lay down on the short grass.

When he woke at daylight, he discovered that he could not move a muscle. His arms, his legs and even his long thick hair had been fastened to the ground with countless thin threads. Something alive was creeping up his left leg and over his chest, almost to his chin. Straining his eyes downward, he saw a human being hardly six inches high, armed with a bow and arrow. At least forty more followed close behind.

Gulliver's astonished roar sent them tumbling to the ground in fright. When he managed to tear one arm loose, however, they answered with a shower of tiny arrows. They stung his skin like needles, and he judged it wiser to lie still and see what would happen. Soon a person of importance climbed onto a little stage beside his head and made a long speech. Gulliver understood nothing, but he made it clear by signs that he was starving. Ladders were set against his sides, and soon a hundred little people were marching up with baskets of food. He swallowed their loaves three at a time and drained two barrels of their wine.

The wine, however, had been mixed with a sleeping drug. While he slept, the Emperor's engineers brought up a wooden platform on twenty-two wheels. Using ropes and pulleys, nine hundred of the strongest men lifted him onto it. Fifteen hundred of the Emperor's largest horses then dragged him to Mildendo, the capital. There they chained him by the left leg inside an ancient temple, the only building large enough to hold him.

The Emperor of Lilliput, taller than his subjects by about the width of a fingernail, came to inspect his prisoner. He ordered that the giant should be fed at public cost and taught the language. Two officers were sent to search Gulliver's pockets, and they recorded everything in a solemn report. His comb was described as a fence with long poles, and his watch as a mysterious engine with a ceaseless noise. They concluded that it was either an unknown animal or his god, since he rarely did anything without looking at it.

Gulliver's gentleness gradually won over the court. Once, when a few rough men shot arrows at him, the soldiers handed six of them over to him for punishment. He lifted one of them towards his open mouth, as if he meant to eat him. Then he cut the man's bonds with his penknife and set him gently on the ground. The crowd was delighted by his mercy. Before long, children were playing hide-and-seek in his hair, and the people were calling him the Man-Mountain.

After many requests, the Emperor agreed to give him his liberty on certain conditions. He promised to fight for Lilliput and to watch his feet in the streets. Flimnap, the treasurer, never stopped complaining about how much the giant ate. It was Reldresal, the chief secretary and a sincere friend, who explained the dangers facing the kingdom. For generations, he said, Lilliput had been torn apart by a quarrel over eggs. Long ago, a young prince had cut his finger while opening an egg at the larger end. His father the emperor then commanded every subject to break eggs at the smaller end. Thousands preferred exile to obedience and fled across the channel to Blefuscu, whose emperor gave them shelter. Now Blefuscu had gathered a great fleet and was preparing to invade.

Gulliver had a better idea than fighting. He ordered fifty strong cables and fifty iron bars, which he bent into hooks. That night he waded into the channel, swimming only where the water was too deep to walk. When the sailors of Blefuscu saw a mountain rising out of the sea, they leaped overboard and swam for the shore. Their arrows rained on his face, and he would surely have been blinded if he had not been wearing his spectacles. Calmly, he fastened a hook to the front of each warship and cut the anchor ropes with his knife. Then, gathering all the cables in one hand, he towed fifty of the enemy's largest ships back to Lilliput.

The Emperor received him on the shore with the highest honor the kingdom could give. Yet the victory only made the ruler greedier. He now demanded every remaining ship, so that he could turn Blefuscu into a province of his empire. Its people would be ruled from Mildendo and forced to break their eggs at the small end. "I will never help to bring a free and brave people into slavery," he declared. The wiser ministers agreed with him, but the Emperor never forgave him.

From then on, Flimnap and his allies plotted to destroy the Man-Mountain. When ambassadors arrived from Blefuscu to make peace, Gulliver treated them kindly, and his enemies called this treason. Late one night, Reldresal came to him in secret and revealed what the council had decided. Charges of treason had been prepared, and the ministers had agreed on a cruel punishment. "You have only three days," the secretary whispered.

Gulliver did not wait. He walked across the channel to Blefuscu, where the emperor and his court welcomed him warmly. Three days later, walking along the coast, he noticed something dark floating far out at sea. His heart leapt when he saw that it was a real boat, large enough for a man of his size. With the help of the emperor's sailors, he towed it ashore, repaired it and fitted it with sails. When Lilliput demanded his return, the emperor of Blefuscu politely refused, and he even offered Gulliver a place in his service. Gulliver thanked him but chose to go home.

He set sail with food and a few tiny cows and sheep. Two days later, an English merchant ship spotted him and took him on board. The captain did not believe his story until Gulliver took the little animals out of his pocket. Back in England, he earned a good sum by showing them to curious visitors. Many people laughed at his tale, but nobody could explain the tiny sheep behind his house.

## Level C1

Lemuel Gulliver, a surgeon by training and a wanderer by temperament, had tried more than once to settle into a quiet practice in England. Each time, the income proved too small for his family and the quiet too heavy for his restless mind. So, in the spring of 1699, he signed on as ship's surgeon aboard the Antelope, bound for the South Seas. For some months the voyage was uneventful, which is the best thing a sailor can say of any voyage. Then a storm of extraordinary violence drove the ship far off its course, and in a thick fog she ran onto a rock and split apart. Gulliver and five companions lowered the boat and rowed until their arms gave out, only for a sudden gust to overturn them. Of the other five he never learned anything more.

He swam on alone, pushed by wind and tide, until, at the very moment his strength failed, his feet struck solid ground. Wading nearly half a mile to the beach, he found neither house nor human being. Weaker than he had ever been, he lay down on the short, soft grass and slept more deeply than he could remember.

He woke to bright sunlight and a most peculiar sensation of being held. Not only his arms and legs but every strand of his long hair had been pinned to the earth with fine threads, stretched across his body from shoulders to thighs. Presently something alive crept along his left leg, advanced across his chest and stopped just short of his chin. Straining his eyes downward, he made out a human creature not six inches tall, with a bow in its hands and a quiver on its back. About forty more of the same kind followed it. When he wrenched one arm free, however, the creatures recovered their nerve with remarkable speed, and a hundred arrows struck his hand like so many needles. It crossed his mind that one sweep of his free hand could scatter the entire army, but prudence advised him to lie quiet and wait.

Before long a little stage rose beside his right ear, and a person of evident rank mounted it to deliver a lengthy oration. Gulliver understood not a word, but the tone, by turns threatening and gracious, was clear enough. He replied in the only language available, putting a finger repeatedly to his mouth. Ladders were set against his sides, and over a hundred of the inhabitants climbed up with baskets of meat and loaves no larger than musket balls. He ate them three at a time and emptied two barrels of wine, while the crowd below cheered every swallow. Having eaten their bread, he felt bound by the laws of hospitality not to harm them, which was, perhaps, exactly what they had intended. Only later did he grasp the full extent of their foresight: the wine had been mixed with a sleeping potion, on the Emperor's orders.

What followed was a masterpiece of engineering on a miniature scale. Five hundred carpenters assembled a wooden frame on twenty-two wheels, and nine hundred of the strongest men hoisted the sleeping giant onto it with pulleys. Fifteen hundred of the Emperor's largest horses, each some four and a half inches high, then hauled him towards Mildendo, the capital. There, in an abandoned temple, he was fastened by the left leg with ninety-one chains, like a great ship moored to a harbor wall.

The Emperor himself came to inspect the prisoner. He stood taller than his courtiers by almost the breadth of a fingernail, a difference quite sufficient to inspire awe in all who saw him. Teachers were appointed, and within weeks Gulliver could converse in the language of Lilliput. Two officers searched his pockets and drew up a solemn inventory, in which his comb became a fence of poles and his watch an engine that roared like a water mill. They concluded, with great learning, that the watch was either an unknown animal or the god he prayed to, since he seldom did anything without consulting it.

Gulliver's mildness slowly won the court over. When a few ruffians in the crowd shot arrows at him, the soldiers delivered six of them into his hands for punishment. He raised one of them towards his open mouth, as though to swallow him whole, and let the poor fellow shriek for a moment. Then he cut the man's bonds with a penknife and set him down, releasing the other five in the same fashion. Before long, children were playing hide-and-seek in his hair, and the whole nation had begun to speak fondly of the Man-Mountain.

The court itself he found more puzzling than its people. Those who sought high office, he learned, were required to dance on a slender rope before the Emperor, and whoever leapt highest without falling won the post. Flimnap, the treasurer, could leap on the tightrope a full inch higher than any other lord, and he never ceased to count what the giant's appetite was costing. At length the Emperor granted Gulliver his liberty, on condition that he keep to the main roads, mind his feet, and fight for Lilliput against its enemies.

It was Reldresal, the principal secretary and one of the few genuine friends Gulliver made at court, who explained who those enemies were. For generations, he confided, the empire had been divided by a quarrel about the correct way to break an egg. A former emperor, having cut his finger as a boy while opening an egg at the larger end, had commanded all his subjects to open theirs at the smaller end. Thousands had preferred exile to obedience and fled across the channel to Blefuscu, and the two empires had been at war ever since. Now Blefuscu, Reldresal said gravely, had gathered a great fleet and was waiting only for a favorable wind.

Gulliver proposed a solution that required neither soldiers nor ships. He asked for fifty of the strongest cables in the kingdom and fifty iron bars, which he twisted into hooks, and that same night he waded into the channel. Only in the middle, for some thirty yards, was the water deep enough to make him swim. When the sailors of Blefuscu saw a mountain rising out of the sea, they leapt overboard in terror. Their arrows rained upon his face and hands, and he would certainly have lost his eyes had he not remembered the spectacles in his pocket. Calmly, as though mooring a fishing boat, he fixed a hook to the bow of each warship and cut its anchor cable with his knife. Then, holding all fifty lines in one fist, he waded home to Lilliput, towing the pride of the enemy's navy behind him like toys on a string.

The Emperor met him on the shore and conferred on him the highest title the realm could offer. Victory, however, has a way of sharpening an appetite rather than satisfying it. His Majesty now proposed that Gulliver fetch the remaining ships, so that Blefuscu could be reduced to a province ruled from Mildendo. Its people, naturally, would be compelled to break their eggs at the proper end. Gulliver protested, as plainly as courtesy allowed, that he would never be the instrument of bringing a free and brave people into slavery. Several of the wiser ministers privately agreed with him; the Emperor never forgave him. So little, Gulliver observed, do the greatest services weigh against a single refusal.

From that day Flimnap and his allies worked steadily to bring the Man-Mountain down. When ambassadors from Blefuscu arrived to negotiate peace, Gulliver received them with ordinary courtesy, and even this was entered against him as evidence of treason. Late one night Reldresal visited him in a closed chair and laid out the charges the council had prepared. Some ministers had demanded his death; the Emperor, anxious to be known for his mercy, preferred to have his sight taken from him and his rations slowly reduced. This, the secretary explained without a trace of irony, was considered a remarkable act of kindness.

Gulliver did not stay to test the Emperor's kindness. He waded across the channel to Blefuscu, whose emperor received him with every courtesy. A few days later, walking along the coast, he noticed something dark drifting far out at sea. With a leap of the heart he recognized it as a real boat, overturned and probably torn from some passing ship in a storm. Workmen from Blefuscu helped him drag it ashore and repair it, while he stitched sails from layers of their finest linen. When an envoy from Lilliput arrived to demand the prisoner, the emperor of Blefuscu replied with exquisite politeness that the giant was leaving anyway.

On the twenty-fourth of September 1701, he set sail with food, a few tiny cows and sheep, and the strange feeling of having been both a giant and a prisoner. Two days later an English merchant ship took him aboard. The captain listened to his story with polite disbelief, until Gulliver drew a living cow from his pocket and set it on the table. Back in England, he made a tidy profit by showing his little herd to the curious, and no one who watched those sheep grazing ever doubted him again.
