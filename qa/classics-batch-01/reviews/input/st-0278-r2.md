# Don Quixote and the Windmills

Summary: A gentleman of La Mancha reads too many old books and rides out to become a knight.

## Level A1

A thin old gentleman lived in La Mancha. His name was Alonso Quixano. He had an old horse and a dog. Every day, he read books about knights. He read late into the night. Soon his head was full of giants.

One morning, he had a big idea. "I am a knight now!" he said. He found some old armor at home. It was dirty, so he cleaned it. He chose a new name for himself. "I am Don Quixote of La Mancha!" His horse got a name too: Rocinante.

A knight needs a lady to love. He thought of a farm girl in Toboso. He never spoke to her. But he called her Dulcinea of Toboso. To him, she was a great lady.

Don Quixote rode out alone on Rocinante. His horse fell, and some men hit him. A kind neighbor took him home. His friends burned many of his books.

His neighbor Sancho Panza was a poor farmer. Sancho was short, round and kind. "Be my helper, Sancho," said Don Quixote. "I can give you an island," he said. Sancho wanted an island very much. So they left the village at night. Sancho rode on his donkey.

Soon they saw thirty or forty windmills. "Look! Giants with long arms!" cried Don Quixote. "No, sir," said Sancho. "They are windmills." "The arms are only sails," he said. Don Quixote did not listen.

He rode fast at the first windmill. Just then, the wind turned the sails. A sail hit him and his horse. They fell hard on the ground. Sancho ran to help him. "I told you, sir!" he said.

Don Quixote sat up slowly. "Freston the wizard did this," he said. Sancho did not understand. "He changed the giants into windmills!" he cried. Sancho helped him back onto Rocinante. The poor horse was hurt too. Then they rode on toward Puerto Lapice. "We can find adventures there," said Don Quixote.

## Level A2

In a village in La Mancha, there lived an old gentleman. His name was Alonso Quixano, and he was nearly fifty. He was tall and thin, with a long, dry face. He lived with his housekeeper, his young niece and an old horse.

This gentleman loved books about knights more than anything. He read them from morning until late at night. He even sold some of his land to buy more books. Because he read so much and slept so little, his mind changed. Soon he believed every story in his books.

Then he had a strange idea. "I can be a knight and help people," he said. In a corner, he found his great-grandfather's old armor. The helmet had no front, so he made one from cardboard.

His horse was old and bony, but he was proud of it. After four days, he gave it a name: Rocinante. Then he named himself Don Quixote of La Mancha. A knight also needs a lady, so he remembered a farm girl. She lived in Toboso, and he never spoke a word to her. But he gave her a grand name: Dulcinea of Toboso.

Early one hot morning, Don Quixote rode out alone. At an inn, the owner made him a knight as a joke. Some travelers on the road beat him, and he could not stand. A neighbor found him and brought him home on a donkey. While he slept, his friends burned most of his books.

But Don Quixote did not change his plan. He went to his neighbor Sancho Panza, a poor farmer. "Come with me as my helper," he said. "One day I can make you ruler of an island," he promised. Sancho loved this idea. So one night, without a word to anyone, the two men left. Sancho rode on his donkey, and Don Quixote rode on Rocinante.

Soon they came to a wide plain with thirty or forty windmills. "Look, Sancho, giants!" cried Don Quixote. "I must fight them!" "What giants?" asked Sancho. "Those over there, with the long arms," said Don Quixote. "Sir, those are windmills," said Sancho. "The arms are just sails." "You know nothing about adventures," said Don Quixote.

He called to Dulcinea for help and rode at the first windmill. Just then, the wind turned the sails. His spear broke into pieces. The sail threw horse and rider across the field. Sancho hurried to him on his donkey. "Did I not tell you, sir? Those are only windmills!" he said.

"Be quiet, friend Sancho," said Don Quixote. "Freston the wizard did this." "He changed the giants into windmills," he went on. "But my sword can beat his magic in the end," he added. Sancho helped him up onto Rocinante. The horse was hurt too, and it walked slowly. They talked about the adventure as they rode. The next afternoon, they saw Puerto Lapice in front of them. "There we can find many adventures," said Don Quixote. And so the two friends rode on together.

## Level B1

In a village in La Mancha, there lived a gentleman of about fifty years. His name was Alonso Quixano, and he had little money but plenty of free time. He lived with a housekeeper, a niece and a boy who helped in the fields. He was thin and strong, he got up early, and he loved hunting.

His real love, however, was reading old books about knights. He read them so often that he forgot his hunting and his farm. He even sold good fields to buy more of these books. The village priest and the barber often argued with him about the best knight. Day and night he read, and he slept less and less. In the end, his brain dried up, and he lost his good sense. He believed that every giant, castle and magic sword in the books was real.

So he decided to become a knight himself. He wanted to ride across the world, fight evil and help the weak. First he cleaned some old armor from the days of his great-grandfather. The helmet had no front, so he made one from strong paper. When he tested it with his sword, he broke it in one blow. He rebuilt it with thin pieces of iron, and this time he did not test it.

His horse was old and bony, but he thought it was the finest in the world. He spent four days looking for a good name and finally chose Rocinante. He spent eight more days choosing his own name: Don Quixote of La Mancha. Every knight needed a lady, so he remembered a pretty farm girl from Toboso. She did not know about his love, but he named her Dulcinea of Toboso.

Early one July morning, before anyone was awake, he rode out alone. That evening he reached a small inn, which looked like a castle to him. The owner was a clever man, and he made Don Quixote a knight as a joke. On the road, Don Quixote tried to fight a group of travelers. Rocinante fell, and one of the men beat the knight as he lay on the ground. A farmer from his village found him and took him home on his donkey.

While Don Quixote was sleeping, the priest and the barber went into his library. With the housekeeper and the niece, they burned most of the books. Then they built a wall across the door of the room. When he woke up, his niece told him a strange story. "An enchanter came on a cloud and took the room away," she said. "That must be Freston, my old enemy," he said.

He stayed at home for fifteen quiet days. But in secret, he was talking to a neighbor, a simple farmer called Sancho Panza. "Come with me as my squire," Don Quixote told him. "I will win an island one day and make you its governor," he promised. Sancho liked the idea of an island very much. One night, without saying goodbye to anyone, they left the village. Don Quixote sat on Rocinante, and Sancho rode behind him on his donkey.

On the plain they saw thirty or forty windmills. "Look at those huge giants, Sancho!" cried Don Quixote. "I am going to fight them all." "What giants?" asked Sancho. "The ones with the long arms over there," his master answered. "Sir, those are windmills," said Sancho, "and the arms are their sails." "You do not understand adventures," said Don Quixote. "Stay here and pray while I fight."

Don Quixote did not listen to Sancho's shouts. He called out to his lady Dulcinea and rode at the first windmill. At that moment the wind turned the great sail. It broke his lance into pieces and threw horse and knight far across the field.

Sancho came as fast as his donkey could go. "Did I not tell you that they were windmills?" he said. "Quiet, friend Sancho," said Don Quixote. "Freston turned these giants into windmills to rob me." "But my good sword will beat his magic in the end," he added. Sancho helped him onto Rocinante, and the poor horse walked slowly.

They took the road to Puerto Lapice, because many people traveled there. Don Quixote did not complain about his pain, because knights never complain. Sancho ate and drank from his bag and felt very happy with his new life. That night Don Quixote stayed awake and thought about Dulcinea, but Sancho slept well. In the afternoon of the next day, they saw Puerto Lapice in front of them. "Here, brother Sancho, we will find adventures," said Don Quixote, and they rode on.

## Level B2

In a village of La Mancha, whose name hardly matters, there lived a gentleman of nearly fifty. He owned an old lance, an ancient shield, a thin horse and a fast hunting dog. Most of his small income went on simple food, and the rest went on his Sunday clothes. His household was made up of a housekeeper past forty, a niece not yet twenty, and a boy for the fields. People said his name was Alonso Quixano, though some called him by other, similar names.

In his free hours, which made up most of the year, he read books of knights and their adventures. He read with such passion that he forgot his hunting and even the care of his land. He sold many acres of good farmland to buy more of these books. With the village priest and the barber, he argued endlessly about which knight had been the greatest. He read from dawn until dark and from dark until dawn, and slept less and less. At last his brain dried up, and he lost his reason completely. Enchantments, battles, wounds and impossible loves filled his head, and he believed every word.

Then he had the strangest idea that any madman ever had. He would become a knight himself and ride through the world on horseback. He would correct every wrong, protect the weak, and win eternal fame. First he cleaned a suit of armor that had belonged to his great-grandfather. Because the helmet lacked a front, he made one from stiff paper. To test it, he struck it with his sword, and the work of a whole week was destroyed. He made it again with strips of iron inside, and he wisely decided not to test it a second time.

Next he went to look at his horse, which was little more than skin and bones. Even so, he thought no famous horse in history could equal it. He spent four days thinking of a name and finally called it Rocinante. He needed eight more days to find a name for himself, and he chose Don Quixote. Like the great knights who added their homeland to their names, he became Don Quixote of La Mancha. Lastly, a knight without a lady was like a tree without leaves. He remembered a good-looking farm girl from nearby Toboso, with whom he had once been a little in love. She had never known it, but he named her Dulcinea of Toboso, a name as musical as his own.

On a hot July morning, without telling anyone, he rode out through the back gate of his yard. At nightfall, he reached an inn, which he took for a castle with towers of silver. The innkeeper saw at once that his guest was mad and agreed to make him a knight in a comic ceremony. On the road home, Don Quixote ordered some merchants to swear that Dulcinea was the most beautiful woman alive. When they laughed, he charged, but Rocinante stumbled and threw him down. One of their servants broke the knight's own lance and beat him with the pieces. A farmer from his village found him lying there and brought him home across his donkey.

While he slept, the priest and the barber examined his library. With the help of the housekeeper and the niece, they burned almost every book. They also bricked up the door so that he would not find the room again. When he woke and searched for his books, his niece told him that an enchanter had carried them away. "That must be the wise Freston," Don Quixote said, "a great enemy of mine."

For fifteen days he stayed quietly at home, but secretly he was talking to a neighbor. Sancho Panza was an honest farmer with a wife and children, good-hearted but not very clever. Don Quixote promised him that one day he might win an island and make him its governor. Sancho was persuaded, and he agreed to leave his family and serve as the knight's squire. One night, without a word to anyone, the two men slipped out of the village. Sancho rode his donkey like an old patriarch, with his bag and his leather wine bottle. On the way, he dreamed of becoming a governor and of his wife becoming a queen.

As they crossed the plain, they discovered thirty or forty windmills. "Luck is on our side today, Sancho, more than we ever hoped," cried Don Quixote. "Look there at those enormous giants, all of whom I intend to fight," he went on. "What giants?" asked Sancho. "Those you see there, with arms almost two leagues long," replied Don Quixote. "Look closely, sir," said Sancho. "Those are windmills, and what look like arms are their sails." "You know nothing about adventures," replied his master. "If you are frightened, go and pray while I fight them."

He spurred Rocinante forward without listening to another word. "Do not run, cowards!" he shouted at the windmills. A little wind began to blow, and the great sails started to move. "Even if you move more arms than the giant Briareus, you will pay for this!" he cried. Asking his lady Dulcinea to help him, he charged the first mill at full speed. The sail turned at that moment and shattered his lance into pieces. It carried both horse and rider with it and dropped them, badly bruised, on the field.

Sancho hurried over as fast as his donkey could trot. "Did I not tell you?" he said. "Anyone could see they were windmills, unless he had windmills in his head." "Quiet, friend Sancho," said Don Quixote. "The affairs of war change constantly." "I believe the same Freston who stole my books has turned these giants into mills," he went on. "He wants to rob me of the glory, but his magic will fail in the end," he added. "May God make it so," said Sancho, and he helped his master back onto Rocinante.

Since his lance was broken, Don Quixote tore a thick branch from an oak to use as a new one. They followed the road to Puerto Lapice, where Don Quixote expected many adventures. He did not complain about his injuries, because knights were not allowed to complain. Sancho, who had no such rule, ate and drank happily as he rode. That night they slept beneath some trees, and Don Quixote stayed awake, thinking of Dulcinea. Sancho, with a full stomach, slept soundly until the sun shone on his face. In the afternoon they caught sight of Puerto Lapice, and the knight lifted his new oak lance. "Here, brother Sancho," he declared, "adventures will come to us faster than we can count them." Then the two of them, the tall knight and his short squire, rode down toward it.

## Level C1

In a village of La Mancha that the storyteller prefers to leave unnamed, there lived, not so long ago, a gentleman of the old, modest sort. He owned a lance gathering dust on its rack, a battered shield, a horse of skin and bone, and a greyhound for the hunt. Plain meals left little of his small income, and that little went on a good coat for feast days. A housekeeper past forty, a niece not yet twenty and a boy who did every job about the place made up his household. Nearly fifty, lean of body and dry of face, he rose early and loved to hunt, and his name, as far as anyone can tell, was Alonso Quixano.

What ruined him was a single, consuming pleasure. In his idle hours, which were most of the year, he read books of chivalry with such devotion that he abandoned his hunting and, soon after, his estate. He sold acre after acre of good farmland to pay for them and carried home every volume he could find. He would quarrel for hours with the village priest and the barber over which hero of the old romances deserved the crown. Night after night he read on into the morning, until, starved of sleep and stuffed with stories, his brain dried out and his reason fled. Enchantments, duels, wounds and hopeless loves swarmed in his head, and no history ever written seemed to him more certain than these fables.

Out of this ruin grew the wildest plan that any lunatic has ever hatched. For his own honor and his country's good, he would turn knight errant and wander the earth in search of adventures. He would mend every injustice, throw himself into every peril, and leave behind a name that no age would forget. First he scrubbed the rust from a suit of armor, once his great-grandfather's, that had lain forgotten in a corner for generations. The helmet lacked a visor, so he fashioned one from pasteboard. Then he tested it with a sword stroke that destroyed in a heartbeat what had cost him a week. Not in the least discouraged, he rebuilt it with iron strips inside and, trusting it completely now, took care never to test it again.

Next he went to consider his horse, whose bones poked out at every angle, and decided that no charger of legend had ever been its equal. For four days he searched for a name worthy of so great a knight's steed, trying names and throwing them away. At last, he chose Rocinante, which seemed to him grand and ringing, and a fair account of what the animal had been and now was. Eight more days went into naming himself, until he settled on Don Quixote. Since the heroes of old had added their homelands to their names, he became Don Quixote of La Mancha. Only a lady was missing, for a knight without someone to love seemed to him as useless as an orchard that never blossoms. In nearby Toboso lived a handsome farm girl with whom he had once, it appears, been a little in love, though she had never known it. He named her Dulcinea of Toboso, a title as musical and as strange as the others he had invented.

Before sunrise on a blazing July day, telling no one, he put on his armor, mounted Rocinante and slipped out through the back gate. At dusk, he reached a roadside inn, and in his eyes it rose up at once as a castle with towers of gleaming silver. The innkeeper, a sly and good-humored man, soon saw what kind of guest had arrived. To keep the peace, he knighted him in a solemn farce, reading aloud from his account book as if it were scripture. On his way home for money and clean shirts, Don Quixote demanded that some merchants swear Dulcinea was the loveliest maiden on earth. When they laughed, he charged in a rage, but Rocinante stumbled, and his master went tumbling across the ground. One of the mule drivers broke the fallen man's lance and thrashed him soundly with the pieces. A farmer from his own village found him there, too bruised to stand, and took him home across the back of his donkey.

While the knight slept off his injuries, the priest and the barber put his library on trial. With the housekeeper and the niece as willing helpers, they found almost every volume guilty and burned the whole pile in the yard. Then they sealed the doorway with bricks, hoping that the source of his madness would vanish along with the books. When Don Quixote went looking for his library and found only a blank wall, his niece said that an enchanter had carried it off on a cloud. "That will have been the wise Freston," he said gravely, "a bitter enemy of mine."

For two weeks he stayed at home, peaceful enough on the surface, while in secret he worked on one of his neighbors. Sancho Panza was a laborer, an honest fellow with a wife and children, generous of heart but rather short of good sense. So much did the knight talk, and so lavishly did he promise, that the poor man agreed at last to ride with him as his squire. Some lucky adventure, Don Quixote assured him, might win them an island almost overnight, and Sancho would then be its governor. Dazzled by such hopes, Sancho left his family, and one night, without a single farewell, the two of them crept out of the village. He rode his donkey like an ancient patriarch, with his saddlebags and leather wine bottle, already longing for his island.

As they crossed the plain of Montiel, thirty or forty windmills came into view, scattered across the open country. "Luck is shaping our road better than we could ever have planned it," cried Don Quixote. "Look over there, friend Sancho, where more than thirty monstrous giants stand waiting," he went on, "and I mean to battle every one." "What giants?" asked Sancho Panza. "Those you see before you," replied his master, "with arms so long that some of them stretch for nearly two leagues." "Take a careful look, sir," said Sancho. "Those are windmills, and the arms are only sails, turned by the wind." "Anyone can tell that you have no experience of adventures," said Don Quixote. "If you are frightened, step aside and pray."

Saying this, he dug his spurs into Rocinante and rode off, so sure of his giants that even close up he could not see the mills. "Stand your ground, you cowardly brutes," he roared, "since it is only one knight who rides against you!" Just then a light wind rose, and the huge sails began to swing round. "Wave as many arms as the giant Briareus himself," shouted Don Quixote, "and you will still have me to deal with!" Calling upon his lady Dulcinea and holding his shield before him, he drove Rocinante at a full gallop toward the nearest mill. The wind swept the sail upward just as his lance struck it, and the lance splintered into pieces. Horse and rider were hoisted into the air and hurled, sorely battered, across the field.

Sancho came as quickly as his donkey could carry him and found his master unable to move. "Heaven help us!" said Sancho. "Did I not tell you, sir, that these were just windmills? Only a man with windmills spinning in his own head could mistake them." "Hush, friend Sancho," answered Don Quixote. "Nothing in this world shifts more often than the luck of a soldier." "I am certain," he continued, "that the same Freston who stole my books has changed these giants into mills, simply to deprive me of the honor of beating them." "Yet in the end," he added, "all his dark tricks will prove useless against the edge of my sword." "Let God decide," said Sancho, and he set his master back on Rocinante, who was limping badly from the fall.

They took the road to Puerto Lapice, a place so busy with travelers that adventures of every sort were sure to await them there. Don Quixote mourned his lance far more than his bruises, and he wrenched a thick branch from an oak to take its place. When Sancho observed that his master was leaning to one side, Don Quixote admitted the pain but explained that knights errant never grumbled about wounds. Sancho answered that he, for his part, would grumble at the smallest ache, and he went on cheerfully eating and drinking as he rode. That night they camped among the trees, where Don Quixote lay awake dreaming of his lady, as the knights in his books had done. Sancho, full and untroubled, slept until the sun woke him. At around three the next afternoon, the town of Puerto Lapice appeared ahead of them. "Here, brother Sancho Panza," said Don Quixote, lifting his new oak lance, "we shall find more adventures than any chronicle could hold." So, with the windmills far behind them, the lean knight and his round squire rode down the slope to meet whatever the road had in store.
